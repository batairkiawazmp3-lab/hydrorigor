**The most effective hydroponic system for a home grower depends on the crop: NFT or deep water culture (DWC) for leafy greens and herbs, Dutch buckets (or another media system) for tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers, and a Kratky bucket if you want to start with no pump at all.** That is what a Virginia Tech side-by-side trial found: NFT and DWC gave the highest spinach and parsley yields, media bags and Dutch buckets the highest tomato and pepper yields, and NFT the lowest for both fruiting crops.

For a first system, start smaller than that. A **Kratky bucket** of lettuce needs no pump or power and about **1–1.5 gallons (about 4–6 L) of nutrient solution per plant** from seed to harvest (UF/IFAS). Move up to DWC or an NFT rack when you want salad every week, and use **ebb and flow** trays for seedlings. Oregon State University Extension puts it in one line: "the best system depends on your crops and space." Every figure below is tied to a university or extension source listed at the end.

## What "most effective" means at home

"Effective" can mean three things, and the systems rank differently on each.

- **Yield for your crop.** Measured directly in the Virginia Tech trial below: water-based systems win for greens and herbs, media systems for fruiting crops.
- **Water and fertilizer use.** University of Missouri Extension ranks the water-based designs: aeroponics uses the least water and fertilizer, then NFT, and DWC the most.
- **Risk of losing the crop.** Missouri also notes that DWC has the lowest construction cost and plant-loss risk of the water-based systems, and is more forgiving for beginners because plants do not wilt immediately if the air pump fails. NFT plants may wilt immediately if its pump stops, and aeroponics carries the highest risk.

So NFT is the more efficient choice for greens if your power is reliable, DWC the safer one, and Kratky the safest of all because there is nothing to fail.

## Which system yielded most: the side-by-side trial

Virginia Tech grew the same crops in several systems and published the yields (SPES-466, 2023). Spinach and parsley figures are the runs with supplemental light. The authors note the trials ran during COVID-19 restrictions and the tomato and pepper harvest was cut short, so read this as a ranking of systems, not a forecast of your harvest.

| Crop | Highest yield | Lowest yield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spinach | NFT 11.55 oz; DWC 8.74 oz | Vertical media stack 3.37 oz |
| Parsley | DWC 4.63 oz; NFT 3.78 oz | Vertical media stack 1.51 oz |
| Tomatoes | Media bags 8.3 lb; Dutch buckets 7.8 lb | NFT 4.4 lb |
| Peppers | Media bags 6.7 lb; Dutch buckets 5.9 lb | NFT 2.4 lb |

Tomatoes: media bags 8.3 lb, Dutch buckets 7.8 lb, NFT 4.4 lb. Peppers: media bags 6.7 lb, Dutch buckets 5.9 lb, NFT 2.4 lb.](/images/guides/home-hydroponic-system-trial-yields.svg)

*Greens and herbs did best in water-based NFT and DWC; tomatoes and peppers did best in media and worst in NFT. Each crop has its own scale. Data from Virginia Tech SPES-466.*

The authors' explanation: media systems drain better, while NFT and DWC keep roots constantly wet, and fruiting crops need more root room and support; they used NFT for tomatoes and peppers only for comparison. They also met Pythium root rot in the NFT and DWC spinach, and note that raising dissolved oxygen helps control it.

## The six home systems at a glance

Systems differ in how roots get water and air, and whether a pump must keep running: Oklahoma State splits them into passive (wick; Kratky too, per UF/IFAS) and active, pumped designs. For the full family, see [types of hydroponic systems](/systems/types-of-hydroponic-systems/).

| System | How it works | Power | Crops the sources name | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Kratky](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) | Plants in a lid over a fixed volume of solution; the level falls as they drink | None | Lettuce, baby bok choy, short-cycle greens | Running out of solution; fruiting crops do better elsewhere (UF/IFAS HS1488) |
| [Deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) | Roots hang in an aerated tank, plants on a raft or in net pots | Air pump, nonstop | Lettuce, spinach, kale, basil, cilantro, mint, parsley | Heavy water volume; root rot if oxygen falls; algae where light gets in (Oregon State EM 9455) |
| [NFT](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) | A thin film of solution runs down sloped channels back to a tank | Pump, nonstop | Lettuce, herbs, spinach, arugula, kale, chard, some strawberries | Plants wilt fast if the pump or power fails; little root space (Oregon State EM 9457) |
| [Ebb and flow](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) | A tray floods, then drains back to the reservoir | Pump on a timer | Seedlings, herbs, peppers, radishes, spinach, chard | Needs a reliable timer; salt build-up (Oregon State EM 9458) |
| [Dutch buckets](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/) | Buckets of perlite, clay pebbles or coir fed by [drip lines](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/); excess drains back | Pump on a timer | Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, zucchini, eggplant, beans | Clogged outlets (screen them); reused media spreads disease (Oregon State EM 9456) |
| Wick | Wicks draw solution up to the roots | None | Not covered by crop in these sources | Oklahoma State calls it the simplest system but gives no crop guidance (HLA-6442) |

[Aeroponics](/systems/aeroponics-vs-hydroponics/), where roots hang in a chamber and are misted, needs a short-cycle timer that runs the pump "for a few seconds every couple of minutes" (Oklahoma State). None of the home-grower guides used here recommends it as a first system, so this guide does not either.

Deep water culture: plants on a raft over a tank with an air pump and air stone; keep oxygen at 7–10 ppm, 4 ppm minimum. ## How to choose: four questions

**1. What will you grow?** This decides most of it. Oregon State's short version: NFT for short-lived crops or vertical gardens, buckets for long-lived or vining plants, DWC for large spaces and ebb and flow for seedlings. For crop targets, see [hydroponic lettuce](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/), [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/) and [hydroponic strawberries](/crops/hydroponic-strawberries/).

**2. How much floor space and weight?** DWC holds a lot of water and must sit on the ground, level to within 1 inch (2.5 cm) (Oregon State). NFT keeps its solution in a separate tank, so racks are easier to support. The [reservoir size guide](/equipment/hydroponic-reservoir-size/) gives gallons per plant by system.

**3. What happens in a power cut?** A Kratky bucket has nothing to switch off; NFT is the most exposed, and Oregon State calls pump and power backups crucial. If your power is unreliable, start passive.

**4. Where is the light?** Greens and herbs yielded more at a DLI of at least 17 mol/m²/day (Virginia Tech), and on racks upper plants shade lower ones (UF/IFAS, Oregon State), so plan a light for every level. The [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) covers the same problem for towers.

## Set up the starter: a Kratky lettuce bucket

This follows UF/IFAS HS1488, which describes a three-plant bucket. The [Kratky hydroponics guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) has the full method.

1. **Container and lid.** A food-grade 5-gallon (19 L) bucket, wider rather than taller, with three 2-inch (5 cm) holes for net cups; at 1–1.5 gal per lettuce plant, three plants fit.
2. **Solution.** Follow your nutrient label; UF/IFAS gives about EC 1.25 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0. The [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) helps with doses.
3. **Fill and plant.** Fill to about halfway up the net cups, leaving about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of air below the lid, and set in seedlings one to three weeks old.
4. **Leave it.** The crop grows on the first fill; the falling level gives the upper roots air. UF/IFAS gives about three to four more weeks to harvest for its Florida cool-season crop.

**When you outgrow it, move to DWC.** Add an air pump and air stone and keep dissolved oxygen at 7–10 ppm (Virginia Tech), size the raft to the tank so no light gets in, and fit a drain valve at the bottom (Oregon State). The [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) covers the build.

## Troubleshooting by system

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NFT plants wilt suddenly | Pump stopped or power cut | Restore flow at once; keep a backup pump or power supply | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Brown, slimy roots in DWC | Low oxygen and Pythium root rot | Raise aeration toward 7–10 ppm; check the pump and stone | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Green algae on the raft or tank | Gaps that let light reach the solution | Size the raft to the tank and close the gaps | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Kratky bucket runs dry before harvest | Too many plants for the volume | Allow 1–1.5 gal per lettuce plant | UF/IFAS HS1488 |
| White crust on ebb and flow media | Salt build-up | Use shallower floods; flood occasionally with distilled or RO water | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Some drip plants dry, others wet | Clogged emitters or media in outlets | Inspect lines often; screen each bucket outlet | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Lower plants in a rack stay small | Shading from the levels above | Light each level, or grow low-light crops below | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Tomatoes or peppers struggle in NFT | Not enough root space | Move them to Dutch buckets or another media system | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most effective hydroponic system?

There is no single best system for every home; it depends on the crop. In Virginia Tech's side-by-side trial, NFT gave the most spinach (11.55 oz) and DWC the most parsley (4.63 oz), while media bags gave the most tomatoes (8.3 lb) and peppers (6.7 lb) and NFT the least of both. So: NFT or DWC for greens and herbs, a media system such as Dutch buckets for fruiting crops. On water and fertilizer use, Missouri Extension ranks NFT ahead of DWC.

### What is the easiest hydroponic system for beginners?

A Kratky bucket. UF/IFAS calls it "set it and forget it": no pumps or electricity, and a well-lit bucket rarely needs pH or nutrient adjustments. See [hydroponic systems for beginners](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/) for more on a first setup.

### Is NFT or DWC better for a home grower?

Both suit greens. NFT gave more spinach in the Virginia Tech trial and uses less water and fertilizer (Missouri), but its plants wilt quickly if the pump stops. DWC gave more parsley and is more forgiving if the air pump fails, at the cost of a heavy tank that must sit on the floor.

### Can you grow tomatoes in DWC or NFT?

You can, but it is not the best match. Virginia Tech found tomatoes "challenging" in NFT, with the lowest yields of the systems tested, and did not use DWC for fruiting crops. Oregon State and Virginia Tech both point to buckets or other media systems for tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers.
