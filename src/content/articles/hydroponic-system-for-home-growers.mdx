The most effective hydroponic system for a home grower depends on the crop: **Kratky or deep water culture for leafy greens, NFT for racks of small plants, and Dutch buckets for tomatoes and peppers.** For lettuce, herbs and other leafy greens, start with a **Kratky bucket**: no pump, no power, and about **1–1.5 gallons of nutrient solution per lettuce plant** from seed to harvest (UF/IFAS). Move up to **deep water culture (DWC)** with an air pump when you want more plants, or to **NFT channels** when you want rows of small plants on a rack. For **tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers**, use **Dutch buckets** (growing media fed by drip lines): in a Virginia Tech side-by-side trial, media-based systems gave the highest tomato and pepper yields and NFT the lowest. Use **ebb and flow** trays for seedlings and mixed pots.

Oregon State University Extension sums it up in one line: "the best system depends on your crops and space." Every figure below is tied to a university or extension source listed at the end.

## The six home systems at a glance

All hydroponic systems feed roots a nutrient solution instead of soil. They differ in how the roots get water and oxygen, and whether a pump has to keep running. Oklahoma State splits them into passive systems, such as the wick system, and active systems that use a pump. The Kratky method is passive too: it needs no pumps or electricity (UF/IFAS).

| System | How it works | Pump and power | Crops the sources name | Watch out for | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kratky (non-circulating) | Plants sit in a lid over a fixed volume of solution; the level falls as they drink | None | Lettuce, baby bok choy and other short-cycle greens | Running out of solution; a full bucket is heavy; fruiting crops do better in other methods | UF/IFAS HS1488 |
| Deep water culture | Plants on a raft or in net pots, roots hanging in an aerated tank | Air pump runs all the time | Lettuce, spinach, kale, basil, cilantro, mint, parsley | Heavy water volume; root rot (Pythium) if oxygen falls; algae where light gets in | Oregon State EM 9455; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| NFT (nutrient film technique) | A thin, continuous film of solution runs down sloped channels and back to a tank | Pump runs nonstop, no timer | Lettuce, herbs, spinach, arugula, kale, chard, mustard, some strawberries | Plants can wilt quickly if the pump or power fails; little root space | Oregon State EM 9457; Oklahoma State HLA-6442 |
| Ebb and flow (flood and drain) | A tray floods with solution, then drains back to the reservoir | Pump on a timer | Seedlings, herbs, peppers, radishes, spinach, chard, arugula | Needs a reliable timer; salt build-up; media drying between floods | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Dutch buckets (drip) | Buckets of perlite, clay pebbles or coir fed by drip lines; excess drains back | Pump on a timer | Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, zucchini, eggplant, beans, small melons | Clogged outlets (screen them); reused media spreads disease | Oregon State EM 9456; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Wick | Wicks and high-capillary media draw solution up to the roots | None | Not covered by crop in these sources | Oklahoma State calls it the simplest system but gives no crop guidance | Oklahoma State HLA-6442 |

Aeroponics, where roots hang in a chamber and are misted, is the other common design. Oklahoma State notes it needs a short-cycle timer that runs the pump "for a few seconds every couple of minutes". None of the home-grower guides used here recommends it as a first system, so this guide does not either.

## What a side-by-side trial found

Virginia Tech grew the same crops in several systems and published the yields (SPES-466, 2023). Spinach and parsley figures below are the runs with supplemental light. It is one trial at one site, so read it as a ranking of systems, not a forecast of your harvest.

| Crop | Highest yield | Lowest yield | What the authors concluded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spinach | NFT 11.55 oz; DWC 8.74 oz | Vertical media stack 3.37 oz | Greens and herbs did best in NFT and DWC |
| Parsley | DWC 4.63 oz; NFT 3.78 oz | Vertical media stack 1.51 oz | Same pattern as spinach |
| Tomatoes | Media bags 8.3 lb; Dutch buckets 7.8 lb | NFT 4.4 lb | Fruiting crops did best in media systems |
| Peppers | Media bags 6.7 lb; Dutch buckets 5.9 lb | NFT 2.4 lb | NFT was the lowest for both fruiting crops |

The authors' explanation: media systems drain better, while NFT and DWC keep roots in constant contact with the solution, and fruiting crops need more root room and support. They used NFT for tomatoes and peppers only for comparison and do not usually recommend it for fruiting crops. They also met Pythium root rot in the NFT and DWC spinach, and note that raising oxygen helps control it.

## How to choose: five questions

**1. What will you grow?** This decides most of it. Oregon State's short version: NFT for short-lived crops or vertical gardens, buckets for long-lived or vining plants, deep water culture for large spaces and ebb and flow for seedlings. For crop-specific targets, see the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/), [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/) and [hydroponic strawberries](/crops/hydroponic-strawberries/).

**2. How much floor space and weight can you give it?** DWC holds a lot of water. Oregon State says it must sit on the ground, with the tank bottom level to within one inch. NFT keeps most of the solution in a separate tank, which makes shelves and racks easier to support.

**3. What happens in a power cut?** A Kratky bucket has nothing to switch off. NFT is the most exposed: Oregon State calls power and pump backups crucial because plants can wilt quickly. If your power is unreliable, start passive.

**4. How much time do you have?** UF/IFAS says a Kratky bucket in a well-lit spot rarely needs pH or nutrient adjustments. Ebb and flow depends on a dependable timer, and UF/IFAS notes that drip lines need constant inspection to make sure every plant gets water.

**5. Where is the light?** Virginia Tech's greens and herbs yielded more when the daily light integral reached at least 17 mol/m²/day. Vertical systems pack in more plants, but UF/IFAS and Oregon State both warn that upper plants shade lower ones, so racks usually need grow lights on every level. The same applies to towers: see the [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) for spacing, pump schedules and light.

## Our recommendation by home situation

| Your situation | Recommended system | What it rests on |

| Your first system, any room | One Kratky bucket of lettuce | No pump or power; 1–1.5 gal of solution per plant; rarely needs adjusting (UF/IFAS HS1488) |
| You want salad every week | A DWC tub with an air pump, or a small NFT rack | Greens and herbs yielded best in NFT and DWC (Virginia Tech); DWC grows fast and holds temperature well (Oregon State) |

## Set up the starter: a Kratky lettuce bucket

This follows UF/IFAS HS1488, which describes a three-plant bucket.

1. **Choose the container.** A food-grade 5-gallon bucket with a lid. Wider is better than taller, so the roots can keep reaching the solution as it falls.
2. **Cut the lid.** Drill three 2-inch holes for net cups, one plant per cup. At 1–1.5 gal per lettuce plant, three plants fit the bucket's volume.
3. **Mix the solution.** Follow your nutrient label; UF/IFAS gives about EC 1,250 µS/cm (1.25 mS/cm, roughly 800 ppm on a TDS meter) and a pH of 5.5–6.0. The [mixing guide](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) help with the doses.
4. **Fill it.** Bring the solution up so it reaches about halfway up the net cups, leaving about an inch of air below the lid.
5. **Plant.** Set seedlings that are one to three weeks old into the net cups.
6. **Leave the level alone.** The whole crop can grow on the first fill. As the plants drink, the level drops and the roots above it get air; Kratky's own trials at the University of Hawaii work the same way.
7. **Harvest.** UF/IFAS gives about three to four more weeks to harvest for its Florida cool-season crop. Indoors under lights, timing depends on your light and temperature.

**When you outgrow it, move to DWC.** Add an air pump and air stone and keep dissolved oxygen in the 7–10 ppm range (Virginia Tech). Check pH, nutrient strength and the aeration kit regularly, size the raft to the tank so no gaps let light in, and fit a drain valve at the bottom (Oregon State). The [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) covers the build.

## Troubleshooting by system

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NFT plants wilt suddenly | Pump stopped or power cut | Restore flow at once; keep a backup pump or power supply | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Brown, slimy roots in DWC | Low oxygen and Pythium root rot | Raise aeration towards 7–10 ppm; check the air pump and stone | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Green algae on the raft or tank | Gaps that let light reach the solution | Size the raft to the tank and close the gaps | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Kratky bucket runs dry before harvest | Too many plants for the volume | Allow 1–1.5 gal per lettuce plant | UF/IFAS HS1488 |
| Media pulls away from pot walls; plants wilt between floods | Floods too far apart | Flood again before the media dries out | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| White crust on media in ebb and flow | Salt build-up | Use shallower floods; flood occasionally with distilled or RO water | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Some drip plants dry, others wet | Clogged emitters (hard water) or media in outlets | Inspect lines often; screen each bucket outlet | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Lower plants in a vertical rack stay small | Shading from the levels above | Add light to each level or grow low-light crops below | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Tomatoes or peppers struggle in NFT | Not enough root space | Move them to Dutch buckets or another media system | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Oregon State EM 9457 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most effective hydroponic system?

There is no single best system for every home; it depends on the crop. In Virginia Tech's side-by-side trial, NFT gave the most spinach (11.55 oz) and DWC the most parsley (4.63 oz), while media bags gave the most tomatoes (8.3 lb) and peppers (6.7 lb) and NFT the least of both. So: NFT or DWC for greens and herbs, a media system such as Dutch buckets for fruiting crops.

### What is the easiest hydroponic system for beginners?

A Kratky bucket. UF/IFAS describes it as "set it and forget it": no pumps or electricity, and a well-lit bucket rarely needs pH or nutrient adjustments. Oklahoma State calls the wick system the simplest design, but the sources here give little crop guidance for it. For more on choosing a first setup, see [hydroponic systems for beginners](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/).

### What equipment does a beginner need?

For a Kratky bucket: a food-grade 5-gallon bucket with a lid, three 2-inch net cups, a hydroponic nutrient and a way to test pH (UF/IFAS). Add an air pump and air stone for DWC, a pump for NFT, ebb and flow or drip, and a grow light for any system indoors. The [equipment table](#what-to-buy-for-each-system) lists each system.

### Are plug-and-play hydroponic kits a good choice?

They can be, if the design inside fits your crop: greens in water-based systems such as Kratky, DWC or NFT, and fruiting crops in media. Check the reservoir size and the light before features. HydroRigor has not tested kits; [what to buy and what to skip](/best/indoor-hydroponic-system-kits/) covers the checks.

### Can you grow tomatoes in DWC or NFT?

You can, but it is not the best match. Virginia Tech found tomatoes "challenging" in NFT, with the lowest yields of the systems tested, and did not use DWC for fruiting crops. Oregon State and Virginia Tech both point to buckets or other media systems for tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers.

### Do I need electricity for hydroponics?

Not for Kratky or wick systems. DWC needs an air pump running all the time, NFT a pump running nonstop, and ebb and flow and drip systems a pump on a timer.

### Is aeroponics better for home growers?

Not as a first system. Oklahoma State notes it needs a short-cycle timer that runs the pump for a few seconds every couple of minutes, and none of the home-grower guides used here recommends it for beginners.

### What pH and EC should I use?

Oklahoma State gives pH 5.0–6.0 and EC 1.5–3 mS/cm as general ranges; for a Kratky lettuce bucket UF/IFAS uses pH 5.5–6.0 and about EC 1.25 mS/cm. Look up your crop in the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).
