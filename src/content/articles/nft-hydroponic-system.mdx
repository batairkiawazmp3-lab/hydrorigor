**An NFT (nutrient film technique) hydroponic system pumps a thin, shallow film of nutrient solution down a sloped channel past bare roots and lets it drain back to the reservoir, around the clock.** For a home system growing lettuce and herbs, set the channel to slope **1–2%** (about 1.2–2.4 in of drop over 10 ft), start the flow at **3–5 gallons (11–19 L) per hour into each channel**, space plants **8 inches (20 cm)** apart, and size the reservoir at **¼–1 gallon (1–3.8 L) per plant**. Keep the solution at **pH 5.5–6.2** and **EC 1.2–2.0 mS/cm**.

Those figures come mainly from Virginia Tech's NFT publication (SPES-463), which is written for small systems, with the slope from Missouri, Alabama and Oregon State Extension. NFT uses less water and fertilizer than deep water culture, but it has one weakness every source stresses: if the pump stops, the film stops, and plants can wilt quickly.

HydroRigor has not built or tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## How an NFT system works

Every NFT system, Virginia Tech says, has the same five parts: a bench or frame that holds the channels, a nutrient reservoir, the plant channels, a way to collect the solution at the low end, and a pump with tubing to deliver it to the high end. A submersible pump in the reservoir is usual in small systems.

The solution enters the high end of each channel, runs downhill in a layer only a few millimeters deep, and falls into a return pipe that drains back to the reservoir. Purdue describes it as a shallow stream recirculated past bare roots in a watertight, dark channel. Because the film is so shallow, the roots are never sunk in a deep tank, so NFT needs no air pump or air stone.

Because the solution keeps moving, Oklahoma State notes that NFT runs continuously and needs no timer. That is also why it saves water: Missouri ranks NFT second only to aeroponics for the lowest water and fertilizer use among water-based systems, with deep water culture using the most. The price is that the roots hold almost no reserve. Missouri warns that plants "may wilt immediately" if the pump fails, and Oregon State calls power and pump backups crucial.

## The settings, one by one

### Slope: 1–2%

The slope moves the film along. Too flat and the solution pools; too steep and it races past the roots. The sources cluster close together but not on one number:

| Source | Slope |
| --- | --- |
| University of Missouri G6986 | 1–2% |
| Alabama Extension ANR-2903 | 1% is often enough for most trough lengths |
| Purdue (Langenhoven) | 1:100 (1%) recommended; 1:30 and 1:40 also used |
| Oregon State EM 9457 | At least 2% |
| Virginia Tech SPES-463 | Sloped enough to drain; no figure |

A 1% slope is a drop of 1 cm per meter, or about 1.2 inches over a 10-foot (3 m) channel; 2% doubles it. Our suggestion for a short home channel is to start at 2%, which satisfies Oregon State and sits inside Missouri's range, and to treat 1% as the floor. Check with a level before planting, and make sure the channel does not sag in the middle, because a sag makes a puddle no slope figure can fix.

### Flow: start at 3–5 gallons per hour per channel

This is where the sources differ most, and the difference has a reason.

| Source | Flow per channel | In liters per minute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virginia Tech SPES-463 (small systems) | 3–5 gal/h | 0.19–0.32 |
| Purdue (Langenhoven) | 1 L/min, upper limit 2 L/min | 1–2 |
| Sato et al. 2007, citing Cooper and Jones | 1–2 L/min widely recommended | 1–2 |
| Alabama ANR-2903 | Usually less than ½ gal/min | Under about 1.9 |

![A bar chart of NFT flow per channel in liters per minute. Virginia Tech, for small systems: 3 to 5 gallons per hour, about 0.19 to 0.32 liters per minute. Purdue: 1 liter per minute, upper limit 2. Sato and colleagues: 1 to 2 liters per minute widely recommended. *Recommended NFT flow per channel from four sources, converted to liters per minute (conversions are HydroRigor's arithmetic). The low Virginia Tech figure is written for small systems; the others come from commercial channels up to 18 m (59 ft) long.*

The 1–2 L/min figure comes from commercial channels, which can run to 10–15 m (33–49 ft) and carry many more plants. Virginia Tech's publication is the one written for the 4–12 ft (1.2–3.7 m) channels a home grower builds, so we suggest starting at its 3–5 gallons per hour and raising the flow if the film does not reach the low end in an even layer, or if plants near the drain look drier than those at the inlet. Stay under 2 L/min (about 32 gal/h), Purdue's upper limit.

To size a pump, add up the channels: four channels at 5 gal/h need about 20 gal/h at the outlets (our arithmetic). Virginia Tech says to choose a continuous-duty pump sized for the height it must lift and the friction in the tubing, and to fit valves so each channel's flow can be adjusted. Our sources give no lift or pump-size figure, so check the maker's pump curve for the flow it delivers at your lift height.

### Channels and spacing: 8 inches apart

Virginia Tech says hobby systems often use 2-inch schedule 40 pipe, while commercial channels are usually about 4 inches (10 cm) wide and 1½–2 inches (4–5 cm) deep with a flat bottom; Alabama gives 2–3 inches deep.

For leafy greens, Virginia Tech spaces plants **8 inches (20 cm) on center**, and Alabama drills holes on 7.5–8 inch centers with channels set 7.5–8 inches apart. At 8-inch centers Alabama counts 2.25 plants per square foot. Keep channels short: Virginia Tech gives 4–12 ft and Alabama calls 8–12 ft standard. Long channels change the solution on its way down; in an 18 m (59 ft) channel, Sato and colleagues measured dissolved oxygen falling by up to 0.9 mg/L and the solution warming by 0.3–1.4 °C (0.5–2.5 °F) from inlet to outlet.

Use white or light-colored channels and tubing. Virginia Tech notes that white tubing reflects heat and keeps the solution cooler, while black does the opposite.

### Reservoir: ¼–1 gallon per plant

Virginia Tech sizes the reservoir at **¼ to 1 gallon (1–3.8 L) of capacity per plant**, so a 50-plant system might use a 25-gallon (95 L) tank, and suggests going larger because more water buffers swings in pH and nutrient strength. For a 24-plant home system that means 6–24 gallons (23–91 L); we'd lean to the top of that range, since a small tank in a warm room drifts fast. Keep it opaque and covered: Illinois Extension says to cover clear sides so light can't grow algae in the solution.

## The solution: pH, EC and temperature

| Setting | Target | Where the sources differ | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH | 5.5–6.2 | Alabama 5.6–6.0 for lettuce; Oklahoma State 6.0–7.0 for lettuce; Kentucky 5.5–6.0 | Virginia Tech SPES-463; Alabama ANR-2903; Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| EC, leafy greens | 1.2–2.0 mS/cm | Oklahoma State 1.2–1.8 for lettuce; Purdue's NFT trial grew lettuce best at 1.3 and worse at 2.9 or more | Virginia Tech SPES-463; Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Purdue HO-309-W |
| Checking | Two to three times a week | Oklahoma State: daily, at the same time | Virginia Tech SPES-463; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Solution temperature | 65–80 °F (18–27 °C) | Oklahoma State 72–75 °F (22–24 °C); Sato cites 13–18 °C (55–64 °F) as best for lettuce roots | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Sato et al. 2007 |
| Dissolved oxygen | No NFT target given | Sato cites below 8 mg/L as reducing calcium, potassium and phosphorus uptake | Sato et al. 2007 |

**pH: aim for 5.6–6.0.** That band sits inside Virginia Tech's, Alabama's and Kentucky's ranges. Alabama adds that pH tends to drop quickly as plants get bigger, so check more often late in the crop.

**EC: about 1.3–1.8 for lettuce.** Purdue's trial of 24 lettuce varieties in NFT found growth was higher at EC 1.3 and 2.0 and lower at 2.9 and 3.9, and recommended 1.3. Oklahoma State gives 1.2–1.8. Basil and other herbs run slightly lower (Oklahoma State gives basil 1.0–1.6). Set EC first, then pH, as Oklahoma State recommends. Our [hydroponic nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) covers mixing and replacing, and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists other crops.

**Temperature: keep it cool.** Oregon State advises keeping the solution cool and out of sunlight so it stays oxygenated, and the film warms as it runs. In Sato's trial the tank averaged 28.3 °C (83 °F), which the authors called too warm for lettuce roots. A room that keeps the air near 65–75 °F (18–24 °C), as Alabama and Kentucky give for lettuce, usually keeps a small reservoir in range.

**Checking frequency: where the sources differ.** Virginia Tech says two to three times a week; Oklahoma State says daily. NFT's small water volume per plant drifts faster than a deep tank, so we suggest daily checks for the first two weeks, then two to three times a week once you know how fast your system moves. You'll need a pH meter and an EC meter; our [pH meter guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) explains how to choose and calibrate one.

## Which crops grow well in NFT

| Crop | Fit | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lettuce (butterhead, romaine, oakleaf, multi-leaf) | Best | Short crop, small roots; about 30 days from transplant | Virginia Tech SPES-463; Alabama ANR-2903 |
| Basil and other herbs | Good | Herbs yielded best in NFT and DWC in Virginia Tech's trial | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Spinach, arugula, kale, chard, mustard | Good | Listed for NFT | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Strawberries | Possible | "Some strawberries" in NFT; occasional in Virginia Tech's list | Oregon State EM 9457; Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers | Poor | Lowest yields in NFT in Virginia Tech's trial; buckets buffer better | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Oregon State EM 9456 |

Oregon State explains the pattern: NFT channels have limited rooting space, so they suit short-lived or small-rooted crops. Big fruiting plants do better with the larger root volume of a bucket, which Oregon State says buffers mistakes better than NFT. For those, drip-fed buckets of perlite are the better system; see our [hydroponic tomatoes guide](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/). For strawberries in channels, our [NFT strawberry guide](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/) has the crop-specific settings, and [hydroponic basil](/crops/hydroponic-basil/) covers herbs.

Light matters as much as the system. Virginia Tech's trial grew greens and herbs at a daily light integral of at least 17 mol/m²/day; Kentucky gives 12–17 for lettuce. Ohio State's 2023 tipburn trial ran lettuce in NFT at DLI 17 over 16 hours and found most varieties showed tipburn 16–24 days after transplanting, while Rabello and Rouxai showed none by day 28. If your lettuce tipburns under strong light, try one of those varieties.

## How to set up a home NFT system, step by step

1. **Choose the crop and count plants.** Lettuce and herbs suit NFT; skip tomatoes and peppers (Virginia Tech SPES-466). At 8-inch spacing, a 4-ft channel holds about six plants (our arithmetic).
2. **Build or buy the channels.** Use 2-inch pipe or flat-bottomed channels about 4 inches wide, 4–12 ft long, in white or a light color, with end caps (Virginia Tech SPES-463). Drill plant holes on 8-inch centers to fit your net pots or plugs.
3. **Set the slope.** Mount the channels on a frame at a 1–2% fall toward the drain end, about 1.2–2.4 inches over 10 ft (Missouri G6986). Check with a level and support the middle so it can't sag.
4. **Fit the reservoir.** Use an opaque, covered, food-grade tank of ¼–1 gallon per plant, placed below the channel outlets so the return drains by gravity (Virginia Tech SPES-463; Illinois).
5. **Install the pump and tubing.** A continuous-duty submersible pump, delivery tubing to the high end of each channel, a valve on each line, and a return pipe from the low ends. Fit a backup pump or keep one ready (Virginia Tech SPES-463).
6. **Run water first.** Before planting, run plain water and adjust each valve until a thin film covers the channel floor and reaches the drain, starting at 3–5 gal/h per channel. Look for leaks and puddles.
7. **Mix the solution.** Fill with nutrient solution, set EC to 1.2–2.0 (about 1.3–1.8 for lettuce), then pH to 5.6–6.0 (Virginia Tech; Purdue; Alabama). The [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) works out the amounts.
8. **Transplant seedlings.** Start seeds in rockwool, foam or peat cubes (Virginia Tech) and move them at about 2 weeks old with 2–3 true leaves (Alabama ANR-2903). Make sure each cube touches the film.
9. **Keep it running.** The pump runs around the clock. Check pH and EC daily at first, then two to three times a week; top up with water as plants drink, and replace the solution about every two weeks (Oklahoma State HLA-6722).
10. **Harvest and clean.** Lettuce is ready about 30 days after transplanting (Alabama; Kentucky gives 21–35 days). Remove the old plants and roots and rinse the channels before the next crop.

If you'd rather build from a parts list, our [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) includes a pipe-frame build.

## NFT vs DWC and Kratky

| | NFT | Deep water culture | Kratky |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pump | Water pump, always on | Air pump | None |
| Water and fertilizer use | Low (second lowest after aeroponics) | Highest of water-based systems | 1–1.5 gal per lettuce plant |
| If the power fails | Plants may wilt quickly | Roots stay in water; no immediate wilting | Nothing changes |
| Best for | Rows of lettuce and herbs | Lettuce, greens, herbs | 1–3 lettuce plants |
| Source | Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9457 | Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9455 | UF/IFAS HS1488 |

NFT wins when you want many plants in rows with little water; deep water culture wins when you want a system that forgives a pump failure. For a handful of plants, Kratky is simpler than either. Our [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/), [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) and [hydroponic lettuce systems comparison](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-systems/) go further, and [types of hydroponic systems](/systems/types-of-hydroponic-systems/) places NFT among all eight types.

## Troubleshooting an NFT system

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole channel wilts at once | Pump or power stopped | Restore flow; keep a backup pump | Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9457; Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Solution pools in the channel | Too flat, or a sag in the middle | Reset to 1–2% slope; support the channel | Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Brown, slimy roots | Too much water around the roots, or warm solution | Thin the film; keep solution 65–80 °F | UF/IFAS HS1422 |
| Plants near the drain grow worse | Flow too low, or channel too long | Raise flow gradually (stay under 2 L/min); keep channels short | Purdue; Sato et al. 2007 |
| Overflow at plant holes | Flow too high for the channel | Turn down that channel's valve | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| pH falling fast | Plants getting bigger | Check more often; adjust after EC | Alabama ANR-2903; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Leaf edges browning on lettuce | Tipburn under strong light | Try Rabello or Rouxai | e-GRO (Ohio State) 2023 |
| Green film on channels or tank | Light reaching the solution | Use opaque parts; cover clear sides | Illinois Extension |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an NFT hydroponic system?

A recirculating system in which a pump sends a thin film of nutrient solution down a sloped, dark channel past bare roots, then back to the reservoir (Purdue; Oklahoma State HLA-6442). The roots sit partly in the film and partly in moist air.

### What slope should an NFT channel have?

1–2% (Missouri G6986), which is 1–2 cm per meter or about 1.2–2.4 inches over 10 feet. Alabama finds 1% often enough; Oregon State says at least 2%.

### What flow rate does an NFT system need?

Virginia Tech gives 3–5 gallons (11–19 L) per hour into each channel for small systems. Commercial sources use about 1 L/min, with 2 L/min as the upper limit (Purdue; Sato et al. 2007). Start low in short home channels and raise the flow until the film reaches the drain.

### Does an NFT pump run 24 hours a day?

Yes. Oklahoma State notes NFT runs continuously and needs no timer. One 2025 Texas A&M trial found that switching the flow on and off raised yields with organic nutrients, but it did not test standard mineral nutrients, so it is not a reason to put a home NFT system on a timer.

### How far apart should plants be in NFT?

8 inches (20 cm) on center for leafy greens (Virginia Tech SPES-463); Alabama uses 7.5–8 inches with channels 7.5–8 inches apart.

### Can you grow tomatoes in NFT?

It is possible but not recommended. In Virginia Tech's trial, tomatoes and peppers yielded least in NFT, and Oregon State notes buckets buffer better because of their larger root volume.

### Is NFT better than deep water culture?

It uses less water and fertilizer, but plants may wilt quickly if the pump stops, while DWC roots stay in water (Missouri G6986). For a first system, DWC or Kratky is more forgiving; NFT suits growers who want rows of lettuce or herbs.
