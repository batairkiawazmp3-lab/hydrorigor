**There are eight common types of hydroponic systems: wick, Kratky, deep water culture (DWC), nutrient film technique (NFT), ebb and flow, drip (including Dutch buckets), aeroponics and vertical towers.** They differ in one thing above all: how they get water, nutrients and air to the roots. Wick and Kratky are passive, with no pump. The other six are active and need electricity for a pump or timer.

For leafy greens and herbs, Kratky, DWC and NFT are the usual choices; for tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers, drip-fed buckets of perlite. That rests on Oregon State University Extension ("the best system depends on your crops and space") and a Virginia Tech trial in which greens and herbs yielded best in NFT and DWC and tomatoes and peppers best in media bags. The numbers that matter differ by type: an NFT channel slopes **1–2%** and carries about **1–2 L (0.26–0.53 gal) per minute**, a DWC tank holds **7–10 ppm of dissolved oxygen**, and a Kratky lettuce plant uses **1–1.5 gallons (about 4–6 L)** from seed to harvest.

HydroRigor has not tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## Three ways to sort hydroponic systems

Names like "NFT" and "Dutch bucket" make the field look more complicated than it is. Three questions place any system.

1. **Passive or active?** The University of Georgia splits home systems into passive ones, with no pump, and active ones, which pump solution. Passive systems keep working in a power cut; active ones grow bigger plants and more crops.
2. **Water-based or media-based?** The University of Missouri separates systems where roots sit in solution (on rafts, in channels or misted) from those where roots grow in a container of coir, perlite or rockwool that is watered with solution.
3. **Closed or open?** Closed systems collect and recirculate the solution; open systems feed fresh solution and let the drainage go to waste (Missouri G6986).

## The eight types, one by one

### 1. Wick system

A wick draws solution from a reservoir up into a growing medium, the way a lamp wick draws oil. Oklahoma State calls it the simplest hydroponic system, and it has no pump to fail. Our sources give no wick material, length, crop list or plant-size limit, so we don't print one; treat it as a small, simple setup and check pH and EC as you would for any system (Oklahoma State HLA-6722).

### 2. Kratky (non-circulating)

You fill a tank once, set the plants so the bottom of each net pot touches the solution, and never top it up. As the plants drink, the level falls and leaves a moist air gap where "oxygen roots" form. UF/IFAS grows three lettuce plants in a 5-gallon (19 L) bucket and gives each plant **1–1.5 gallons (about 4–6 L)** from seed to harvest, with about **1 inch (2.5 cm)** of air space, EC about 1.25 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6. Harvest comes about 3–4 weeks after transplanting.

Bernard Kratky's own University of Hawaii guide allows more water, **1.5–2 gallons (about 6–7.5 L) per plant**, in a tank about 5½ inches (14 cm) deep, and gives 4–6 weeks to harvest. The two are not far apart; we plan on 1.5 gallons a plant so the tank is less likely to run dry before harvest. Don't raise the level once the gap forms: Kratky's 2004 paper warns that raising it more than 2 cm (about ¾ in) may injure the plants. Fruiting crops do better in other systems (UF/IFAS). Full build: our [Kratky hydroponics guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/).

### 3. Deep water culture (DWC)

Roots hang from a floating raft or lid into a tank of solution that an air pump and air stone keep oxygenated. Virginia Tech gives **7–10 ppm dissolved oxygen, never below 4 ppm**. Other sources set the floor lower: the University of Kentucky gives 6–8 ppm for lettuce and UF/IFAS HS1422 5 mg/L (the same as 5 ppm). Aiming for 7 ppm or more satisfies all three. UF/IFAS keeps the solution at 65–80 °F (18–27 °C).

**Depth: where the sources differ.** Missouri gives 4–12 inches (10–30 cm); Kentucky uses at least 12 inches (30 cm) for lettuce rafts at 3–4 plants per square foot. Deeper tanks hold more water, which Oregon State notes buffers temperature, but they are heavy and belong on the ground. Missouri ranks DWC lowest in build cost and plant-loss risk, because plants don't wilt at once if the air pump fails; it also uses the most water and fertilizer of the water-based systems. A one-plant 5-gallon bucket version works for tomatoes and peppers (UF/IFAS Volusia County). See our [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

### 4. Nutrient film technique (NFT)

A pump sends a thin film of solution down a sloped, dark channel past bare roots and back to the tank. Oklahoma State notes the flow is constant, so NFT needs no timer. Three numbers set it up:

| Setting | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slope | 1–2% (Missouri); at least 2% (Oregon State); 1:100 recommended, 1:30 and 1:40 also used (Purdue) | Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9457; Purdue |
| Flow | 1 L (0.26 gal) per minute, upper limit 2 L (0.53 gal) | Purdue; Sato et al. 2007 |
| Channel length | No more than 10–15 m (33–49 ft); Sato cites no more than 9 m (about 30 ft) | Purdue; Sato et al. 2007 |

Length matters because the solution changes on its way down. In an 18 m (59 ft) channel, Sato and colleagues measured dissolved oxygen falling by up to 0.9 mg/L from inlet to outlet and the solution warming by 0.3–1.4 °C (0.5–2.5 °F). A home channel is far shorter, so this is mostly a reason not to chain channels end to end.

Oregon State lists lettuce, herbs, spinach, arugula, kale, chard, mustard and some strawberries for NFT, and warns that power and pump backups are crucial because plants can wilt quickly. Missouri says they "may wilt immediately". Our [NFT hydroponic system guide](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) covers the full home setup, and the [NFT strawberry guide](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/) walks through a strawberry build.

### 5. Ebb and flow (flood and drain)

A tray of media or pots floods from a reservoir below, then drains back, on a timer. Oregon State floods to about an inch (2.5 cm) below the top of the media, drains once the media is soaked, and floods again before it dries and shrinks. It lists seedlings, peppers, herbs, radishes and leafy greens, says a reliable timer is essential, and suggests shallow floods and an occasional flood of plain water to limit salt buildup.

**Timing: no single schedule.** Virginia Tech's trial flooded once a day for 12 minutes, while Oregon State gives a rule ("before the media dries") rather than a time. Both can be right: how fast media dries depends on the medium, the plant size and the room. Start from the trial's schedule and add floods if the top of the media dries out between them. Our [ebb and flow hydroponics guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) covers flood timing, media and setup in full.

### 6. Drip and Dutch buckets

Emitters drip solution onto a medium such as perlite or coir in buckets, bags or slabs. Oklahoma State calls drip probably the most widely used system, and it can be closed (drainage returns to the tank) or open (drainage goes to waste). Dutch buckets are the recirculating home version for tomatoes, cucumbers and other vining crops (Missouri; Oregon State EM 9456).

Virginia Tech's trial ran drip stakes at **0.5 gallon (about 1.9 L) per hour**. Alabama Extension puts greenhouse tomatoes at **about 2 quarts (1.9 L) of water per plant per day** at EC 2.0–2.5 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.5. Oregon State says the larger root volume buffers mistakes better than NFT, to screen each outlet, and not to reuse the media. For emitters, run timing and reading the drain, see our [drip hydroponic system guide](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/); for the recirculating bucket version, the [Dutch bucket hydroponics guide](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/); for the crop itself, see [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).

### 7. Aeroponics

Roots hang in a closed chamber and are misted with solution. Missouri ranks aeroponics lowest of the water-based systems in water and fertilizer use, but highest in build cost and in the risk of losing plants, and says it needs high-quality water and pressurized misters. Oklahoma State says it needs a short-cycle timer that runs every few minutes. Our sources give no droplet size, nozzle pressure or exact on/off times, so we don't give any; follow the maker's instructions for a kit.

### 8. Vertical towers

A pump lifts solution to the top of a column; it trickles down past plants in side pockets and returns to the tank. Oklahoma State's build guide uses a 45-gallon (170 L) tank, a 400-gallon-per-hour (about 1,500 L/h) pump and 28 plants per tower. It runs the pump continuously for lettuce and 7–10 times a day for an hour for strawberries, and warns that splashing can spread fungal disease. Towers produce more per square foot of floor, but light and plant weight fall toward the base (Touliatos et al. 2016). Our [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) covers it in full.

## How to choose and set up a system, step by step

1. **Start with the crop.** Greens and herbs: Kratky, DWC or NFT. Fruiting crops: drip-fed buckets (Virginia Tech; Oregon State EM 9456).
2. **Decide how much power risk you can accept.** No pump at all: Kratky. A pump that can fail without killing the crop: DWC. NFT and aeroponics need a backup plan (Missouri; Oregon State EM 9457).
3. **Use food-grade, opaque containers.** Oregon State recommends food-grade materials; Illinois says to cover clear sides so light can't grow algae in the solution.
4. **Size the reservoir.** For Kratky lettuce, plan on 1–1.5 gallons (4–6 L) a plant (UF/IFAS) or up to 2 gallons (7.5 L) (Kratky). For pumped systems, more volume means slower pH and EC drift.
5. **Set the type-specific numbers.** NFT: 1–2% slope and 1–2 L/min. DWC: an air stone and check valve (Illinois), aiming for 7–10 ppm oxygen. Ebb and flow: a timer, flooding before the media dries. Towers: pump schedule by crop (Oklahoma State HLA-6724).
6. **Mix and check the solution.** Set EC for the crop first, then pH; lettuce runs 1.2–1.8 mS/cm (Oklahoma State HLA-6722). The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists crops, and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) does the amounts.
7. **Keep up the routine.** In pumped systems, check pH and EC daily and replace the solution about every two weeks (Oklahoma State HLA-6722). In Kratky, don't top up.

If you would rather build than buy, our [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) has parts lists for three builds. New to the subject? Start with [what hydroponics is and how it works](/systems/what-is-hydroponics/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the main types of hydroponic systems?

Wick, Kratky, deep water culture, nutrient film technique, ebb and flow, drip, aeroponics and vertical towers (Oklahoma State HLA-6442; Oregon State EM 9453; UF/IFAS HS1488). The first two are passive; the rest use a pump.

### What is the easiest type of hydroponic system?

Oklahoma State calls the wick system the simplest. For food crops, Kratky is the usual first system because it needs no pump and grows a full head of lettuce from 1–1.5 gallons of solution (UF/IFAS HS1488). Missouri calls DWC "more resilient and forgiving" for beginners among pumped systems.

### Which hydroponic system is the most productive?

It depends on the crop. In Virginia Tech's trial, greens and herbs yielded most in NFT and DWC, and tomatoes and peppers in media bags, with NFT lowest for those crops.

### What is the difference between NFT and DWC?

In DWC the roots sit in a deep tank of aerated solution; in NFT a thin film flows past them in a sloped channel. DWC uses more water but tolerates a pump failure better; NFT uses less but plants can wilt quickly if the flow stops (Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9457).

### Is aeroponics better than other types?

It uses the least water and fertilizer of the water-based systems, but Missouri ranks it highest in build cost and in the risk of losing plants, and it needs clean water and pressurized misters. For most homes, DWC or NFT is easier.

### Which hydroponic systems work without electricity?

Wick and Kratky (Oklahoma State HLA-6442; UF/IFAS HS1488). Indoors you still need power for a grow light.
