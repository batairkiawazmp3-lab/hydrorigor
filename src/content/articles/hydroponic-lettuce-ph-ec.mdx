Hydroponic lettuce grows best with the nutrient solution at **pH 5.5–6.0 and EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm**, with seedlings on a weaker **EC 1.0**. Aim for the middle: pH about **5.8–6.0** and EC about **1.4–1.5** suits most home setups. The University of Kentucky sets pH 5.5–6.0 and an example recipe at about EC 1.5. UF/IFAS and Oklahoma State both give EC 1.2–1.8.

pH matters more than most growers think. In a Cornell trial, butterhead lettuce held at **pH 7.0 made 26% less fresh weight** than lettuce at pH 5.8 (Anderson et al. 2017). EC involves a trade-off. In an Ohio State and USDA trial, the heaviest heads came at **EC 1.8**, but tipburn was lowest at **EC 1.4** (Samarakoon et al. 2020). Purdue, testing 24 varieties, recommends **1.3**.

## Quick answer: lettuce pH and EC targets

| Measure | Target | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solution pH | 5.5–6.0 | University of Kentucky (CCD-CP-63) |
| pH with equal yield in a trial | 5.8–6.2; lower yield at 6.4 in two of three varieties | Samarakoon et al. 2020 (Ohio State, USDA-ARS) |
| Cost of pH 7.0 | −26% head fresh weight, −18% dry weight vs pH 5.8 | Anderson et al. 2017 (Cornell) |
| EC, growing plants | 1.2–1.8 mS/cm | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| EC, example recipe | About 1.5 mS/cm | University of Kentucky |
| EC, seedlings and plugs | 1.0 mS/cm, pH 5.5–6.0 | University of Kentucky |
| One EC for every variety | 1.3 mS/cm (24 varieties, NFT and raft) | Purdue HO-309-W |
| EC for least tipburn | 1.4 (heaviest heads at 1.8) | Samarakoon et al. 2020 |
| Too strong | Less growth at 2.9 and 3.9 | Purdue HO-309-W |
| Check | 2–3 times a week; daily in small reservoirs | Virginia Extension SPES-463; Oklahoma State |

On a meter that reads ppm, EC 1.2–1.8 is about **600–900 ppm** on a 0.5 scale or **840–1,260 ppm** on a 0.7 scale (our conversion; the conversion table below covers each step). ## Lettuce pH: what the sources say

Every extension source we read lands near pH 5.5–6.2, and two trials show what happens on either side.

![Chart of lettuce pH ranges by source on a scale from 5.0 to 7.5: Kentucky 5.5 to 6.0, Alabama 5.6 to 6.0, Virginia 5.5 to 6.2 for NFT greens, the Ohio State trial with equal yield from 5.8 to 6.2, Oklahoma State 6.0 to 7.0, and a Cornell trial where pH 7.0 gave 26 percent lighter heads than pH 5.8. All ranges overlap at 5.8 to 6.0.](/images/guides/hydroponic-lettuce-ph-sources.svg)

*Lettuce pH by source. Kentucky CCD-CP-63; Alabama ANR-2903; Virginia SPES-463 (leafy greens in NFT); Samarakoon et al. 2020 (three Salanova lettuces in NFT); Oklahoma State HLA-6722 (a general table for many crops); Anderson et al. 2017 (butterhead 'Flandria' in deep-water rafts).*

| Source | pH | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| University of Kentucky | 5.5–6.0 | Source water often above 7.0 and needs acid |
| Alabama Extension (ANR-2903) | 5.6–6.0 | Higher is possible with the right chelated iron; pH tends to drop as plants age |
| Virginia Extension (SPES-463) | 5.5–6.2 | Leafy greens in NFT |
| Ohio State and USDA trial | 5.8–6.2 equal | Best yield at 6.0–6.2; lower at 6.4 for 'Red Oakleaf' and 'Red Butter' |
| Cornell trial | 5.8 beat 7.0 | 26% more fresh weight at 5.8 |
| Oklahoma State (HLA-6722) | 6.0–7.0 | General crop table; the outlier |

**The conflict:** Oklahoma State lists lettuce at pH 6.0–7.0, while Kentucky, Alabama and Virginia stop at 6.0–6.2. The trials side with the lower range. At Cornell, pH 7.0 cost a quarter of the harvest. At Ohio State, leaf calcium was deficient above pH 6.2. We recommend **5.8–6.0** and treat anything above 6.2 as a reading to correct.

### Why pH changes what lettuce can take up

Cornell's paper explains that a moderately low pH such as 5.8 keeps most nutrients dissolved and available, while a pH above about 6.5 can make them precipitate out of solution. Iron and phosphorus are the elements most affected. The Ohio State trial measured this in the leaves: **calcium fell steadily as pH rose and was deficient above 6.2**, and iron, manganese, zinc and molybdenum all declined gradually. Lettuce at pH 7.0 in the Cornell trial had less copper, nitrogen and molybdenum in its leaves.

Low pH has costs too. Kentucky and the Ohio State team both give a floor of 5.5 or 5.8, and Kentucky treats a substrate drain reading below 5.5 as a reason to adjust.

## Lettuce EC: how strong to make the solution

EC (electrical conductivity) measures how much fertilizer salt is in the water. Here the sources agree on a band but not on a single number.

![EC ladder for hydroponic lettuce, low at the bottom: 1.0 for seedlings and plugs (Kentucky); 1.3, Purdue's pick across 24 varieties; 1.4, least tipburn but smaller heads (Ohio State); 1.2 to 1.8, the usual growing range (UF/IFAS, Oklahoma State); 1.8, heaviest heads but more tipburn (Ohio State); above about 2.5, dilute (Kentucky); 2.9 to 3.9, less growth (Purdue).](/images/guides/hydroponic-lettuce-ec-ladder.svg)

*EC steps for lettuce in mS/cm, not drawn to scale. Seedling EC and the 2.5 drain limit: University of Kentucky. 1.3 and the 2.9–3.9 slowdown: Purdue HO-309-W. 1.4 and 1.8: Samarakoon et al. 2020. Growing range: UF/IFAS and Oklahoma State.*

| Source | EC (mS/cm) | What it rests on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purdue HO-309-W | 1.3 | 24 varieties in NFT and raft at 1.3, 2.0, 2.9 and 3.9; growth higher at 1.3 and 2.0; best EC the same for every system and variety |
| University of Kentucky | About 1.5; 1.0 for seedlings | Example recipe; adjust substrate drain below 0.8 or above about 2.5 |
| UF/IFAS HS1422 | 1.2–1.8 | Small home and hobby systems |
| Oklahoma State HLA-6722 | 1.2–1.8 | General crop table |
| Virginia SPES-463 | 1.2–2.0 | Leafy greens in NFT |
| Alabama ANR-2903 | 1.15–2.1 above source water | Greenhouse lettuce; 130–150 ppm nitrogen |
| Kratky (University of Hawaii) | About 1.5 at the start | Non-circulating tanks, nothing added later |
| Ohio State and USDA trial | Heaviest at 1.8; least tipburn at 1.4 | NFT greenhouse trial, average 24 °C (75 °F) |

**The conflict:** Purdue found 1.3 grew as well as 2.0 in every variety, while the Ohio State and USDA trial found 'Green Butter' heads were **75 g lighter at 1.4 than at 1.8**. 'Green Butter' and 'Red Butter' peaked at 1.8 or above and 'Red Oakleaf' at 1.6 or above, with no gain from 1.8 to 2.0. Below 1.6, nitrogen, potassium, calcium and sulfur in the leaves fell below sufficiency. But the same trial found tipburn rose from a rating of **1.2 at EC 1.4 to 2.7 at EC 1.8**, and the authors suggest growers who see tipburn may need to run 1.4.

What we recommend from this: **start at EC 1.4–1.5.** If heads are slow and leaves pale with no tipburn, move up toward 1.8. If tipburn shows, come back down toward 1.4. Going above 2.0 gained nothing in either trial, and Purdue found growth fell at 2.9 and 3.9.

### EC to ppm conversion for lettuce

Meters that show ppm (TDS) multiply EC by a fixed factor, usually 500 or 700. Check which scale your meter uses before comparing numbers.

| EC (mS/cm) | ppm, 0.5 scale | ppm, 0.7 scale | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.0 | 500 | 700 | Seedlings |
| 1.2 | 600 | 840 | Low end of growing range |
| 1.3 | 650 | 910 | Purdue's pick |
| 1.4 | 700 | 980 | Least tipburn |
| 1.5 | 750 | 1,050 | Kentucky recipe, Kratky start |
| 1.8 | 900 | 1,260 | Top of growing range |

*Conversions are our arithmetic, EC × 500 or × 700. The [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains the two scales.*

## What a lettuce solution at EC 1.5 contains

Kentucky's example recipe at about EC 1.5 supplies, as printed, **150 ppm nitrogen, 39 phosphorus, 162 potassium, 139 calcium and 47 magnesium**, plus iron at 1.3 ppm and smaller amounts of manganese, zinc, boron, copper and molybdenum. Alabama gives 130–150 ppm nitrogen for lettuce. A complete hydroponic fertilizer mixed to the label usually lands near this; you do not need to build it from single salts. The [how to mix hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) covers order and rates, and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a label dose to your reservoir.

## Source water and why pH drifts

Your tap water decides how hard pH is to hold. Kentucky notes that its water is typically alkaline, above pH 7.0, with alkalinity around 80 ppm (as calcium carbonate), so acid is often needed. Oklahoma State says alkalinity above 75 ppm pushes pH up, and suggests acidic fertilizer, phosphoric or citric acid, vinegar or reverse osmosis to remove it.

pH can move either way once plants are growing:

- **Downward:** Alabama notes pH tends to drop as lettuce ages.
- **Upward:** Cornell's paper notes that topping up with highly alkaline water raises pH and can cut the availability of iron, potassium and phosphorus. In their trial, the alkaline municipal water started at 120 mg/L and was used up within the first few days.

The Cornell team checked pH twice a day and adjusted it daily. That is more than a home grower needs, but in a small reservoir pH can move quickly, which is why Oklahoma State advises a daily check at the same time.

## Step by step: setting and holding pH and EC

1. **Calibrate both meters.** Oklahoma State uses a 1.41 mS/cm standard for EC and buffer 4, 7 or 10 for pH. Our [pH meter guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) covers calibration.
2. **Test your source water** for pH, EC and alkalinity. Alabama gives lettuce EC as an amount above the source water, so note its starting EC.
3. **Mix nutrients to the label**, stir, and let the EC reading settle; Oklahoma State notes this can take a couple of minutes.
4. **Set EC first.** Aim for 1.0 for seedlings and 1.4–1.5 once transplanted. Add concentrate to raise it, water to lower it.
5. **Then set pH** to 5.8–6.0. Oklahoma State says to adjust pH only after EC is in range. Add pH down slowly and wait several minutes before adding more.
6. **Check two or three times a week** (Virginia), or daily in small reservoirs (Oklahoma State). Measure at the same time of day.
7. **Top up every few days** to replace water the plants drink (Kentucky), then recheck EC and pH.
8. **Replace the solution** every 3–4 weeks (Kentucky) or about every two weeks (Oklahoma State). Lettuce often goes from transplant to harvest in about 30 days (Alabama), so one or two changes per crop is typical.

**Kratky jars and tanks are the exception.** In Kratky's method all the solution goes in before planting, starting at about EC 1.5, and nothing is added later; a lettuce plant uses about 3–6 L (0.8–1.6 gal). Check pH at the start rather than chasing it mid-crop. See the [Kratky hydroponics guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/). For which system suits lettuce best, see [hydroponic lettuce systems compared](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-systems/).

## Troubleshooting lettuce pH and EC problems

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slow growth, smaller heads, no obvious spots | pH near 7.0 | Bring pH back to 5.8–6.0 | Anderson et al. 2017 |
| Brown, dry edges on young inner leaves | Tipburn: calcium not reaching growing tips; more likely at higher EC | Drop EC toward 1.4; cut heat and light; grow tolerant cultivars | Samarakoon et al. 2020; Kentucky; e-GRO 2023 |
| Pale plants, slow growth | EC too low, below about 1.4–1.6 | Raise EC gradually toward 1.8 | Samarakoon et al. 2020 |
| Stunted plants, dark leaves | EC too high (2.9 or more) | Dilute with water to 1.4–1.8 | Purdue HO-309-W |
| pH climbs back up after each adjustment | Alkaline source or top-up water | Acidify top-up water; consider RO water | Oklahoma State; Anderson et al. 2017 |
| pH keeps falling late in the crop | Normal as plants age | Raise with pH up; change the solution | Alabama ANR-2903 |
| EC rising while the water level drops | Plants drinking water faster than they take up salts | Top up with plain water, not full-strength solution | Kentucky (top up and recheck) |
| Readings jump around | Meter out of calibration or reading too soon | Recalibrate; wait for the reading to settle | Oklahoma State |

On tipburn, the Ohio State and USDA team also tried calcium chloride sprays. Twice-weekly sprays at 400–800 mg/L calcium cut the tipburn rating of 'Green Butter' from 2.5 to 0.75, but heads were 25–30 g lighter. Adding calcium to the solution itself may not help, they note, because leaf calcium did not rise above EC 1.6. In an Ohio State e-GRO cultivar trial, 'Rabello' and 'Rouxai' showed no visible tipburn at 28 days; picking cultivars like these is simpler than spraying. Choosing seed is covered in [hydroponic lettuce seeds](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-seeds/).

A related warning comes from an Illinois ebb-and-flow trial: a solution kept at low EC (below 1.0) and high pH (about 7.2) cut leaf chlorophyll by up to 37% and marketable yield of basil and kale by 44% and 76% (Wortman 2015, abstract). Lettuce was not reported there, but it shows how much a weak, alkaline solution can cost leafy crops.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best pH for hydroponic lettuce?

5.8–6.0. Kentucky gives 5.5–6.0, and the Ohio State and USDA trial found equal yield from 5.8 to 6.2, with the best results at 6.0–6.2.

### Is pH 6.5 too high for lettuce?

Yes, it is above what most sources recommend. In the Ohio State trial, leaf calcium was deficient above pH 6.2 and two of three varieties yielded less at 6.4. Oklahoma State's general table allows up to 7.0, but the Cornell trial found pH 7.0 cut fresh weight by 26%.

### What EC should lettuce be at?

1.2–1.8 mS/cm for growing plants (UF/IFAS, Oklahoma State), and 1.0 for seedlings (Kentucky). Start at 1.4–1.5 and adjust from how the plants look.

### What ppm is that?

About 600–900 ppm on a 0.5-scale meter, or 840–1,260 ppm on a 0.7-scale meter, for EC 1.2–1.8 (our conversion).

### Will a stronger solution grow bigger lettuce?

Only up to a point. Heads got heavier up to EC 1.8 in the Ohio State trial, with no gain at 2.0, and Purdue found growth fell at 2.9 and 3.9. Higher EC also brought more tipburn.

### How often should I check pH and EC?

Two or three times a week in a larger reservoir (Virginia), and daily in a small one (Oklahoma State).
