**LECA works best in systems that flood and drain or drip, not in ones that rely on the medium to stay wet.** Light expanded clay aggregate (also sold as clay pebbles, grow rocks or Hydroton) is kiln-fired clay with a pH of **about 7.0** that releases almost no nutrients (Oklahoma State). It drains and dries fast and rarely clogs, so Oklahoma State calls it "a great option" for **ebb and flow** and **aquaponics media beds**; Illinois calls it "great" for ebb and flow, and Oregon State lists it for **Dutch buckets**. Washed and sterilized, it lasts **many years**.

Its weak points are water and cost. LECA holds little water: an Auburn University study found one brand absorbed **up to 50% of its own weight** in water in 24 hours, and UF/IFAS rates its water holding **low**. It is **fairly expensive** (Oklahoma State) or **very expensive** (UF/IFAS), **floats for the first few months**, and can bind tightly around roots in buckets. In a Hungarian greenhouse trial, peppers in expanded clay yielded **18.36 kg/m² vs 17.76 in rockwool**, with no significant difference (Slezák et al. 2011).

HydroRigor has not grown in LECA for this guide; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## What LECA is and how it behaves

Expanded clay is made by heating dry clay until it puffs into round, porous pellets (Oklahoma State). UF/IFAS gives sizes from **1 to 18 mm**; the brand Auburn tested was screened to **0.46 cm (3/16 in)**. Three properties decide where it belongs:

- **It drains fast.** Oklahoma State says the pellets "drain and dry very fast, which may cause roots to dry out", and rarely clog. Auburn's thesis describes "a low water holding capacity".
- **It is chemically quiet.** A near-neutral pH of about 7.0 and almost no nutrient release mean the nutrient solution sets the pH and feeds the plant. Keep the solution at **pH 5.5–6.5** (Kentucky) and adjust it, not the pebbles.
- **It lasts.** Unlike rockwool and coir, which UF/IFAS lists as not reusable, expanded clay can be steam or chemically sterilized and used again (UF/IFAS; Oklahoma State).

### Where the sources differ

**Nutrient holding (CEC).** UF/IFAS's media table rates expanded clay's cation exchange capacity **high**. Oklahoma State says it releases "almost no nutrients", and Auburn measured **5.12 meq/100 mL** for one brand. The figures describe different things and products, so we don't rank it; in any recirculating or drain-to-waste system the solution, not the pebble, is what feeds the roots.

**How much water.** Sources describe it in words ("low", "do not have good water-holding capacity") rather than numbers. The one figure we found, up to 50% of its weight in 24 hours, came from Auburn's preliminary studies of a single brand. No source gives a percentage by volume, so we can't compare it directly with perlite or coir.

### Ebb and flow

This is where LECA earns its price. Each flood fills the tray, then drains back and pulls air into the medium (Oregon State). Because clay pebbles hold little water, they need flooding again before they dry: Oregon State's rule is to flood to **about 1 in (2.5 cm) below the top of the media**, drain once it is soaked, and flood again before the media dries. Flood lengths in the sources run from **about 10 minutes** (UMass, for greenhouse benches at ¾–1 in depth) to **20–30 minutes** (Illinois). Our [ebb and flow guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) has the full timing table.

### Dutch buckets

Oregon State lists expanded clay with perlite, coir, washed river rock and gravel for buckets, and advises a screen on every bucket outlet so media stays out of the pipes. Oklahoma State adds a warning specific to buckets: clay pellets "often bind tightly around roots", which makes cleaning out a bucket at the end of a crop harder. Perlite is the more common bucket fill; see our [Dutch bucket guide](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/).

### DWC, Kratky and net pots

The photo at the top of this page shows [net pots](/equipment/hydroponic-net-pots/) topped up with clay pebbles around the plants. Virginia Tech propagates in rockwool, foam or peat/coir cubes and sets no fill medium for the pot, and no source we read tested pebbles against a bare plug. It can work, but it is a grower's habit, not a researched practice. See our [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) and [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

## What the trials found

Few trials test LECA for food crops. The clearest is a Corvinus University of Budapest study that grew four vegetables in bags or containers of expanded clay (5 L, about 1.3 gal, per plant for pepper and tomato) against standard media:

| Crop | Expanded clay (kg/m²) | Standard medium (kg/m²) | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sweet pepper | 18.36 | Rockwool 17.76 | No significant difference | Slezák et al. 2011 Table 2 |
| Tomato | 26.82–27.04 | Rockwool 28.09 | Rockwool slightly higher | Slezák et al. 2011 Table 3 |
| Cucumber | 17.59–18.40 | Peat + perlite 21.58 | Clay 15–20% lower (authors) | Slezák et al. 2011 Table 5 |

The two clay figures for tomato and cucumber are two grades of the same product. The authors concluded expanded clay is suitable as a root medium for all four crops they grew; the cucumber result, below a water-holding peat mix, fits the sources' warning that clay dries fast. For reference, 18.36 kg/m² is about 3.8 lb per sq ft (HydroRigor's conversion). The trial reported no pH, EC or water-use figures, and no trial we found tested LECA for lettuce or herbs.

For how LECA compares with other media, see our [growing media guide](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/) and [rockwool vs coco coir guide](/equipment/rockwool-vs-coco-coir/).

## How to use LECA, step by step

These steps follow the sources above. They are a starting point, not a tested HydroRigor method.

1. **Pick the system first.** Use LECA for ebb and flow, buckets or media beds; keep it out of NFT channels and seed trays (Virginia Tech; UF/IFAS).
2. **Rinse new pebbles** to wash off dust and fine grit. None of our sources gives a method or a soak time, so rinse until the water runs clear.
3. **Expect floating.** Oklahoma State says new pellets float for the first few months until saturated and can be drawn into filters and drain lines. Fit screens on drains and bucket outlets (Oregon State) and check filters often at first.
4. **Plant a started seedling,** in its rockwool, foam or peat/coir plug, into the pebbles (Virginia Tech).
5. **Water often enough.** Flood or drip again before the pebbles dry (Oregon State); expect to water more often than with coir or perlite.
6. **Manage the solution, not the medium.** Keep pH at **5.5–6.5** (Kentucky) and use the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) for crop ranges.
7. **Clean between crops** (next section).

## Cleaning and reusing LECA

![Four steps to reuse LECA. 1: remove old roots and debris. 2: wash off grit and residue. 3: sterilize at 180 degrees Fahrenheit (82 Celsius) for 30 minutes, or soak in 10 percent bleach for 20 minutes. 4: rinse several times in tap water, then replant.](/images/guides/leca-hydroponics-reuse-steps.svg)

*Reuse steps from Oklahoma State HLA-6728; UF/IFAS EP623 lists expanded clay as reusable after steam or chemical sterilization.*

Reuse is LECA's main advantage over rockwool and coir. Oklahoma State gives two methods for media in general: **pasteurize at 180 °F (82 °C) for 30 minutes**, or **soak in a 10% bleach solution for 20 minutes**, then rinse several times with tap water. UF/IFAS lists expanded clay with sand, gravel and pumice as reusable after steam (above 180 °F) or chemical sterilization. Neither gives a number of reuses; Oklahoma State says only that the pellets "can last for many years". Oregon State warns that reusing materials is a main cause of disease spread in home systems, which is why the sterilizing step matters.

| Task | How | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remove roots | Pick or rinse out; roots bind tightly in buckets | Oklahoma State |
| Heat | 180 °F (82 °C) for 30 minutes | Oklahoma State; UF/IFAS EP623 |
| Bleach | 10% solution, 20 minutes, then several rinses | Oklahoma State |
| How often reusable | "Many years"; no count given | Oklahoma State |

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pebbles floating off the tray | New pellets float for months | Expected; weigh down or wait | Oklahoma State |
| Drain or filter blocked | Floating pellets drawn into lines | Screens on outlets; check filters | Oklahoma State; Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Plants wilt between floods | LECA dries fast | Flood again before the media dries | Oklahoma State; Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Salt crust near the base of plants | Salt buildup in flood systems | Shallow floods; occasional plain-water flood | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Roots matted into pebbles | Clay binds tightly around roots | Soak and pick out; expect it in buckets | Oklahoma State |
| Disease after reuse | Pebbles not sterilized | Heat or bleach, then rinse | Oklahoma State; Oregon State EM 9456 |
| pH drifting | Solution, not the clay (pH about 7.0) | Adjust the solution to 5.5–6.5 | Oklahoma State; Kentucky |
| Seeds fail in pebbles | LECA isn't a seed-starting medium | Start in rockwool, foam or peat/coir plugs | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |

## Frequently asked questions

### Is LECA good for hydroponics?

Yes, for ebb and flow, Dutch buckets and aquaponics media beds, where its fast drainage and reuse pay off (Oklahoma State; Illinois; Oregon State). It is a poor fit for seed starting and NFT channels.

### What pH is LECA?

About 7.0, and it releases almost no nutrients (Oklahoma State), so set the nutrient solution to pH 5.5–6.5 (Kentucky).

### Do I need to soak LECA before use?

Rinse it to remove dust. No source we read gives a soak time; Oklahoma State notes new pellets float for months until saturated.

### Can you reuse LECA?

Yes. Wash it, then pasteurize at 180 °F (82 °C) for 30 minutes or soak in 10% bleach for 20 minutes and rinse (Oklahoma State). It can last many years.

### Does LECA hold water?

Not much. UF/IFAS rates its water holding low, and one brand absorbed up to 50% of its weight in 24 hours (Auburn). That is why it suits systems that water often.

### Is LECA better than rockwool?

For peppers in bags, yields were about equal (18.36 vs 17.76 kg/m², Slezák et al. 2011); for tomatoes, rockwool was slightly higher. LECA can be reused; UF/IFAS says rockwool cannot. For seed starting, rockwool cubes are the standard (Virginia Tech).

### Can I use LECA in Kratky or DWC?

You can fill around a plug in a net pot, but no source we read tested it or sets a fill medium for those systems (Virginia Tech).
