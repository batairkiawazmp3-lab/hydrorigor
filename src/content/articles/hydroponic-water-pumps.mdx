**Choose a hydroponic water pump by the flow it delivers at your lift height, not by the number on the box.** Virginia Tech gives **3 to 5 gallons per hour (about 11–19 L/h) into each NFT channel**, so a four-channel home system needs about 12–20 gph at the channels. Research and commercial guidance runs higher, at **1–2 liters per minute (about 16–32 gph) per channel** (Purdue; Sato et al.). Every foot you lift the water costs flow: **2.31 feet of head equals 1 psi**, and a pump gives its highest flow at the lowest head (North Dakota State; LSU AgCenter). Oklahoma State's 5–6 ft tower build uses a **400 gph submersible pump**.

Use a continuous-duty pump (Virginia Tech), plug it into a ground-fault (GFCI) outlet (University of Maine), and keep a spare: Missouri and Oregon State warn that NFT plants can wilt right away when the pump stops.

HydroRigor has not tested pumps; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end, and where we did arithmetic, we say so.

## Do you need a water pump at all?

Not every system has one. Kratky needs **no pumps or electricity** (UF/IFAS), and deep water culture uses an **air pump**, not a water pump, to oxygenate the tank (see our [DWC air pump guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/)). Missouri calls DWC more forgiving for beginners because plants don't wilt immediately if the air pump fails. If either fits your crops, see our [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) or [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

You need a water pump for any system that moves solution from a reservoir to the roots and lets it drain back: NFT, towers, ebb and flow, drip and Dutch buckets, and aeroponics. The [types of hydroponic systems guide](/systems/types-of-hydroponic-systems/) compares them.

## Submersible or external (inline)?

| | Submersible | External (inline) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where it sits | In the reservoir | Outside, beside the tank | North Dakota State AE1057 |
| Priming | Not needed | Must be filled with water before it runs | North Dakota State AE1057 |
| Suction lift | None; already under water | Limited; within 20 vertical ft of the water | North Dakota State AE1057 |
| Typical use | Most small systems | Larger systems (pool-type centrifugal) | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Watch for | Needs water moving past the motor | Clogged suction lines or filters cause cavitation | North Dakota State; UF/IFAS AE579 |

For a home system, a **submersible pump** is the usual choice: Virginia Tech says most small NFT systems use one. North Dakota State notes that submersible motors are less efficient than surface motors and need water moving past them to stay cool, so never let the level drop below the pump. Whatever the type, Virginia Tech says to use a **continuous-duty** pump sized for **head and friction**.

## How to read pump flow and head

A pump's box usually gives one flow number. That is its best case. The useful figure is the flow at the height you need to lift the water.

**Head.** North Dakota State defines total dynamic head as the vertical distance the pump lifts the water, plus pressure head, plus friction in the pipe and fittings (plus velocity head, which it calls usually negligible). For a home system, the vertical lift is the distance from the water surface in the reservoir to where the solution comes out at the top.

**The curve.** LSU AgCenter: "The highest head occurs at the lowest flow rate, and the highest flow rate occurs at the lowest head." Lift the water higher and the same pump delivers less. The height where flow drops to zero is the **shut-off head**.

**Pressure.** A column of water **2.31 feet (0.70 m)** high equals **1 psi** (North Dakota State; LSU). UF/IFAS rounds it to 2.3 feet. A 5-foot lift is about 2.2 psi by our arithmetic.

**Friction.** LSU says friction loss rises roughly with the square of the water's speed, so narrow tubing and many elbows eat flow. UF/IFAS suggests keeping water speed at or below **5 ft/s (1.5 m/s)** and counts a 2-inch PVC elbow as about **5.17 feet of straight pipe**.

**Efficiency.** A pump runs at peak efficiency only in a narrow band of flow and head (North Dakota State). For centrifugal pumps, UF/IFAS puts the best efficiency point at about **75% of shut-off head** for 3600 rpm pumps. Small hydroponic pumps don't always publish a curve; when one does, aim to work in its middle, not at either end.

## NFT flow: a source conflict

This is the number most pump pages skip, and the sources disagree by a factor of about five:

| Source | Flow per channel | In gph | Context |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Virginia Tech SPES-463 | 3–5 gph | 3–5 | Extension guide, small and commercial NFT |
| Minnesota Extension | 8–10 oz/min | About 3.8–4.7 | Table-top unit, 4-ft channels, 25-gal tank |
| Purdue (Langenhoven) | 1 L/min, upper limit 2 | About 16–32 | Channels up to 10–15 m (33–49 ft) |
| Sato et al. 2007 | 1–2 L/min "widely recommended" | About 16–32 | Research, citing Cooper and Jones |
| Alabama ANR-2903 | Under ½ gal/min | Under 30 | Greenhouse troughs 8–12 ft |
| Texas A&M 2018 | 5 L/min | About 79 | 6-m (20-ft) research trough |

The gph conversions are ours. Our reading: the two home-scale figures, Virginia Tech's and Minnesota's, agree at about **3–5 gph per channel**, and both come from systems with short channels like a home build. The 1–2 L/min figures come from long commercial or research channels, where more solution has to reach plants far down the line. For a home system with channels under about 8 ft, start near 4 gph per channel and adjust with a valve until every plant's roots sit in a thin, moving film. Virginia Tech says valves can adjust flow to each channel. Our [NFT system guide](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) covers slope and channel length.

## How to size a pump, step by step

These steps combine the figures above. They are a planning method, not a tested HydroRigor build.

1. **Find the flow you need.** Multiply channels, towers or emitters by the per-unit flow from the quick-answer table. Four NFT channels × 3–5 gph = **12–20 gph**. Ten 0.5 gph drip stakes = **5 gph**.
2. **Measure the lift.** From the reservoir's water surface to the highest outlet. Oklahoma State's tower is 5–6 ft (1.5–1.8 m) tall.
3. **Allow for friction.** Count elbows and long runs of narrow tubing; LSU and UF/IFAS say both cost head.
4. **Read the pump's chart at your head,** not its headline number. Choose one that still gives your flow there, with some to spare: UF/IFAS suggests a slightly oversized pump, and Virginia Tech's valves let you turn flow down.
5. **Check the reservoir.** The pump must stay covered when the channels are full; see our [reservoir size guide](/equipment/hydroponic-reservoir-size/).
6. **Test with plain water.** Time a bucket: UF/IFAS's example is a 1-gallon bucket filling in **30 seconds = 2 gpm**. For small flows, time a measuring cup at the end of a channel.
7. **Plug into a GFCI outlet.** The University of Maine says to use ground-fault outlets wherever water is present and to avoid extension cords unless absolutely necessary.
8. **Keep a backup.** Virginia Tech recommends a backup pump; Oregon State says backups for power and pumps are crucial in NFT.

## Timers and run schedules

| System | Schedule | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFT | Runs continuously; no timer | Oklahoma State HLA-6442 |
| NFT, organic solution (trial) | 15 min on, 105 off grew the most lettuce | Liu et al. 2025 |
| Tower, lettuce | Continuous, 24 h | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Tower, strawberries | 7–10 times a day for 1 h | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Ebb and flow | 20–30 min flood; needs a reliable timer | Illinois; Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Aeroponics | Short-cycle timer every few minutes; 3 on, 12 off in one trial | Oklahoma State HLA-6442; Georgia 2025 |
| Media drip | Small growers often use a simple on/off timer | UF/IFAS HS1274 |

The intermittent NFT result comes from an organic-nutrient trial at Texas A&M, where continuous flow let biofilm coat the roots; the authors noted it also saved pump energy. It has not been shown for standard mineral solutions, so keep conventional NFT running continuously. For flood timing, see our [ebb and flow guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/); for towers, the [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/).

## Power failures and backups

Water culture systems differ in how long plants last without the pump:

| System | If the pump stops | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFT | Plants may wilt immediately as the film stops | Missouri G6986; Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Aeroponics | Highest risk of plant loss of the water-based systems | Missouri G6986 |
| DWC (air pump) | Plants don't wilt immediately | Missouri G6986 |

None of these sources gives a number of minutes before damage, so we don't either. Practical cover: a spare pump on the shelf (Virginia Tech), and backup power for NFT and aeroponics (Oregon State). The [aeroponics vs hydroponics guide](/systems/aeroponics-vs-hydroponics/) covers misting risk.

## Troubleshooting hydroponic pumps

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weak flow at the top | Lift too high for the pump | Choose by flow at your head, not headline gph | LSU 3241-H; North Dakota State |
| Flow drops over weeks | Clogged intake, filter or tubing | Clean the intake and lines; clean the system between crops | UF/IFAS AE579; Minnesota Extension |
| Pump hot, noisy or rattling | Running outside its range, or starved of water | Keep the intake covered; work mid-curve | LSU 3241-H; UF/IFAS AE579 |
| Uneven flow between channels | Unbalanced lines | Add a valve to each channel | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Plants wilt suddenly in NFT | Pump or power failure | Restore flow; keep a spare pump | Missouri G6986; Virginia Tech |
| Solution too warm | Black tubing, warm room | White tubing reflects heat | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Algae clogging tubing | Light reaching the solution | Opaque tank and lid | Minnesota Extension |
| Tripped outlet | Water reached a plug | Dry everything; use a GFCI outlet | University of Maine 2311 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What size pump do I need for hydroponics?

Add up the flow: about 3–5 gph per NFT channel at home (Virginia Tech), 0.5 gph per Dutch bucket stake (Virginia Tech), then pick a pump that still delivers it at your lift height. Oklahoma State used a 400 gph pump for a 5–6 ft tower.

### What does "max head" mean on a pump?

It is the height at which the pump's flow drops to zero, its shut-off head. Flow falls as head rises (LSU AgCenter), so you need a pump whose maximum head is well above your lift.

### What flow rate for NFT?

Virginia Tech gives 3–5 gph per channel and Minnesota's table-top unit ran 8–10 oz per minute; Purdue and Sato et al. give 1–2 L/min for longer commercial channels. Start near the home figure and adjust.

### Should an NFT pump run all the time?

Yes for standard nutrient solutions: Oklahoma State says NFT has constant flow, with no timer. An intermittent schedule helped in one organic-nutrient trial (Liu et al. 2025).

### Submersible or inline pump?

Submersible for most small systems (Virginia Tech). An external pump must be primed and sit within 20 vertical feet of the water (North Dakota State).

### Does DWC need a water pump?

No. Deep water culture uses an air pump to oxygenate the tank; Missouri says plants don't wilt immediately if it fails.

### How do I measure my pump's real flow?

Time how long it takes to fill a known volume at the outlet. UF/IFAS's example: a 1-gallon bucket filling in 30 seconds is 2 gallons per minute.
