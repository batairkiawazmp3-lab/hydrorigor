**Aeroponics is a type of hydroponics: instead of sitting in solution, the roots hang in a dark chamber and are misted with it on a timer.** For a home grower the useful question is whether misting beats the simpler water-culture systems, deep water culture (DWC) and the nutrient film technique (NFT). The research says: **not for lettuce, as far as yield goes.** In a Texas A&M trial, NFT lettuce shoots weighed **about twice** the aeroponic ones; a 2025 University of Georgia trial concluded lettuce is best grown in **DWC or NFT**; a Cornell project found **no difference** against DWC. Aeroponics did grow much larger roots, and the Georgia trial found arugula did best in it.

The price is risk. Missouri Extension ranks aeroponics highest of the water-based systems for build cost and for the chance of losing plants, because the roots have no water around them when a pump or nozzle fails. For most homes, DWC or NFT is the better first system; aeroponics suits growers who want to experiment, and crops grown for their roots.

HydroRigor has not built or tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## How aeroponics differs from DWC and NFT

All three are hydroponic: no soil, and the solution drains back to a reservoir and is reused. What changes is where the roots sit.

**Aeroponics.** Plants sit in holes in the lid of a closed, dark box. A pump pushes solution to nozzles inside, which spray the hanging roots for a few seconds or minutes, then stop. Missouri says it needs **high-quality water and pressurized misters**, and Oklahoma State says it needs a **short-cycle timer that runs every few minutes**. Between mists, the roots sit in moist air.

**Deep water culture.** Roots hang in a tank of solution, and an air pump bubbles oxygen into it. Virginia Tech gives **7–10 ppm dissolved oxygen, and at least 4 ppm**, for DWC. Missouri calls DWC more forgiving for beginners because plants don't wilt immediately if the air pump fails. Our [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) covers the setup.

**NFT.** A thin film of solution runs down a sloped channel past the roots. Missouri gives a **1–2% slope**; both trials below used 1%. Plants may wilt immediately if the pump fails. See the [NFT system guide](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/).

## What the yield trials found

Most pages comparing these systems say aeroponics grows plants faster without citing a trial. We found four studies of lettuce that compared aeroponics with water culture directly, and they don't agree.

**Texas A&M, 2018 (Horticulturae).** Lettuce grew in aeroponic A-frames, NFT channels and a peat-perlite substrate in a glasshouse for 45 days after transplanting. NFT shoots weighed **approximately twice** the aeroponic ones, fresh and dry. Aeroponic plants had a **root:shoot ratio almost three times** that of NFT, and in one variety **four to five times** the root length, area and volume. The authors wrote that the bigger roots "did not lead to more shoot biomass", and concluded aeroponics may suit **true root crops**. Two notes: the aeroponic system broke down for a day during the trial, and the study was funded by the nozzle maker, whose employee was a co-author.

**University of Georgia, 2025 (HortScience).** Lettuce and arugula grew in DWC, NFT, a vertical tower and aeroponics in a greenhouse. For lettuce, the only significant fresh-weight difference was DWC beating the tower by **30%**; aeroponic lettuce had **13% less** dry shoot weight than DWC and **13% less** leaf area than the other systems. Arugula went the other way: **48% more** fresh shoot weight in the three recirculating systems than DWC, and **91% more** root dry weight in aeroponics. The authors concluded "arugula is optimally grown in aeroponics, and lettuce is optimally grown in DWC or NFT."

**Cornell, 2021 (master's project).** Several aeroponic irrigation timings were compared with a DWC control. The abstract reports **no difference in shoot fresh or dry weight** between DWC and the aeroponic treatments. We could read only the abstract, so its timing values aren't on this page.

**Texas Tech (poster abstract, year not given).** Ten White Boston lettuce plants grew in aeroponics or DWC for 53 days. At week 5, aeroponic heads weighed **211 g against 67 g**, and roots were more extensive. But the aeroponic system **used about twice the water**. It is a student poster, not a peer-reviewed paper, with no EC, pH, temperature or light reported, so we give it the least weight.

### Why the results disagree

The trials used different nozzles, mist schedules, varieties and seasons, and the systems they compared differ too (NFT in one, DWC in two). The Texas A&M authors suggest roots sitting in solution all the time get a steadier supply of water and nutrients than roots misted at intervals, which pushes growth into roots instead of leaves. They add that pressure, droplet size and misting interval for leafy crops still need research. Our reading: a well-run aeroponic system can match water culture for lettuce, but none of the peer-reviewed trials shows it beating it.

## Water use: a source conflict

Missouri ranks aeroponics **lowest** of the water-based systems for water and fertilizer use, with DWC highest. The Texas Tech poster found its aeroponic system **used about twice the water** of DWC, though its water use efficiency was numerically higher. Both can be true: water use depends on how much the system loses to evaporation and leaks, and on how much the plants grow. Neither source measured a home unit, so don't choose aeroponics to save water without checking your own reservoir top-ups.

## Mist timing, droplets and nozzles

No source we read gives one schedule for home aeroponics. The four studies with settings ran very different cycles:

| Study | Mist on | Mist off | Share of time on | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Texas A&M, 2018 | 20 s | 30 s | 40% | Texas A&M 2018 |
| Georgia, 2025 | 3 min | 12 min | 20% | Georgia 2025 |
| Texas Tech (seedling) | 30 s | 4 min | About 11% | Texas Tech |
| Texas Tech (vegetative) | 30 s | 8 min | About 6% | Texas Tech |
| Texas Tech (mature) | 30 s | 6 min | About 8% | Texas Tech |
| Jiangsu, 2021 | 5 min | Repeated every 30, 45 or 60 min | Not stated | Jiangsu 2021 |

The "share of time on" column is our arithmetic from each schedule (Georgia states its 20% itself). Oklahoma State's general rule, a timer that runs every few minutes, fits the first three studies.

*Mist cycles in four studies. Percentages are HydroRigor arithmetic from each schedule.*

**Droplets.** Texas A&M set its nozzles to **50 µm** droplets. Jiangsu University compared four nozzles with droplets of **4.89 to 26.35 µm**: a high-pressure nozzle with air, a low-pressure nozzle without air, and ultrasonic nozzles. The **air-assisted nozzle (11.24 µm)** held the solution's pH and EC steadiest and gave better phosphorus, potassium and magnesium uptake, and the authors recommend it at a **30-minute interval with 5 minutes of spray**. The ultrasonic nozzle showed **no significant response** to the spray interval.

**Pressure.** Texas A&M's nozzles were rated for 0.7–4 bar (about 10–58 psi), but the paper doesn't say what pressure it used, and the Georgia paper gives none either. Home aeroponics guides often quote a specific pressure for "high-pressure aeroponics"; we found no university source for one, so we don't give it. For a kit, follow the maker's settings.

## The nutrient solution and growing conditions

The Georgia trial ran all four systems from one solution, targeted at **pH 5.5–6.5 and EC 1.25–1.75**. Its aeroponic reservoir ran higher in pH than the others: **6.6 for lettuce against 5.8–6.1** in DWC, NFT and the tower. Check pH more often in aeroponics.

| Setting | Aeroponics (trial) | Lettuce in water culture | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH | 5.5–6.5 target | 5.5–6.0 (Kentucky); 6.0–7.0 (Oklahoma State) | Georgia 2025; Kentucky CCD-CP-63; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| EC (mS/cm) | 1.25–1.75 target | About 1.5 (Kentucky); 1.2–1.8 (UF/IFAS, Oklahoma State) | Georgia 2025; Kentucky; UF/IFAS HS1422; HLA-6722 |
| Solution temperature | 26.7 °C (80 °F) measured | 65–80 °F (18–27 °C) | Georgia 2025; UF/IFAS HS1422 |
| Dissolved oxygen | Not a target in misted roots | 7–10 ppm (Virginia Tech); 6–8 ppm (Kentucky) | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Kentucky |
| Light | DLI 20.2 (greenhouse) | DLI 12–17 for lettuce | Georgia 2025; Kentucky |
| Spacing | 20 cm (8 in) | 3–4 plants per sq ft in a raft | Texas A&M; Georgia; Kentucky |
| Days to harvest | 28 after transplant (Georgia); 45 (Texas A&M) | 21–35 after transplant | Georgia 2025; Texas A&M 2018; Kentucky |

Where the sources differ: Kentucky's lettuce pH (5.5–6.0) and Oklahoma State's (6.0–7.0) touch only at 6.0, which also sits inside Georgia's 5.5–6.5 target, so aim near **6.0**. In the Georgia greenhouse, with air at 25.7 °C (78 °F), the DWC tank reached **28.8 °C (84 °F)** while the aeroponic reservoir stayed at 26.7 °C (80 °F); both are above UF/IFAS's 80 °F (27 °C) ceiling for small lettuce systems, so keep any reservoir cool. The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) has crop ranges, and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) works out amounts.

## How to set up a small aeroponic system, step by step

These steps follow the settings in the studies above. They are a starting point, not a tested HydroRigor build.

1. **Use a closed, dark chamber.** A lidded tote or box with net-pot holes; light should not reach the roots or the solution (Texas A&M used closed A-frames with foam sides).
2. **Space the holes 20 cm (8 in) apart.** Both trials spaced lettuce at about 20 cm.
3. **Fit nozzles to wet every root.** Texas A&M placed its nozzles at the ends and middle of each unit; Georgia used 16 nozzles across its system.
4. **Add a filter and use clean water.** Missouri says aeroponics needs high-quality water; fine nozzles clog.
5. **Set a short-cycle timer.** Start near Georgia's 3 minutes on, 12 off, or Texas Tech's 30 seconds on, 4 minutes off for seedlings, and make sure the timer handles seconds (Oklahoma State HLA-6442).
6. **Mix the solution.** pH 5.5–6.5 and EC 1.25–1.75 (Georgia 2025); aim near pH 6.0 for lettuce.
7. **Test with plain water first.** Run a full cycle with the lid off and check that every nozzle sprays.
8. **Plant and watch the roots.** Lift a net pot every few days: roots should be white and moist.
9. **Check pH often.** Georgia's aeroponic reservoir ran higher in pH than the other systems.
10. **Plan for failure.** Missouri rates aeroponics highest for plant loss; a battery backup or an alarm on the pump is worth having.

## Troubleshooting aeroponics

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plants wilt suddenly | Pump, timer or power failed; roots dried | Restore mist at once; add backup power or an alarm | Missouri G6986 |
| One plant wilts, others fine | Clogged or misaimed nozzle | Clean the nozzle; check spray covers every root | Missouri G6986 |
| Big roots, small heads | Misting favors root growth | Expected for lettuce; try DWC or NFT if heads matter most | Texas A&M 2018 |
| pH drifting up | Aeroponic reservoir ran higher in one trial | Check and adjust pH more often | Georgia 2025 |
| Reservoir needs frequent top-ups | Evaporation or leaks | Seal the chamber; check for overspray | Texas Tech |
| Warm solution | Warm room or lights | Keep it at or below 80 °F (27 °C) | UF/IFAS HS1422 |

## Frequently asked questions

### Is aeroponics better than hydroponics?

Aeroponics is a kind of hydroponics. For lettuce, two peer-reviewed trials found DWC or NFT did as well or better (Texas A&M 2018; Georgia 2025), and a Cornell project found no difference. Aeroponics grew larger roots and did best for arugula.

### Does aeroponics grow faster?

Not in the peer-reviewed lettuce trials we read. Only a Texas Tech student poster found heavier aeroponic heads, and that system used about twice the water.

### Does aeroponics use less water?

Missouri ranks it lowest of the water-based systems for water and fertilizer use, but the Texas Tech poster measured about twice the water of DWC. It depends on how well the system is sealed.

### How often should aeroponic misters run?

The studies range from 20 seconds on and 30 off (Texas A&M) to 3 minutes on and 12 off (Georgia) to 5 minutes every 30 minutes (Jiangsu). Oklahoma State says to use a short-cycle timer that runs every few minutes.

### What droplet size does aeroponics need?

Texas A&M used 50 µm; Jiangsu tested 4.89–26.35 µm and found the air-assisted 11.24 µm nozzle worked best. No source sets one size for home systems.

### What is the biggest risk with aeroponics?

Losing plants when a pump, timer or nozzle fails. Missouri ranks aeroponics highest of the water-based systems for that risk and for build cost.

### Is a vertical tower aeroponic?

Not necessarily. In the Georgia trial the tower was a separate system from aeroponics, with solution running down past the roots. Our [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) covers how towers work.
