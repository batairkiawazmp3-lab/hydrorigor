The best herbs for hydroponics are **basil, parsley, mint, chives, oregano, cilantro and thyme**, the seven Iowa State University Extension lists as doing best indoors. They can share one system: run the solution at **pH 5.5–6.0** (Iowa State's Christopher Currey) and **EC 1.2–2.0 mS/cm**, the range Virginia Tech gives for leafy greens and herbs in NFT. Give them **12–16 hours of light a day** with the lamp 6–12 in (15–30 cm) from the leaves (Iowa State), aim for a daily light integral (DLI) of **at least 17 mol/m²/day** (Virginia Tech), and space plants about **8 in (20 cm) apart** (Virginia Tech; Currey and colleagues).

Rosemary, sage, lemon balm, marjoram, chervil and bay can work too, but Iowa State warns they need more light than usual or attract pests such as spider mites. Below, each herb's numbers, what the research says about light and feeding, and how to set up a mixed herb system.

## The best herbs for hydroponics, ranked by ease

Iowa State's list is written for indoor pots, but the reasons a herb succeeds indoors (modest light, compact roots, regrowth after cutting) are the same ones that matter in a home hydroponic unit. Virginia Tech's system trials found greens and herbs grew best in NFT and deep water culture, and Oregon State lists basil, cilantro and mint among the common crops for ebb and flow.

| Herb | EC (mS/cm) | pH | Ease | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basil | 1.0–1.6 | 5.5–6.0 | Easy | Cut 3 weeks after transplant (Walters et al. 2023); leaves from 6 weeks after sowing (University of Florida) |
| Parsley | 1.8–2.2 | 6.0–6.5 | Easy | Fresh mass up 120% under more light (Currey et al. 2019) |
| Mint | Not given | Not given | Easy | Spearmint cut 3 weeks after transplant (Walters et al. 2023) |
| Chives | Not given | Not given | Easy | Perennial: cut the top one-third (Iowa State) |
| Oregano | Not given | Not given | Easy | Perennial: cut the top one-third (Iowa State) |
| Cilantro | 0.5–4.0 range tested | 6.0 in trial | Easy | Fresh mass up 154% under more light (Currey et al. 2019) |
| Thyme | Not given | Not given | Easy | Perennial: cut the top one-third (Iowa State) |
| Dill | 0.5–4.0 range tested | 6.0 in trial | Moderate | Fresh mass up 241% under more light (Currey et al. 2019) |
| Sage | 1.0–1.6 | 5.5–6.5 | Harder | Higher light need; cut 5 weeks after transplant (Iowa State; Walters et al. 2023) |
| Rosemary | Not given | Not given | Harder | Higher light need, spider mites (Iowa State) |

*EC and pH: Oklahoma State HLA-6722 Table 2 for basil, parsley and sage. Cilantro and dill: the range tested by Currey, Walters and Flax (2019), which found no yield difference across it. Ease: Iowa State's two lists; dill is not on either, and we rank it moderate because it was the herb most dependent on light in the Iowa State trial.*

**What is missing:** none of the extension or research sources we read gives an EC or pH target for mint, chives, oregano, thyme or rosemary. Grow them in the shared range below rather than chasing a number nobody has published.

## One solution for a mixed herb garden

Most home growers keep several herbs in one reservoir, so the real question is which single EC and pH suit them all. Two findings make that easier than it looks.

![Bar chart of EC targets for hydroponic herbs: basil and sage 1.0 to 1.6, parsley 1.8 to 2.2, a shared range of 1.2 to 2.0 for mixed herbs, and 0.5 to 4.0 as the range that gave the same yields of basil, parsley, dill and cilantro in Iowa State research.](/images/guides/best-herbs-hydroponics-ec-targets.svg)

*EC targets by herb (Oklahoma State HLA-6722 Table 2), the shared NFT range (Virginia Tech SPES-463) and the range that gave the same yields in Iowa State trials (Currey et al. 2019; Currey 2024).*

**Herbs tolerate a wide EC.** In an Iowa State NFT trial, cilantro, dill and parsley grew the same at every EC from 0.5 to 4.0 mS/cm, at both low and high light. Currey later wrote that the same range "didn't affect yields" of basil, parsley, dill and cilantro, but that 4.0 is "simply a waste of fertilizer" and 0.5 may let nutrient imbalances develop sooner.

**The published targets still differ by herb.** Oklahoma State lists basil and sage at EC 1.0–1.6 and parsley at 1.8–2.2. These are recommended ranges, not limits; Currey's trials show herbs will not fail just outside them.

**pH: a small conflict.** Currey keeps herb solutions at pH 5.5–6.0, Virginia Tech gives 5.5–6.2 for NFT greens and herbs, and Oklahoma State lists parsley at 6.0–6.5 and sage at 5.5–6.5. All of them overlap at about **pH 6.0**, the value the Iowa State herb trial held. Newer Ohio State and Iowa State work, Currey notes, found pH as low as 4.0–4.5 did not harm herbs and may help suppress Pythium root rot, but he presents that as a possible change rather than a standard.

**What we recommend:** a mixed herb system at **EC 1.2–2.0 and pH 5.8–6.0**. Start near 1.2–1.6 if basil dominates and near 2.0 if parsley does. Currey adds that basil needs more magnesium than most crops and that calcium-heavy fertilizers can suppress its uptake, so use a complete hydroponic fertilizer rather than calcium nitrate alone. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) shows how to mix to a target EC, the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a label rate to your reservoir, and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists the crop ranges in one place.

## Light: the factor that matters most

If EC hardly changes herb yields, light changes them a great deal. In the same Iowa State trial, raising the DLI from 7 to 18 mol/m²/day increased fresh mass by **154% for cilantro, 241% for dill and 120% for parsley**, whatever the EC.

![Bar chart of the increase in fresh mass when daily light rose from 7 to 18 moles per square meter per day: dill 241 percent, cilantro 154 percent, parsley 120 percent.](/images/guides/best-herbs-hydroponics-light-response.svg)

*Fresh mass gain at DLI 18 compared with DLI 7, NFT, 4 weeks after transplant (Currey, Walters and Flax, Agronomy 2019).*

Other trials point the same way:

- **Spearmint:** fresh mass rose by 60 g as the DLI rose from 6.1 to 19.4 mol/m²/day at 23 °C (73 °F) (Walters, Tarr and Lopez, 2023).
- **Sage:** at 22.8 °C (73 °F), raising the DLI from 4.9 to 18.3 gave 2.4 times the fresh mass (Walters et al. 2023).
- **Basil:** in University of Florida small-scale units, basil grown indoors at DLI 9 and a constant 21 °C (70 °F) yielded 344–359 g per four-plant unit, against 1,417–1,452 g in a greenhouse at DLI 26.1 (Solis-Toapanta, Fisher and Gómez, 2020). The greenhouse was also warmer, so not all of that gap is light.

| Source | Hours a day | Intensity or DLI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iowa State | 12–16 on a timer | Lamp 6–12 in (15–30 cm) from the leaves |
| Virginia Tech SPES-466 | Not given | DLI at least 17 for greens and herbs |
| Singapore Food Agency | Based on 12 | 200–400 µmol/m²/s for herbs |
| Iowa State herb trial | 16 | DLI 7 or 18 |

**In practice:** a DLI of 17 at 16 hours a day takes about 300 µmol/m²/s at leaf height (300 × 3,600 × 16 ÷ 1,000,000 = 17.3). That is inside the Singapore Food Agency's 200–400 range for herbs and stronger than many countertop units. If your light's maker gives PPFD at a stated distance, set the height to reach about 300; if it does not, keep the lamp 6–12 in from the leaves as Iowa State advises and run it 16 hours. A windowsill alone rarely reaches these levels in winter.

## Temperature: basil likes it warm, mint and sage cooler

The Iowa State herb trial held air and solution at **22 °C (72 °F)**, and Iowa State Extension says normal indoor temperatures suit herbs. Within that, herbs differ:

- **Basil** grows little below a base temperature of about 11 °C (52 °F), and in a 2023 growth model its best mean daily temperature for fresh mass was 32.6–35.5 °C (91–96 °F), rising as light increased (Walters et al. 2023, citing Walters and Currey).
- **Sage** produced more as temperature fell from 35.1 to 22.8 °C (95 to 73 °F), most of all under low light (Walters et al. 2023).
- **Spearmint** gained 60 g from extra light at 23.0 °C (73 °F) but only 32 g at 35.1 °C (95 °F) (Walters et al. 2023).

**What we recommend:** keep a mixed herb room at about 70–75 °F (21–24 °C). It sits close to the trial's 72 °F, is warm enough for basil, and avoids the heat that held back mint and sage. If you grow basil on its own, warmer is better; see the [hydroponic basil guide](/crops/hydroponic-basil/).

## Which system suits herbs

| System | Best for | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFT channels | Basil, parsley, cilantro, dill, mint | Greens and herbs did best in NFT and DWC (Virginia Tech SPES-466); herbs on 8 in centers (SPES-463); the Iowa State herb trial used NFT |
| Deep water culture | Basil, mint, parsley | Best with NFT in Virginia Tech's trials; University of Florida basil grew in 7.6 L (2 gal) aerated units |
| Ebb and flow | Basil, cilantro, mint | Listed among common crops (Oregon State EM 9458) |
| Countertop unit | Small batches of basil, parsley, chives | Herbs "in as few as 3–4 weeks" in pod units (UF/IFAS) |
| Kratky jar | A single plant, as an experiment | No pump; none of our sources tested herbs this way |

**Our pick for a home grower:** a small [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tub or an [NFT system](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) under an LED. Both did best for herbs in Virginia Tech's trials. A [Kratky jar](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) is the cheapest experiment, though no source we read tested herbs in one, and the [smart indoor systems guide](/systems/smart-indoor-hydroponic-systems/) covers countertop units.

## Spacing and plants per tray

Virginia Tech's NFT guide spaces leafy greens and herbs **8 in (20 cm) on center**, and the Iowa State herb trial used 20 cm centers, or 25 plants per square meter (about 2.3 per sq ft). For basil cut once and replanted, Iowa State research found **4 in (10 cm) spacing** gave the most per square foot under lights, but warned it is likely too dense for repeated harvests (Walters, 2015). For a cut-and-come-again herb garden, stay at about 8 in.

## Step by step: set up a mixed herb system

1. **Pick three to five easy herbs.** Basil, parsley, mint, chives and oregano share the solution well. Leave rosemary and sage for later.
2. **Start seedlings in plugs.** Sow two seeds per cell and thin to one, as the Iowa State trial did; transplant at about 2–4 weeks (Walters et al. 2023; Currey et al. 2019).
3. **Set up the system.** Fill the reservoir and set pH 5.8–6.0 and EC about 1.2–1.6 for young plants. A [pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) makes this reliable.
4. **Space at 8 in (20 cm).** Put mint at one end; it grows fast and can crowd neighbors.
5. **Light 16 hours a day.** Hang the LED 6–12 in (15–30 cm) above the leaves on a timer, aiming for about 300 µmol/m²/s.
6. **Raise EC as plants grow.** Move toward 1.6–2.0 once they are established, and check pH and EC two to three times a week (Virginia Tech).
7. **First harvest at 3–5 weeks after transplant.** Basil and mint are usually first, sage last (Walters et al. 2023).
8. **Cut the right way.** For annuals such as basil, cilantro and dill, leave about 4–6 in (10–15 cm) of stem; for perennials such as chives, mint, oregano and thyme, take the top one-third (Iowa State).
9. **Replace tired annuals.** After several harvests, Iowa State advises discarding old annual herbs and starting new plants from seed.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tall, thin, slow herbs | Too little light | Lower the lamp to 6–12 in, run 12–16 h, aim for DLI 17 | Iowa State; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Basil pale or slow while EC is in range | Possible magnesium shortfall: basil needs more than most crops, and calcium-heavy feeds suppress uptake | Use a complete hydroponic fertilizer that supplies magnesium | Currey 2024 |
| Herbs stall even with good light | pH drifted high | Bring pH back to 5.5–6.0; growth suffers much above 7.0–7.5 | Currey 2024 |
| Uneven growth across herbs | EC off for a heavy feeder such as parsley | Hold 1.2–2.0; parsley prefers the top of the range | Virginia Tech; Oklahoma State |
| Fine webbing, speckled leaves on rosemary or sage | Spider mites | Inspect leaf undersides; grow the easy herbs first | Iowa State |
| Brown, slimy roots | Root rot (Pythium) | Keep pH at 5.5–6.0 or slightly lower, aerate the solution (the Iowa State trial held 8.3 mg/L dissolved oxygen), clean between crops | Currey 2024; Currey et al. 2019 |
| Plants stop regrowing after cutting | Cut too low | Leave 4–6 in on annuals; take only the top one-third of perennials | Iowa State |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the easiest herb to grow hydroponically?

Basil is the safest start: it has a published EC and pH (1.0–1.6 and 5.5–6.0, Oklahoma State), grows fast and is the most studied herb in hydroponics. Mint and chives are also on Iowa State's easy list.

### Can you grow different herbs in the same hydroponic system?

Yes. Iowa State research found basil, parsley, dill and cilantro yielded the same across EC 0.5–4.0, so one solution at EC 1.2–2.0 and pH 5.8–6.0 suits a mixed garden. Keep mint at one end so it does not crowd the others.

### How much light do hydroponic herbs need?

12–16 hours a day from a lamp 6–12 in away (Iowa State), with a DLI of at least 17 mol/m²/day (Virginia Tech). More light raised cilantro, dill and parsley yields by 120–241% in Iowa State trials.

### Which herbs are hard to grow hydroponically?

Rosemary, sage, lemon balm, marjoram, chervil and bay need more light than usual or attract pests such as spider mites (Iowa State). None of our sources gives hydroponic EC or pH targets for rosemary.

### How soon can I harvest hydroponic herbs?

Spearmint and basil were ready about 3 weeks after transplant in a 2023 greenhouse trial, sage after 5 (Walters et al. 2023). Counted from seed it takes longer: the University of Florida study notes basil leaves can be picked from about 6 weeks after sowing. For seedlings cut young, see [hydroponic microgreens](/crops/hydroponic-microgreens/).
