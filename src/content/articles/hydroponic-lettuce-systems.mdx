The best hydroponic system for lettuce depends on how many heads you want. For **one to three plants**, use a **Kratky bucket**: no pump, about **1–1.5 gallons of solution per plant** for the whole crop, and harvest about 3–4 weeks after transplanting (UF/IFAS). For **a tray of heads**, use **deep water culture (DWC)**: a floating raft on solution **at least 12 inches deep**, **3–4 plants per square foot**, and an air pump holding dissolved oxygen at **7–10 ppm** (University of Kentucky; Virginia Tech). For **long rows or many plants**, use a **nutrient film technique (NFT) channel** with a slope of **1:100 to 2%** and about **1 liter (0.26 gallon) per minute** of flow per channel (Purdue; Oregon State). All three run lettuce at **pH 5.5–6.0** and **EC about 1.5 mS/cm**.

Virginia Tech's trials got the best leafy-green yields from NFT and DWC, so the real choice is which failure you can live with. A Kratky bucket has nothing to break. DWC roots stay wet if the air pump stops. In NFT, Oregon State warns plants can wilt quickly if the pump stops.

How to pick:

- **Pick Kratky** if you want two or three heads on a windowsill or under one light and no moving parts. UF/IFAS says the method needs no pumps or electricity and notes that fruiting crops do better in other methods, so this is a greens system.
- **Pick DWC** if you want a steady supply from one tub or tote. Missouri Extension rates floating-raft DWC among the simpler systems to build, and plants keep their roots in water if a pump stops. UF/IFAS calls the floating raft ideal for short-term crops such as lettuce.
- **Pick NFT** if you want many plants in a compact run, or a wall or tower. Oklahoma State notes that short-term crops like lettuce are usually grown in NFT or flood-and-drain, and that NFT's constant flow means no timer. The trade-off is that Oregon State calls pump and power backups crucial, because plants can wilt quickly when the film stops.

For a broader look at all six home system types, see [indoor hydroponic systems compared](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/). If this is your first grow of anything, the [beginner system guide](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/) explains why Kratky comes first.

## Kratky: lettuce with no pump

In Kratky's non-circulating method all the solution goes in before planting and none is added afterwards. As the lettuce drinks, the level falls, and the upper roots grow into the moist air gap and take in oxygen there, so no air stone is needed (Kratky, University of Hawaii). Kratky reports each lettuce plant used 3–6 liters (about 0.8–1.6 gallons) over the crop, starting at an EC of about 1.5.

UF/IFAS turns this into a home build: a food-grade 5-gallon bucket and lid with three 2-inch net cups, 1–1.5 gallons of water per lettuce plant from seed to harvest, about 1 inch of air between the solution and the lid at the start, EC about 1,250 µS/cm (1.25 mS/cm) and pH 5.5–6. Transplant after 1–3 weeks, then expect about 3–4 more weeks to harvest.

The one rule that matters: **do not top the bucket back up to the lid.** The air gap is where the roots breathe. Refilling it drowns those roots.

## Deep water culture: the raft system

In DWC, lettuce sits in holes in a floating raft and its roots hang in a deep, aerated tank. Kentucky gives a solution depth of at least 12 inches (30 cm), 3–4 plants per square foot, topping up every few days and replacing the solution fully or partly every 3–4 weeks. The Cornell CEA lettuce handbook schedules its pond (deep-flow raft) crop at transplant on Day 21 and harvest on Day 35.

Oxygen is the number to watch, and the sources disagree on it:

- **Virginia Tech (SPES-466):** 7–10 ppm, with 4 ppm the minimum.
- **University of Kentucky:** 6–8 ppm for lettuce in DWC.
- **UF/IFAS (HS1422):** 5 mg/L (mg/L and ppm are the same unit).

We suggest Virginia Tech's 7–10 ppm, which also covers Kentucky's range, and treating anything below 4 ppm as an emergency. Run the air pump all day, not on a timer.

Oregon State adds two practical points. The large water volume buffers temperature, but it is heavy, so the tank must sit on the ground. And the raft should fit the reservoir closely, because gaps let light in and algae grow. The full build, from tote to air stone, is in the [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

## NFT: the nutrient film system

A nutrient film technique (NFT) system runs a shallow stream of solution down a sloped, watertight and dark channel past bare roots, then back to the tank to recirculate (Purdue). Oregon State says the limited rooting space suits short-lived or small-rooted crops and lists lettuce first.

![NFT channel numbers for lettuce. Slope: 1:100 recommended, 1:30 to 1:40 also used (Purdue); Oregon State gives a 2 percent minimum. Flow per channel: 1 liter (0.26 gallon) per minute, upper limit 2 liters (0.53 gallon) per minute. Channel length: no more than 10 to 15 meters (33 to 49 feet), or 9 meters (30 feet) per Jones as cited by Sato and colleagues. Along an 18 meter channel, dissolved oxygen fell by up to 0.9 mg/L and the outlet ran 0.3 to 1.4 °C warmer than the inlet. The pump runs nonstop, so back up power and the pump.](/images/guides/nft-lettuce-channel-numbers.svg)

*Slope, flow and length for an NFT lettuce channel. From Purdue (Langenhoven, 2016), Oregon State EM 9457, Oklahoma State HLA-6442 and Sato et al. (2007).*

**Slope: the sources conflict.** Purdue recommends a slope of 1:100 (a 1% fall) and notes 1:30 and 1:40 are also used. Oregon State asks for at least 2% in horizontal channels. For a short home channel we suggest 2%, about ¼ inch of fall per foot. It meets Oregon State's minimum, sits inside the range Purdue says growers use, and makes sure the channel drains rather than pooling. Pooling matters: UF/IFAS warns that too much water in NFT can cause root rot.

**Flow.** Purdue gives 1 liter (0.26 gallon) per minute per channel, with an upper limit of 2 liters (0.53 gallon) per minute, and Sato and colleagues note that 1–2 liters per minute is widely recommended. One liter per minute is about 16 gallons per hour, per channel, so size the pump for the number of channels plus the height it has to lift.

**Length.** Purdue says a channel should not exceed 10–15 m (33–49 ft); Sato's team cites 9 m (30 ft) or less. Home channels are far shorter, but the reason still applies. In Sato's 18 m lettuce channel, dissolved oxygen fell by up to 0.9 mg/L from inlet to outlet, and the outlet ran 0.3–1.4 °C warmer than the inlet. Plants at the far end get warmer, less oxygenated solution, so keep runs short.

**Heat.** In the same trial the tank averaged 28.3 °C (83 °F) and peaked at 38.9 °C (102 °F), which the authors called too warm for lettuce roots, citing an optimum of 13–18 °C (55–64 °F). That cited optimum is cooler than UF/IFAS's 65–80 °F (18–27 °C) working range for small systems. Keep the tank in the shade and at room temperature; a thin film in a warm room heats quickly.

**Pump timing.** NFT runs continuously, so it needs no timer (Oklahoma State). Oklahoma State's vertical tower plan also runs lettuce continuously, 24 hours a day. Because the roots have no reserve, Oregon State calls power and pump backups crucial.

**Spacing.** None of our sources gives a plant spacing for home NFT channels. In Sato's research channel, holes were 15 cm (6 in) apart, but that was a trial setup, not a recommendation. On a vertical tower, Oklahoma State gives 6–8 inches for thin-leaf lettuce and 12 inches for head lettuce, a reasonable guide for loose-leaf versus heading types. On towers and walls, Touliatos and colleagues measured less light and lower shoot weight from top to base, so plan light for the bottom row (see the [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/)). The same NFT numbers, applied to a longer-lived crop, are in the [NFT strawberry guide](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/).

## Light, temperature and timing are the same in every system

| Daily light integral (mol/m²/day) | Lettuce fresh weight after 28 days | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5.6 | 0.90 lb per m² | Purdue HO-283-W |
| 10.8 | 2.1 lb per m² | Purdue HO-283-W |
| 19.8 | 6.7 lb per m² | Purdue HO-283-W |

Purdue's trial of 15 lettuce varieties shows why light comes first: raising the light about 3.5 times, from 5.6 to 19.8 mol/m²/day, gave about seven times the yield. The targets differ by source. Kentucky gives 12–17 mol/m²/day for lettuce, Purdue 15–20 for leafy greens, and Virginia Tech at least 17 for greens and herbs. We suggest 12–17 as the working range for home growers and the top of it if your light allows.

Keep days up to 75 °F (24 °C) and nights up to 65 °F (18 °C) (Kentucky, which also covers tipburn and bolting). Tipburn is a crop problem, not a system problem. In an Ohio State tipburn trial run in NFT at 23/19 °C (73/66 °F), DLI 17 and 16 hours of light, most of 20 cultivars showed tipburn 16–24 days after transplanting, while 'Rabello' and 'Rouxai' showed none by day 28.

For variety choice, seed starting and the full feeding plan, see the main [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) and [hydroponic lettuce seeds](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-seeds/).

## Step-by-step: set up a lettuce system

1. **Start seedlings.** Sow one seed per 1-inch rockwool plug (Cornell). Kentucky transplants 14–21 days after seeding, at 3–4 true leaves; feed seedlings at about EC 1.0.
2. **Mix the solution.** Mix to EC about 1.5 mS/cm (Kratky: about 1.25 per UF/IFAS) and pH 5.5–6.0; see [hydroponic nutrient solution](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/). Use the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) to scale the label dose to your tank.
3. **Fill to the system's level.**
   - Kratky: leave about 1 inch of air under the lid.
   - DWC: at least 12 inches deep, with the air stone running before plants go in.
   - NFT: check the channel slope (aim for 2%), then run the pump and measure flow by timing how long one channel takes to fill a 1-liter jug (aim for about 1 minute).
4. **Transplant.** Kratky: three plants per 5-gallon bucket. DWC: 3–4 plants per square foot of raft. NFT and towers: 6–8 inches for loose-leaf, 12 inches for heads.
5. **Light it.** Aim for 12–17 mol/m²/day and keep days up to 75 °F (24 °C).
6. **Maintain it.**
   - Kratky: don't refill; check the level and the plants.
   - DWC: top up every few days and replace the solution every 3–4 weeks; keep the air on.
   - NFT: check flow and look for leaks and root mats daily; keep the tank cool.
7. **Harvest.** Kentucky gives 21–35 days after transplanting; UF/IFAS gives about 3–4 weeks for Kratky lettuce.

Check pH and EC against our [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) as you go.

## Troubleshooting by system

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kratky plants wilt or roots brown | Bucket topped up over the air roots | Let the level fall again; refill only to below the upper roots | Kratky, University of Hawaii |
| DWC roots brown and slimy | Low dissolved oxygen | Check the air pump and stone; aim for 7–10 ppm | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Green film on the raft or in the tank | Light reaching the solution | Fit the raft closely and cover clear sides | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| NFT plants wilt suddenly | Pump stopped, power cut or a clog | Restore flow at once; add a backup pump or power | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| NFT roots rot in a channel | Water pooling; too little slope | Raise the fall toward 2%; check the outlet | UF/IFAS HS1422; Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Brown leaf edges (tipburn) | Warm, fast growth and a sensitive cultivar | Keep days up to 75 °F (24 °C); try 'Rabello' or 'Rouxai' | University of Kentucky; Ohio State e-GRO |
| Slow, leggy plants | Too little light | Raise DLI toward 12–17 mol/m²/day | University of Kentucky; Purdue HO-283-W |

## FAQ

### What is the best hydroponic system for lettuce?

For one to three plants, a Kratky bucket; for a tray of heads, DWC; for many plants in rows, NFT. Virginia Tech's trials found leafy greens yielded best in NFT and DWC, so both grow good lettuce. DWC is more forgiving because roots stay in water if the pump stops.

### What is a nutrient film hydroponic system?

A nutrient film technique (NFT) system pumps a thin film of solution down a sloped, dark channel past bare roots and back to the tank. Purdue recommends a 1:100 slope and about 1 liter (0.26 gallon) per minute per channel; Oregon State gives a 2% minimum slope.

### Is NFT or DWC better for lettuce?

Both suit lettuce. NFT fits long rows or walls; DWC's large volume buffers temperature and keeps roots wet if power fails. For most home growers DWC is safer unless you can back up the NFT pump.

### How far apart should lettuce be in hydroponics?

In a DWC raft, 3–4 plants per square foot (University of Kentucky). On a tower, 6–8 inches for thin-leaf lettuce and 12 inches for head lettuce (Oklahoma State). Our sources give no spacing for home NFT channels.

### How long does hydroponic lettuce take?

Transplant 14–21 days after seeding and harvest 21–35 days after transplanting (University of Kentucky), so about five to eight weeks from seed in total. In a Kratky bucket UF/IFAS gives about 3–4 weeks from transplant.
