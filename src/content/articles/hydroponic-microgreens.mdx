Hydroponic microgreens are seedlings cut **7–21 days after sowing**, at about **2–3 in (5–7.5 cm) tall**, once the first true leaves appear (UF/IFAS, University of Maryland, Penn State). Sow about **10–15 g of small seed per 10 × 20 in (25 × 50 cm) tray** (Penn State; Maryland), or up to 28 g for most crops in Utah State's chart, and far more for large seed such as pea (150 g). Give them **16–18 hours of light a day** and a daily light integral (DLI) of **9–16 mol/m²/day** (Maryland; Utah State; Virginia Tech), keep the room at **60–75 °F (16–24 °C)**, and, if you feed at all, use a weak solution at **EC about 1.0 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0** (Virginia Tech).

The sources disagree most on seed rate and on whether to feed at all. This guide shows both sides and says what we would do.

## Which hydroponic setup suits microgreens

Microgreens are cut above the roots and need only a shallow, evenly wet root zone for one to three weeks. Sprouts, by contrast, are eaten whole at 3–5 days (Virginia Tech), and baby greens are cut at 4–6 in after 21–40 days (Maryland). Virginia Tech lists trays, ebb and flow, NFT, deep water culture and aeroponics in commercial use.

| Setup | How it works | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tray on a mat, bottom-watered | A 10 × 20 in tray with drain holes sits in a solid tray; you pour water or solution underneath | Bottom-water from once every two days to several times a day (Virginia Tech); sub-irrigation keeps leaves dry (Penn State) |
| Ebb and flow bench | Trays flood from below, then drain | Recommended by Penn State to keep leaves dry; see our [ebb and flow guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) |
| NFT trough with mats | Burlap or food-grade mats lie in a channel with a thin, moving film | Used commercially (UF/IFAS); example timer 10 minutes, three times a day (Virginia Tech); see [NFT setup](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) |
| Soilless mix in a tray | Peat, coir or vermiculite ½–2 in deep | Common and forgiving; not strictly hydroponic (UF/IFAS HS1164; Maryland) |

**Our pick for a home grower:** a 10 × 20 in tray with drain holes, nested in a solid tray, with a hemp or coir mat. Water from below. It needs no pump, and hemp holds more water than burlap (Virginia Tech). Move to an ebb and flow or NFT rack only for many trays. More on mats and media: [hydroponic growing media](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/).

## Seed rate: the biggest disagreement

UF/IFAS calls seeding density "difficult to recommend". Here is what each source prints for one standard tray.

![Bar chart of seed per 10 by 20 inch tray from Utah State Extension: arugula 10 grams, basil, broccoli, kale and cilantro 28 grams, radish 42 grams, sunflower 48 grams, pea 150 grams. Penn State and Maryland use 10 to 15 grams of small seed per tray.](/images/guides/hydroponic-microgreens-seed-rates.svg)

*Seed per 10 × 20 in tray (Utah State Table 1; Penn State; University of Maryland). Large seeds weigh more, so a heavier rate does not mean a thicker stand.*

- **Penn State:** 10–15 g per standard tray (12 × 20 in, 10 rows). One tablespoon of brassica seed is about 11–12 g.
- **Maryland:** about 1 tablespoon, or 10–15 g, per 10 × 20 in flat, which is 1,500–4,500 seeds depending on seed size.
- **Utah State:** 10 g of arugula, 28 g for most small-seeded crops, 42 g of radish, 48 g of sunflower and 150 g of pea per 1020 tray.
- **Alberta:** 50–70 g for small seed and 100–120 g for sunflower and pea, with no tray size given.

**Why they differ:** grams hide seed size. A gram of mustard seed holds about ten times as many seeds as a gram of beet (Maryland's seed-count table gives 530 against 53 per gram). Penn State's Di Gioia counts seeds per area instead: about **2 per square inch for large seed** (pea, sunflower) up to **12 for small seed** (broccoli).

**What we recommend:** start small-seeded brassicas at 10–15 g per tray, the figure two universities share, and raise it only if the stand looks thin. Too thick, and stems stretch and disease risk rises (UF/IFAS).

Alberta advises against soaking gel-coated seed (arugula, basil, cress); Maryland soaks pea, sunflower, beet, chard and cilantro 6–12 hours. Penn State's trays averaged **0.30 lb (136 g)**, from 46.8 g (cilantro) to 201 g (Red Rain mustard).

**Skip nightshades.** Penn State warns that tomato, pepper, eggplant and potato seedlings contain alkaloids and are not suitable as microgreens.

## Light: hours, DLI and intensity

Microgreens are a low-light crop by greenhouse standards. Penn State puts their need **below 20 mol/m²/day**, and Virginia Tech gives a general range of **9–16 mol/m²/day**. Its own farm runs **18 hours at about 250 µmol/m²/s**, a DLI of 16.2, and its study table lists arugula at DLI 9 and mizuna and mustard at 12 or more.

| Source | Hours a day | Intensity or DLI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virginia Tech SPES-756 | 18 | About 250 µmol/m²/s; DLI 9–16 |
| University of Maryland | About 16 | Fixtures 6–12 in (15–30 cm) above leaves |
| Utah State; Alberta | 18 on, 6 off; 12–18 | Not given |
| Singapore Food Agency | Based on 12 | At least 100–150 µmol/m²/s for seedlings and microgreens |

**In practice:** put an LED panel on a timer for 16–18 hours, 6–12 in above the trays. If your light's maker gives PPFD at a stated distance, aim for about 150–250 µmol/m²/s at canopy height; at 16 hours, 200 µmol/m²/s works out to a DLI of about 11.5, inside Virginia Tech's range. A sunny window "may suffice in some cases" (Maryland).

## Temperature, humidity and airflow

Utah State germinates at **74 °F (23 °C)**, then runs **72 °F (22 °C) with lights on and 68 °F (20 °C) with lights off**; Maryland finds brassicas sprout in about 36 hours at 72 °F. Virginia Tech and Penn State keep growing air at **60–70 °F (16–21 °C)**, with humidity at **50–70%** (Virginia Tech) or 40–60% (Alberta). Both Utah State and Maryland run a gentle fan.

Penn State found damping-off "the only disease or pest issue" in its classes, favored by warm, humid conditions; Alberta also links heat and humidity to disease. **We recommend** about 68–72 °F (20–22 °C) and a small fan once the cover comes off.

## Feeding: water only, or a weak solution?

- **No feed:** Maryland says no fertilizer is needed, and Penn State's Di Gioia notes they can be grown without any.
- **Light feed:** Penn State says little or none is needed, but if you feed, **50–100 ppm nitrogen** is enough. Its class used 100 ppm N, and trays on clear water sometimes showed iron deficiency. UF/IFAS floats some slower crops (carrot, dill, celery, chard) for 30 seconds in about **80 ppm N**.
- **Hydroponic feed:** Virginia Tech recommends fertilizing for yield and speed. On mats it uses a standard leafy-green solution at **EC at least 1.0 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0**, running EC 1.0 and pH 5.9 from germination to harvest, and checks EC and pH from daily to at least twice a week. A review in *Foods* (2022) also found that adding mineral nutrients increases yields.

**What we recommend:** on a bare mat, where there is no potting mix to supply anything, feed a half-strength leafy-green solution at about **EC 1.0 and pH 5.8** once seedlings are up. On a peat or compost mix, plain water is fine for fast brassicas. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) shows how to mix to a target EC, the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a label rate to your tray, and a [pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) or the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) helps you hit 5.5–6.0.

## Step by step: one tray of hydroponic microgreens

![Six steps for a tray of microgreens: soak large seed 6 to 12 hours, sow 10 to 15 grams on a wet mat, cover 2 to 5 days, light 16 to 18 hours a day, bottom-water with EC 1.0 at pH 5.5 to 6.0, cut at 2 to 3 inches after 7 to 21 days.](/images/guides/hydroponic-microgreens-tray-steps.svg)

*One tray from seed to harvest (Utah State; Maryland; Virginia Tech SPES-756; Penn State).*

1. **Buy seed sold for sprouts or microgreens.** Penn State and Di Gioia both warn against seed treated with pesticides or coatings, since the seed coat can be eaten.
2. **Soak large seed only.** Pea 6 hours and sunflower 12 hours (Utah State). Do not soak arugula, basil or cress (Alberta).
3. **Wet the mat.** Lay a hemp or coir mat in a 10 × 20 in tray with drain holes, inside a solid tray. If you use coir loose, Utah State uses about 2 quarts (about 2 L) per tray, moist like a wrung-out sponge.
4. **Sow evenly.** About 10–15 g of small seed per tray, or the crop rate in the table above. Mist the seed (Maryland).
5. **Cover for 2–5 days.** Put an upside-down tray on top, weighted with 2–5 lb (0.9–2.3 kg) as Utah State does. Uncover when most seedlings are up (Virginia Tech).
6. **Turn on the lights.** 16–18 hours a day, 6–12 in above the seedlings. Add a gentle fan.
7. **Water from below.** Pour water or EC 1.0 solution into the solid tray, let the mat soak it up, then pour off the rest. Repeat whenever the mat feels light, from every two days to several times a day (Virginia Tech).
8. **Harvest at first true leaves.** Cut just above the mat with clean scissors at about 2–3 in, 7–21 days after sowing.
9. **Chill, do not wash until use.** Penn State notes washing shortens post-harvest life. Refrigerate dry at 41 °F (5 °C) in the dark.

## Food safety: why seed and water matter

Microgreens are eaten raw, and a wet tray can spread germs. A 2022 review in *Foods* summarizes studies showing that *E. coli* O157:H7 survived and grew on radish microgreens in both soil-substitute and hydroponic systems, that contaminated seed led to contamination of the whole plant, and that hydroponic pads gave the highest share of *Salmonella*-positive samples in one trial. A norovirus surrogate stayed infectious in recirculated hydroponic water for up to 12 days. The review concludes that washing the harvest is "not likely to be an effective control strategy".

**So prevention starts before sowing:** use clean seed sold for microgreens, a new mat or fresh media each cycle (Penn State), and fresh solution for each tray rather than water recycled between trays. Virginia Tech's farm cleans towers with a hydrogen peroxide sanitizer at the label rate; Alberta mentions diluted hydrogen peroxide or hot water for seed but gives no figures, so we do not print one.

Maryland notes that baby greens can regrow for one or two more cuts, but no source we read measured regrowth of microgreens, so plan a fresh tray each week or so for a steady supply.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seedlings collapse at the base in patches | Damping-off (Pythium, Phytophthora, possibly Rhizoctonia), favored by warm, humid conditions | Lower the seed rate, add a fan, keep leaves dry by bottom watering, start each tray on a new mat | Penn State; Virginia Tech SPES-756 |
| Mold on the mat or seed | Moisture trapped under a cover with still air | Vent humidity domes, run a gentle fan, uncover once most seeds are up | Maryland; Utah State; Virginia Tech SPES-756 |
| Long, pale, floppy stems | Too little light, too long under cover, or seed too dense | Uncover once most seeds are up; lights 6–12 in away for 16–18 h; seed less | Virginia Tech; Maryland; UF/IFAS HS1164 |
| Patchy germination | Uneven moisture or old seed | Pre-wet the mat evenly; mist for the first days; sow seed within three years of purchase | Maryland |
| Yellowing leaves on plain water | Low iron or nitrogen on bare mats | Feed a weak solution, about 50–100 ppm N or EC 1.0 | Penn State; Virginia Tech |
| Greens wilt or go slimy within days | Stored warm or washed before storage | Chill at 41 °F (5 °C) dry; wash just before eating | Penn State |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best hydroponic system for microgreens?

For a home grower, a 10 × 20 in tray with a hemp or coir mat, watered from below. Penn State recommends sub-irrigation to keep leaves dry, and Virginia Tech lists ebb and flow and NFT for larger racks. A [Kratky jar](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) suits longer crops such as lettuce, not dense trays of seedlings.

### Do microgreens need nutrients in hydroponics?

Not strictly: Maryland and Penn State say little or no fertilizer is needed. On a bare mat, though, Virginia Tech recommends a leafy-green solution at EC about 1.0 and pH 5.5–6.0, and Penn State saw iron deficiency on plain water.

### How long do microgreens take to grow?

7–21 days from sowing for most crops. Radish and arugula can be ready in 6–10 days; cilantro takes 21–28 days (Virginia Tech; Utah State).

### Can I grow microgreens and lettuce in the same system?

The solution can be shared: Virginia Tech's microgreen target of pH 5.5–6.0 and EC about 1.0 sits close to leafy-green targets. Microgreen trays need a shallow mat rather than net pots, though. See the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) and [hydroponic basil guide](/crops/hydroponic-basil/) for full-size crops.
