Hydroponic green onions (scallions, bunching onions) are one of the easiest crops to grow in water, but almost nobody has published hydroponic targets for them. The research that exists points to a **gentle feed at pH about 6.5**. In a Journal of Plant Nutrition study, green onions made the most biomass at **solution pH 6.5** rather than 5.8, and **half-strength Hoagland's solution** was the best of the mixes tested (Kane et al. 2006). A 2021 trial found **50–100% of a standard solution** grew better plants than 125% (Santos et al. 2021).

Germinate seed at **61–75 °F (16–24 °C)** (British Columbia production guide). Grow at a cool **60–65 °F (16–18 °C) by day** (SARE). Start cutting once plants are about **6 in (15 cm) tall**, and the leaves will grow back several times (Oregon State). From seed, expect a harvest **60–80 days after planting** (UC Master Gardeners). Regrowing store-bought scallions in a glass is quicker: new growth shows in about **three days** and reaches **5–7 in in about a week** (UC Master Gardeners, Riverside County).

## Green onions, scallions and bunching onions

The names overlap. Seed sold as "bunching onion" or "Welsh onion" is usually *Allium fistulosum*, which forms leaves and a straight white shank but no real bulb. "Green onion" and "scallion" also cover ordinary bulb onions (*Allium cepa*) pulled young. Both kinds appear in the research below: the Kane et al. trial grew two *A. cepa* varieties and one *A. fistulosum*, and the Korean and later trials used *A. fistulosum* varieties such as 'Tokio Kuro'.

For a hydroponic system, a bunching type is the better choice because you can cut it repeatedly without waiting for a bulb.

**How many to grow:** SARE's harvest manual bunches **6–8 scallions, about 4 oz (113 g)**, per bunch. None of our sources gives a home yield per plug, so count on cutting clumps in rotation and sowing a new tray every few weeks once you know how fast you use them.

## What the research says about feeding

Green onions are grown commercially in soil, so the hydroponic evidence is thin: three small trials, none of them giving an EC.

**pH 6.5 beat pH 5.8.** Kane and colleagues grew two *Allium cepa* onion varieties ('Deep Purple' and 'Purplette') and the bunching onion *Allium fistulosum* 'Kinka' as green onions in three nutrient solutions at pH 5.8 and 6.5. Total biomass was greatest at pH 6.5, and they named half-strength Hoagland's solution at pH 6.5 "the preferred nutrient solution evaluated in this research." Plants were harvested 30 days after they went into the hydroponic units.

**Too strong a feed hurts.** Santos and colleagues grew the bunching onion 'Tokio Kuro' at 50, 75, 100 and 125% of a standard solution. Concentrations "between 50 and 100%" gave better growth and an earlier harvest, while 125% reduced leaf number and fresh leaf weight (Santos et al. 2021).

**The recipe mattered in Korea.** In deep water culture of young Welsh onion, Korean researchers compared four published onion and leafy-vegetable solutions and chose Yamazaki's solution for Welsh onion seedlings as the best (Won et al. 2005). We read only the abstract, which gives no concentrations.

![Bar chart comparing green onion growth in hydroponic trials: pH 6.5 gave more biomass than pH 5.8; nutrient strength of 50 to 100 percent grew better than 125 percent; half-strength Hoagland's was the preferred solution.](/images/guides/hydroponic-green-onions-feeding.svg)

*What three trials found: Kane et al. 2006 (pH and solution), Santos et al. 2021 (solution strength) and Won et al. 2005 (Welsh onion water culture). No trial published an EC target.*

**What we recommend, and what it rests on:** mix a complete hydroponic fertilizer at **half to full label strength for leafy greens**, then hold **pH 6.0–6.5**. The pH comes straight from the Kane et al. result. The strength comes from the half-strength and 50–100% findings. Because no source gives a green onion EC, we suggest measuring your mix with a meter and keeping it near the low end of the lettuce range Oklahoma State lists (EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm). That is a working starting point based on a similar leafy crop, not a published onion target. If you grow green onions in a shared tank with lettuce, they will cope. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains how to mix to an EC, the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a label rate to your reservoir, and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists the nearby crops.

## Temperature: cool and steady

Temperature figures come from soil and greenhouse growing guides, since the hydroponic trials did not report them.

- **Germination:** the British Columbia production guide says seed germinates at 8–30 °C (46–86 °F), with the best range **16–24 °C (61–75 °F)**. SARE's greenhouse guidelines give 60–85 °F (16–29 °C).
- **Growing:** SARE grows scallion transplants at **60–65 °F (16–18 °C) by day and 55–60 °F (13–16 °C) at night**.

**What we recommend:** germinate at about 65–75 °F (18–24 °C), then grow in the coolest bright spot you have, ideally 60–70 °F (16–21 °C). That is a little warmer than SARE's greenhouse range because most homes are; no source we read tested green onions at room temperature. Avoid hot spots right under a lamp.

## Light

Green onions need strong light. The University of Minnesota says "all onions require full sun," and on a windowsill they stretch thin and pale.

No source gives a DLI or PPFD target for hydroponic green onions. The nearest guidance is for chives, a close relative that Iowa State lists among the easiest herbs to grow indoors: run lights **12–16 hours a day with the lamp 6–12 in (15–30 cm) from the leaves**. In a study of Welsh onion grown under different colors of LED at **300 µmol/m²/s**, white light gave the heaviest plants: about **19.5 g of leaf per plant** at day 30, against about 11.5 g under yellow light (Gao et al. 2020). For a home grower, that means a full-spectrum white LED is the right choice and colored "bloom" lights are not.

## Two ways to grow: from seed or from scraps

There are two routes, and they suit different people.

| Route | Time to first cut | Best for | What to expect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seed in plugs | About 60–80 days from planting | A steady supply in a DWC tub or NFT | Bunches of thin onions; leaves regrow after cutting (UC Master Gardeners; Oregon State) |
| Regrow store-bought scallions | About 1 week for 5–7 in of new leaves | A quick kitchen-counter crop | About 2 regrowths before the bulb is spent (UC Master Gardeners, Riverside) |

### Regrowing scallions in water

The Riverside County Master Gardener fact sheet gives a simple method: cut the scallion and keep 2–3 in (5–8 cm) of the white and light green end. Stand the ends in a glass with **about 2 in (5 cm) of water** in a sunny spot. **Change the water every day.** You should see growth after three days. When the greens are **5–7 in (13–18 cm)** above the white part, about a week later, cut them. The sheet says one scallion can be regrown **about 2 times**.

Plain water has no nutrients, so this is regrowth from the bulb's own reserves, not hydroponics. For more harvests, move the rooted ends into a net pot over a dilute nutrient solution, as in a [Kratky jar](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/). None of our sources tested how many extra cuts that gives.

### Growing from seed

Seed gives far more onions for the money and a crop you can keep cutting. SARE sows **7–9 seeds per cell** in 128-cell trays and grows each clump as one unit. The University of Minnesota thins garden scallions to one plant per inch. In a hydroponic system, keep the clumps whole: one plug of 7–9 seedlings per net pot.

## Which system to use

| System | Fit | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glass of water | Quick regrowth only | Change water daily; about 2 regrowths (UC Master Gardeners) |
| Kratky jar | Good for a few clumps | No pump; top up with solution, not plain water |
| Deep water culture | Best for a steady supply | The Korean Welsh onion trial was a deep water culture; a lid keeps light off the water |
| NFT channel | Works | Clumps in net cups; check plugs do not block the flow |

**Our pick:** a small [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tub. It matches the one hydroponic system named in the research (the Korean water-culture trial) and needs no pump timing. Virginia Tech notes that deep water culture ponds are typically 4–12 in deep, so a shallow tote is enough. Roots should hang into aerated solution, as in our [air pump guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/).

## Step by step: green onions in a DWC tub

1. **Start plugs.** Sow 7–9 seeds per rockwool or coir plug, about ¼ in (6 mm) deep (UC Master Gardeners give ¼ in for soil), and keep them at 61–75 °F (16–24 °C). Our [growing media guide](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/) compares plug types.
2. **Grow the seedlings.** UC Master Gardeners start transplants about 5 weeks ahead in dense pots. Give the plugs bright light from the day they sprout.
3. **Set up the tub.** Use an opaque tote at least 6 in (15 cm) deep (Oregon State's container minimum), an air pump and stone, and a lid with [net pots](/equipment/hydroponic-net-pots/) about 2–3 in (5–8 cm) apart.
4. **Mix the solution.** Use half- to full-strength leafy-greens fertilizer and set pH to 6.0–6.5. A [pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) makes this reliable.
5. **Transplant.** Set each plug in a net pot with the base just touching the solution.
6. **Light 12–16 hours.** Hang a white LED 6–12 in (15–30 cm) above the leaves.
7. **Check weekly.** Keep pH at 6.0–6.5, top up the water, and replace the solution about every two weeks, as Oklahoma State advises for hydroponic solutions.
8. **First cut at 6 in (15 cm).** Cut the leaves and leave 2–3 in (5–8 cm) of the white and light green base, as the UC Master Gardeners advise for regrowth. Oregon State says leaves grow back several times.
9. **Or harvest whole.** Pull clumps when stems reach about pencil thickness to ½ in (13 mm) with tops at least 12 in (30 cm) tall, SARE's harvest standard, and replant.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thin, floppy, pale leaves | Too little light | White LED 6–12 in away, 12–16 h a day | Iowa State; Gao et al. 2020 |
| Slow growth, fewer leaves | Feed too strong | Dilute toward half strength; 125% cut leaf number and weight | Santos et al. 2021 |
| Slow growth at good feed strength | pH low | Raise toward 6.5 | Kane et al. 2006 |
| Few seeds come up | Too cold or too hot | Germinate at 61–75 °F (16–24 °C) | BC production guide |
| Scraps in water go slimy or smell | Water not changed | Change it daily, or move to a nutrient solution | UC Master Gardeners, Riverside |
| Regrowth gets weaker each time | Bulb reserves used up | Start new scraps, or grow from seed | UC Master Gardeners, Riverside |
| Green film in the tub | Light on the water | Cover all openings with an opaque lid | Oregon State EM 9455 |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you grow green onions hydroponically?

Yes. Researchers have grown green onions and Welsh onions in hydroponic solutions in at least three published trials. The best results came from a mild feed: half-strength Hoagland's at pH 6.5 (Kane et al. 2006) or 50–100% of a standard solution (Santos et al. 2021).

### What pH do hydroponic green onions need?

About 6.5. In the Kane et al. trial, plants at pH 6.5 made more biomass than plants at 5.8. We suggest holding 6.0–6.5, which also suits a mixed tank.

### What EC or ppm is best for green onions?

No university or research source we found gives one. The trials worked in strengths rather than EC, and full strength or less beat 125%. As a starting point, we suggest the low end of Oklahoma State's lettuce range, EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm. That comes from a similar leafy crop, not from onion research.

### How long do hydroponic green onions take?

From seed, about 60–80 days after planting (UC Master Gardeners); the Kane et al. plants were harvested 30 days after transplant. Regrowing scallion ends in water gives 5–7 in of new leaves in about a week.

### How many times can green onions regrow?

In plain water, about two times, according to the UC Master Gardeners. Seed-grown plants in a nutrient solution keep regrowing for longer: Oregon State says leaves grow back several times when you cut them.

### Are green onions easier than lettuce?

They are easier to keep alive and slower to harvest. If you want a fast first crop, start with [hydroponic lettuce](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/), and see [best herbs for hydroponics](/crops/best-herbs-for-hydroponics/) for chives and other easy companions.
