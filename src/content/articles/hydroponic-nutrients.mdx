In hydroponics there is no soil to hold a reserve of food, so everything a plant needs to grow has to be dissolved in the water you give it. Get the nutrient solution right and plants grow faster and cleaner than in soil. Get it wrong and you see yellow leaves, burned edges or stalled growth within days.

The good news is that you only need to control four things: **use a complete hydroponic nutrient, mix it in the right order, keep EC (strength) in the right range for your crop, and keep pH where roots can take nutrients up.** This guide explains each one, with the numbers from university extension sources.

## What "complete" hydroponic nutrients contain

Penn State Extension lists 17 elements plants need. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come from air and water. The other 14 have to be in your solution:

| Group | Elements | What they do (Penn State) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary macronutrients | Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K) | Proteins and growth (N); energy transfer and roots (P); water balance and enzymes (K) |
| Secondary macronutrients | Calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sulfur (S) | Cell walls and signaling (Ca); the center of chlorophyll (Mg); proteins (S) |
| Micronutrients | Iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, chlorine, molybdenum, nickel | Photosynthesis, enzymes and cell division, needed in tiny amounts |

**Recommendation:** buy a nutrient labeled for hydroponics that lists calcium, magnesium and the micronutrients, not just N-P-K. Ordinary garden fertilizers are designed to work with soil, which supplies many of these elements; in water nothing fills the gap. For an overview of product types, see [the best hydroponic nutrients for home growers](/best/hydroponic-nutrients-for-home-growers/) and [powder vs liquid hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/powder-vs-liquid-hydroponic-nutrients/).

## Why two-part (A/B) nutrients exist

Many hydroponic nutrients come as two or three bottles or bags. The University of Missouri explains why: calcium fertilizers react with phosphate and sulfate fertilizers and form insoluble solids that settle out, so roots can no longer use them. Keeping them in separate concentrates stops that reaction.

That leads to the one mixing rule you must never break: **never pour concentrate A straight into concentrate B.** Always dilute each in the full volume of water first.

## How to mix a nutrient solution

1. **Start with clean water** in the reservoir, close to your target temperature.
2. **Add part A**, measured from the label for your reservoir volume, and stir until fully dissolved. Missouri advises dissolving each formulation completely before combining.
3. **Add part B** (and C, if your program has one) the same way.
4. **Check EC first.** Oklahoma State says to get EC in range before adjusting pH: add concentrate if it is low, or dilute with water if it is high.
5. **Adjust pH last**, in small steps, waiting a few minutes between additions.
6. **Record** the EC, pH and date.

Use the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) to scale the label dose to your reservoir, and the step-by-step [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) guide for more detail.

## EC: how strong the solution is

Electrical conductivity (EC) measures the total amount of dissolved salts. Higher EC means a stronger solution. Oklahoma State recommends reading it in mS/cm. The University of Missouri adds an important limit: **EC tells you the total, not whether each nutrient is in balance**, which is why you start from a complete, correctly mixed product rather than chasing a number.

Meters that display "ppm" convert EC with a built-in factor, and different meters use different factors. Record EC in mS/cm so your numbers always mean the same thing.

### EC and pH ranges by crop

Oklahoma State Extension publishes a general chart. A selection:

| Crop | EC (mS/cm) | pH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basil | 1.0–1.6 | 5.5–6.0 |
| Lettuce | 1.2–1.8 | 6.0–7.0 |
| Peppers | 0.8–1.8 | 5.5–6.0 |
| Cucumber | 1.7–2.0 | 5.0–5.5 |
| Spinach | 1.8–2.3 | 6.0–7.0 |
| Parsley | 1.8–2.2 | 6.0–6.5 |
| Tomato | 2.0–4.0 | 6.0–6.5 |
| Strawberry | 1.8–2.2 | 6.0 |

**Use crop-specific research when it exists.** General charts and crop studies don't always agree. For strawberries, the University of Arizona's controlled-environment work recommends a much weaker solution, an EC of 1.0 or less, and pH 5.5–6.0. ## pH: whether roots can take nutrients up

pH controls nutrient availability. Oklahoma State notes that nutrients are most available in the pH 5–6 range, and the University of Missouri gives 5.5–6.5 as the rule of thumb for most hydroponic crops.

- **To lower pH:** Missouri lists citric or acetic acid as the safest to handle, then phosphoric or sulfuric acid; nitric acid is the most hazardous. Commercial "pH down" products contain these acids.
- **To raise pH:** potassium bicarbonate or potassium hydroxide ("pH up"). Missouri warns against sodium-based bases, which can be toxic to plants.
- **Go slowly:** add a few drops, stir, wait several minutes and re-test.

pH drifts as plants feed, so expect to adjust it regularly, especially in small reservoirs.

## Start with good water

Your tap water already contains dissolved minerals. The University of Missouri gives these targets for source water:

| Measure | Target |
| --- | --- |
| EC | 0.2–0.8 dS/m, ideally below 1 |
| pH | 5.5–7.0 |
| Alkalinity | 40–160 ppm as calcium carbonate |
| Sodium | Below 50 ppm |
| Chlorine | Below 70 ppm |
| Calcium | Below 150 ppm |
| Boron | Below 0.5 ppm |

Alkalinity matters more than many beginners expect. Above about 160 ppm you need more acid to move pH; below about 40 ppm, pH swings easily. Oklahoma State adds that alkalinity above 75 ppm pushes pH up over time, so check pH more often. If your water is hard or salty, filtered or reverse-osmosis water gives you a cleaner start. Ask your water supplier for a report, or send your water to a lab for testing.

## Look after your meters

Meters that read wrong are worse than no meter at all.

- Calibrate the pH meter weekly with pH 4 and pH 7 buffers (University of Missouri); calibrate EC meters with a standard such as 1.41 mS/cm (Oklahoma State).
- Rinse probes with distilled or deionised water between readings.
- Never store a pH probe in plain water; use storage solution or pH 4 buffer.
- Expect a pH probe to last 1–2 years (Missouri).
- Avoid pH test strips in nutrient solutions; fertilizer dyes can distort the color.

## Reading your plants: deficiency and excess

| What you see | Likely cause (Penn State) | First check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pale, light green plants, slow growth | Nitrogen shortage | EC too low, or solution old |
| Yellowing between veins on **new** leaves | Iron deficiency, most often caused by high pH | Bring pH back into range |
| Yellowing between veins on **older** leaves | Magnesium deficiency | Use a complete nutrient with Mg |
| Brown edges on new leaves, tipburn in lettuce, blossom-end rot in tomatoes | Calcium not reaching fast-growing tissue | Check EC and airflow; avoid heat and very high light |
| Wilting even though roots are wet, burned leaf edges, dark leaves | Too much salt (solution too strong) | Dilute or replace the solution |
| Yellow, dead spots on leaf margins | Boron excess (it has the narrowest safe range) | Check your water's boron |

Penn State also warns about **antagonisms**: too much of one nutrient can block another, for example excess potassium reducing calcium or magnesium uptake. That's another reason not to add single nutrients by guesswork.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use regular plant food in hydroponics?

Use a nutrient made for hydroponics that includes calcium, magnesium and micronutrients. Many soil fertilizers rely on the soil to supply part of what plants need.

### How often should I change hydroponic nutrient solution?

Oklahoma State suggests replacing it completely about every two weeks. Larger systems are often topped up and replaced every few weeks; the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) gives a 3–4 week example.

### Should I adjust pH or EC first?

EC first, then pH, because adding nutrients or water changes pH.

### What is the best pH for hydroponics?

5.5–6.5 for most crops, with most solutions run near 5.5–6.0. Check crop-specific guides where they exist.
