Feed hydroponic vegetables a **complete hydroponic nutrient** (one that supplies calcium, magnesium and micronutrients, not just N-P-K), set to the **EC for the crop** and held near **pH 6.0**. Oklahoma State's crop table puts **lettuce at EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm, basil 1.0–1.6, spinach 1.8–2.3, strawberry 1.8–2.2, broccoli 2.8–3.5 and tomato 2.0–4.0**. Leafy greens want a weaker solution than fruiting crops. Most crops in that table include pH 6.0, so pH is usually easy to share; the exceptions are cucumber (pH 5.0–5.5) and celery, okra, leek, cabbage and pak choi (6.5 or higher). EC is harder: group crops with overlapping EC ranges in one tank and give the rest their own.

The rest of this guide covers the full crop table, which vegetables can share a reservoir, how feeding changes from seedling to fruit, your water, and keeping a solution on track. Every figure comes from a university or extension source listed at the end.

## Leafy greens and fruiting crops eat differently

![Bar chart of EC in mS/cm from Oklahoma State. Low group: pepper 0.8–1.8, basil 1.0–1.6, lettuce 1.2–1.8. Middle group: spinach 1.8–2.3, strawberry 1.8–2.2. High group: tomato 2.0–4.0, broccoli 2.8–3.5. Crops in the same group can share a tank.](/images/guides/hydroponic-vegetables-ec-by-crop.svg)

*EC by crop from Oklahoma State HLA-6722 Table 2, sorted into three groups. The University of Tennessee runs fruiting crops, peppers included, at 2.0–3.0, higher than this table gives for pepper.*

The pattern is simple: **leafy crops and herbs run weaker, fruiting crops run stronger.** Lettuce and basil sit around EC 1.0–1.8, while Oklahoma State gives tomatoes 2.0–4.0 and the University of Tennessee holds tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers at 2.0–3.0 for most of the season.

The reasons are practical. Lettuce is in the system for a few weeks; the University of Kentucky harvests it 21–35 days after transplant. Fruiting crops stay for months, grow large root systems and change as they flower and set fruit, and Tennessee notes that larger plants throw a recirculating solution out of balance more quickly.

Systems follow the same split. In Virginia Tech's side-by-side trial, greens and herbs yielded best in NFT and deep water culture, while tomatoes and peppers did best in bags of media and worst in NFT. The [best hydroponic system for home growers](/best/hydroponic-system-for-home-growers/) matches each system to each crop.

**One conflict to know about: pepper.** Oklahoma State's table gives pepper EC 0.8–1.8, about the same as lettuce. Tennessee groups peppers with tomatoes and cucumbers at 2.0–3.0. HydroRigor's reading: start young pepper plants in Oklahoma State's range and move toward Tennessee's 2.0 once they flower.

## What "complete" means on a label

Penn State lists 17 elements plants need. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come from air and water; the other 14 have to come from the solution. A hydroponic nutrient has to supply them all, because there is no soil to fill the gaps.

- **Look past N-P-K.** The three numbers on the front cover nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium only. Check the label lists calcium, magnesium, sulfur and micronutrients such as iron.
- **Two- and three-part products** exist because calcium reacts with phosphates and sulfates to form solids roots can't use (University of Missouri). Mix each part into the water separately; never combine concentrates.
- **Some single-bag fertilizers leave out calcium and magnesium.** Penn State notes that magnesium and calcium may not be included in complete soluble fertilizers, which is why its tomato program adds calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate to a 4-18-38 base.
- **Too much of one nutrient blocks another.** Penn State gives potassium as the example: in excess, it reduces uptake of calcium or magnesium.

For product types, see the [best hydroponic nutrients for home growers](/best/hydroponic-nutrients-for-home-growers/). For how much to add, follow the label and scale it to your reservoir with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/).

## Which vegetables can share a tank?

![Bar chart of pH by crop from Oklahoma State: basil 5.5–6.0, pepper 5.5–6.0, strawberry 6.0, tomato 6.0–6.5, broccoli 6.0–6.8, lettuce 6.0–7.0, spinach 6.0–7.0 all include pH 6.0, marked by a dashed line. Exceptions: cucumber 5.0–5.5, celery 6.5, cabbage 6.5–7.0 and pak choi 7.0. The University of Missouri gives pH 5.5–6.5 for most crops.](/images/guides/hydroponic-vegetables-ph-by-crop.svg)

*pH by crop from Oklahoma State HLA-6722 Table 2. pH 6.0 lies inside the green ranges; the orange crops need a tank of their own or a pH set for them.*

pH is usually the easy part: most crops in Oklahoma State's table include 6.0. Cucumber (5.0–5.5) and the crops at 6.5 or above are the exceptions. EC is what decides most pairings. HydroRigor worked out the overlaps from the table:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basil + pepper | 1.0–1.6 | 5.5–6.0 | Good match |
| Lettuce + basil | 1.2–1.6 | 6.0 | Good match |
| Lettuce + pepper | 1.2–1.8 | 6.0 | Good match (young pepper) |
| Spinach + strawberry | 1.8–2.2 | 6.0 | Good match |
| Tomato + broccoli | 2.8–3.5 | 6.0–6.5 | Good match |
| Tomato + strawberry | 2.0–2.2 | 6.0 | Narrow: low end for tomato |
| Lettuce + spinach | 1.8 only | 6.0–7.0 | Narrow: one value |
| Lettuce + pak choi | 1.5–1.8 | 7.0 | Good match, at pH 7.0 |
| Tomato + eggplant | 2.5–3.5 | 6.0 | Good match |
| Broccoli + cabbage | 2.8–3.0 | 6.5–6.8 | Good match |

**Recommendation:** run one tank for greens and herbs at about EC 1.4–1.6 and pH 6.0, a separate one for tomatoes at 2.0 or above, and give cucumbers their own. If you only have one reservoir, grow crops from one group, not a mix.

## Feeding by growth stage

Seedlings need a weaker solution than mature plants. The two sources that give stage figures:

| Stage | Fruiting crops (Tennessee) | Lettuce (Kentucky) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seedlings | EC 0.5–1.0 once the seed leaves open | EC about 1.0 |
| At transplant | EC 1.0–1.5 | EC about 1.5 |
| Growing and fruiting | EC 2.0–3.0 for much of the season | EC about 1.5; pH 5.5–6.0 |

Lettuce stays at about the same strength from transplant to harvest. Fruiting crops step up: start them gently and raise EC as they flower. The [tomato feeding guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-tomatoes/) gives the full stage-by-stage plan, including Penn State's 4-18-38 program.

## Start with your water

What comes out of the tap is part of the solution. The University of Missouri gives these limits for source water:

| Water test | Limit | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EC | 0.2–0.8 dS/m (mS/cm), ideally below 1 | University of Missouri |
| pH | 5.5–7.0 | University of Missouri |
| Alkalinity | 40–160 ppm as CaCO₃ | University of Missouri |
| Sodium | Below 50 ppm | University of Missouri |
| Chloride | Below 70 ppm | University of Missouri |
| Calcium | Below 150 ppm | University of Missouri |
| Boron | Below 0.5 ppm | University of Missouri |

Oklahoma State adds that alkalinity above 75 ppm pushes pH up, so hard water means correcting pH more often. Penn State's tip for custom recipes is to subtract what the water already supplies: with 30 ppm potassium in the water and a 210 ppm target, add 180. If your water fails these limits, filtered or reverse-osmosis water is the fix.

## Mixing and managing the solution

1. **Fill the reservoir** with water and note its starting EC.
2. **Add each nutrient part separately**, stirring between parts, in the order the label gives. Never mix concentrates together (University of Missouri).
3. **Read EC.** Oklahoma State says to stir and let the reading settle, which can take a couple of minutes. Dilute with water if it's high; add concentrate if it's low.
4. **Adjust pH last.** Oklahoma State's rule is to get EC in range first. Add pH down or up slowly and wait several minutes before adding more. Aim for 6.0 in a mixed tank.
5. **Check daily at the same time** (Oklahoma State) and log both numbers. A [pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) you calibrate is worth more than one you don't.
6. **Top up** with source water or concentrate as plants drink (Tennessee).
7. **Replace the solution.** Oklahoma State replaces it completely about every two weeks; Tennessee changes small recirculating tanks every one to two weeks; Kentucky replaces lettuce tanks every 3–4 weeks. Weekly topping up can't fix the balance: EC shows the total, not which nutrients are left (University of Missouri).

Keep the solution at about 72–75 °F (22–24 °C), Oklahoma State's figure. For the full method, see [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) and the [hydroponic nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/).

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Check first | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yellowing between veins on new leaves | pH too high, locking out iron | Bring pH back to 6.0 | Penn State |
| Brown leaf tips on lettuce | Calcium-related tipburn | Check calcium is in the nutrient; see the lettuce guide | Penn State |
| Dark, sunken patch at the blossom end of tomatoes | Calcium movement into the fruit | Check calcium in the nutrient; avoid high or swinging temperatures | Penn State; Tennessee |
| Wilting with leaf burn | Too many salts | Dilute or replace the solution | Penn State |
| Greens fine, tomatoes weak in a shared tank | EC too low for fruiting crops | Give tomatoes their own tank at 2.0+ | Oklahoma State; Tennessee |
| pH climbs a day after correcting | Alkaline source water | Test alkalinity; above 75 ppm pushes pH up | Oklahoma State |
| EC right but plants decline over weeks | Nutrients out of balance | Replace the solution every 1–2 weeks | Tennessee; University of Missouri |
| Strawberries struggle at the table's EC | Sources disagree | Try the University of Arizona's lower EC, at or below 1.0 | University of Arizona |

For a full guide to reading leaf symptoms, see the [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/).

## Where the sources disagree

- **Pepper:** Oklahoma State gives EC 0.8–1.8; Tennessee 2.0–3.0 for fruiting crops. Start low, raise at flowering.
- **Strawberry:** Oklahoma State gives EC 1.8–2.2; the University of Arizona's hydroponic strawberry program runs at or below 1.0. The [hydroponic strawberries guide](/crops/hydroponic-strawberries/) explains which to follow.
- **Lettuce pH:** Oklahoma State and UF/IFAS give 6.0–7.0; Kentucky gives 5.5–6.0. pH 6.0 sits at the edge of both.
- **Tomato EC:** Oklahoma State's 2.0–4.0 is wider than Tennessee's 2.0–3.0. Stay in the shared 2.0–3.0 unless you have a reason to go higher.
- **Kale and Swiss chard** aren't in Oklahoma State's table. No source here gives them a figure, so this guide doesn't.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best nutrient for hydroponic vegetables?

A complete hydroponic nutrient: one whose label lists calcium, magnesium and micronutrients as well as N-P-K, made for hydroponics rather than soil. Penn State lists 14 elements plants need from the solution. Two- and three-part products keep calcium away from phosphates and sulfates until they reach the water.

### Can I grow lettuce and tomatoes in the same reservoir?

Not well. Oklahoma State puts lettuce at EC 1.2–1.8 and tomatoes at 2.0–4.0, so the ranges don't overlap. Give tomatoes their own tank. Lettuce shares well with basil (EC 1.2–1.6, pH 6.0).

### What pH should hydroponic vegetables be at?

pH 6.0 fits most crops in Oklahoma State's table, from basil (5.5–6.0) to lettuce and spinach (6.0–7.0). The exceptions are cucumber (5.0–5.5) and celery, okra, leek, cabbage and pak choi (6.5 or higher). The University of Missouri's rule of thumb for most hydroponic crops is 5.5–6.5.

### Do vegetables need different nutrients at different stages?

Fruiting crops do. Tennessee starts seedlings at EC 0.5–1.0, raises it to 1.0–1.5 at transplant and holds 2.0–3.0 for much of the season. Lettuce stays at about EC 1.5 from transplant to harvest (Kentucky).

### How often should I change the nutrient solution?

About every two weeks (Oklahoma State), or every one to two weeks for small recirculating tanks (Tennessee). Lettuce tanks can go 3–4 weeks (Kentucky). Check pH and EC daily in between.

### Can I use regular garden fertilizer?

Use one made for hydroponics. Garden fertilizers often rely on the soil to supply calcium, magnesium and micronutrients, and Penn State notes that magnesium and calcium may be missing from complete soluble fertilizers. See the [lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) for a full crop example.
