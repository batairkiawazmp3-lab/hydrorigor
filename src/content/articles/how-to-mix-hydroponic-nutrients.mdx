To mix hydroponic nutrients, **start with the full volume of water, add one product at a time, and let each dissolve completely before adding the next**. Keep calcium apart from phosphates and sulfates while they are concentrated: University of Missouri Extension explains that they react to form solids roots cannot use. In a one-batch recipe such as Masterblend's tomato formula, that means **4-18-38 first, then magnesium sulfate (Epsom salt), then calcium nitrate**, at **2.4 g, 1.5 g and 2.4 g per gallon** (manufacturer's table). Then **set EC first and pH second** (Oklahoma State Extension): for lettuce, EC about **1.2–1.8 mS/cm** (UF/IFAS) and pH **5.5–6.0** (University of Kentucky); for most crops, pH **5.5–6.5** (Missouri).

Order matters because a mistake can hide. Missouri Extension notes that EC shows the total amount of fertilizer, not the balance of individual nutrients, so a batch where calcium and phosphate have dropped out as solids can still read close to target. HydroRigor has not tested these products; rates are the makers' and targets come from the university sources listed at the end.

## The mixing order, step by step

The rule behind every manufacturer's order is the same: dilute first, combine second. Missouri Extension tells growers to dissolve each formulation completely before combining. General Hydroponics puts it plainly for its three-part liquid: "Never combine concentrated FloraSeries components together undiluted", and add them one at a time, mixing well between each.

![Mixing order for hydroponic nutrients in six steps. 1: fill with the full volume of water. 2: add part one, the base fertilizer, and stir until dissolved. 3: add part two, magnesium sulfate, and stir until dissolved. 4: add calcium nitrate last and stir until dissolved. 5: check EC and dilute or add concentrate. 6: adjust pH to 5.5 to 6.5.](/images/guides/nutrient-mixing-order-steps.svg)

*The order behind most labels: water, then each part on its own, calcium last, then EC and pH. Based on Masterblend's instructions, Missouri Extension and Oklahoma State HLA-6722.*

1. **Measure the water.** Fill the reservoir or bucket to its final volume. Masterblend's first step is to choose the amount of final solution, because that sets every rate.
2. **Add the first part** and stir until it has dissolved. For Masterblend that is the 4-18-38; for FloraSeries it is FloraMicro.
3. **Add the second part** to the now-diluted solution and dissolve it.
4. **Add the calcium part last** (calcium nitrate in a dry three-part mix) and dissolve it.
5. **Stir and let the meter settle.** Oklahoma State says an EC reading "may take a couple of minutes" to stabilize.
6. **Correct EC, then pH.** Details are below.

Never measure two concentrates in the same cup or pour one into the other. If a label gives a different order from the one above, follow the label.

## Three ways to make a nutrient solution

Penn State Extension groups home and commercial nutrient solutions into three approaches. Which one you use decides how many parts you mix.

| Approach | What you mix | Example | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fertilizer program | A base plus calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate | 4-18-38 tomato base; growers add calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate by variety or stage | Penn State |
| Recipe from single salts | Each salt weighed to hit target ppm | Modified Sonneveld herb recipe: N 150, P 31, K 210, Ca 90, Mg 24 ppm | Penn State |
| Complete soluble fertilizer | One product | Lettuce at 100–150 ppm N; magnesium and calcium may not be included | Penn State |

For most home growers, a ready-made two- or three-part product is the simplest route. Single-salt recipes give the most control but need a scale that can weigh small amounts accurately; Penn State notes a high-precision scale is needed for micronutrients. More on choosing a product is in the [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/) and the [powder vs liquid nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/powder-vs-liquid-hydroponic-nutrients/).

Masterblend publishes its rates in grams for set volumes. The table below copies them, with liters added.

| 1 gal (3.8 L) | 2.4 g | 1.5 g | 2.4 g |
| 2 gal (7.6 L) | 4.5 g | 2.8 g | 4.5 g |
| 10 gal (38 L) | 23.0 g | 14.0 g | 23.0 g |
| 25 gal (95 L) | 57.0 g | 35.0 g | 57.0 g |
| 100 gal (379 L) | 227.0 g | 142.0 g | 227.0 g |

*Rates from Masterblend's mixing instructions (manufacturer). Liters are HydroRigor's conversion.*

The table has no 5-gallon row, which is the size of the common bucket. Halving the 10-gallon row gives **11.5 g, 7 g and 11.5 g**; five times the 1-gallon row gives 12 g, 7.5 g and 12 g. The small gap is rounding in the maker's table. Either is close enough for a home reservoir, and you then confirm the result with an EC meter. For young seedlings, Masterblend says to use half rates until they are well established.

The label gives no EC or pH target, so measure both after mixing.

**Recommendation:** weigh dry fertilizers in grams on a kitchen or jeweler's scale rather than using spoons; the manufacturer's table is in grams and gives no spoon equivalents. For liquid products dosed in mL per gallon, the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales each part to your reservoir size.

## Stock concentrates: why two tanks

Growers with injectors, or anyone who wants to mix once and dose for weeks, make concentrated stock solutions. Penn State says stock concentrations of 50×, 100× or 200× are common, depending on the injector and how much each salt will dissolve. At those strengths, calcium must be kept apart: The Ohio State University's tomato fact sheet notes the calcium portion "must be separated from the other macronutrients" at 100×, or it forms gypsum and phosphate precipitates.

![Two-tank stock concentrate system. Tank 1 holds monopotassium phosphate, magnesium sulfate and potassium sulfate or nitrate. Tank 2 holds calcium nitrate, calcium chloride and iron chelate. Never mix the two concentrates; dilute each into the reservoir water, 1 part in 100 for a 100X stock.](/images/guides/nutrient-stock-two-tanks.svg)

*Keep calcium in its own tank. Tank contents from Ohio State HYG-1437 Table 4 (100× stocks); Penn State describes the same split.*

Ohio State's recipe for the first stage of its three-stage tomato solution, as 100× stocks:

| Tank | Salt | Grams per liter of stock (stage 1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Monopotassium phosphate | 20.7 |
| 1 | Magnesium sulfate | 61.2 |
| 1 | Potassium sulfate | 20.7 |
| 2 | Calcium nitrate | 57.9 |
| 2 | Calcium chloride | 9.4 |
| 2 | Iron chelate (DTPA) | 2.0 |

*Ohio State HYG-1437 Table 4, stage 1. Later stages add potassium nitrate and more calcium nitrate. The recipe is written for reverse-osmosis water.*

Penn State's own example is the same idea at home scale: concentrated calcium nitrate in one tank, 4-18-38 plus magnesium sulfate in the other. Dissolving matters too. Penn State's solubility table shows how differently salts behave in cold water: about 121 g of calcium nitrate dissolves in 100 mL, but only about 13 g of potassium nitrate. A strong concentrate of a low-solubility salt will leave crystals at the bottom. Penn State also recommends keeping large tanks agitated with a propeller, circulating pump or air pump.

## Start with the water

What is already in your water changes the mix. Missouri Extension gives source-water limits for hydroponics: EC 0.2–0.8 dS/m (ideally below 1), pH 5.5–7.0, alkalinity 40–160 ppm as calcium carbonate, sodium below 50 ppm, chlorine below 70 ppm and calcium below 150 ppm. Oklahoma State adds that alkalinity above 75 ppm will push pH up, and that it can be removed with an acidic fertilizer, phosphoric acid, citric acid, vinegar or reverse osmosis.

If you know what your water contains, subtract it. Penn State's example: with 30 ppm potassium in the water and a 210 ppm target, add 180 ppm. A water report from your utility or a lab gives those numbers; test the starting EC yourself with your meter.

## Check EC first, then pH

EC tells you how much fertilizer is in the water; pH decides whether plants can take it up. Oklahoma State is specific: "pH should always be checked after getting the EC into the optimum range," because adding more nutrient or more water shifts pH again.

**EC, following Oklahoma State:**

1. Calibrate the probe with a standard, usually 1.41 mS/cm (Michigan State uses the same).
2. Stir and let the reading settle for a couple of minutes.
3. If EC is high, dilute with more water and measure again.
4. If EC is low, add nutrient concentrate (in the same order) until it reaches the target.

**pH, following Oklahoma State:**

1. Calibrate with buffer, commonly pH 4, 7 or 10.
2. If pH is high, add phosphoric acid, citric acid, vinegar or a pH-down product slowly, and "wait several minutes before adding more".
3. If pH is low, add potassium hydroxide, potassium carbonate or a pH-up product slowly.
4. Rinse and store the probe.

![Bar chart of target EC by crop in mS per cm. Lettuce 1.2 to 1.8 (UF/IFAS); Purdue recommends 1.3. Basil 1.0 to 1.6 (Oklahoma State). Pepper 0.8 to 1.8 (Oklahoma State). Fruiting crops 2.0 to 3.0 (University of Tennessee). Tomato 2.0 to 4.0 (Oklahoma State).](/images/guides/nutrient-ec-targets-by-crop.svg)

*EC targets to aim for after mixing. Figures from UF/IFAS HS1422, Purdue HO-309-W, Oklahoma State HLA-6722 and University of Tennessee W 844-C.*

**Where sources differ.** For lettuce pH, Oklahoma State's table gives 6.0–7.0 and the University of Kentucky 5.5–6.0; Missouri's rule of thumb for most crops is 5.5–6.5. The ranges meet at 6.0, so this guide suggests 5.5–6.0 for lettuce, where Kentucky's production guide sits, and 5.5–6.5 for other crops. For lettuce EC, UF/IFAS gives 1.2–1.8 and Purdue's trial of 24 varieties recommends 1.3, finding slower growth at 2.9 and 3.9. For tomatoes, Oklahoma State's 2.0–4.0 is wider than Tennessee's 2.0–3.0; start in the overlap, 2.0–3.0. Solution temperature also differs: Oklahoma State calls 72–75 °F (22–24 °C) optimal, while UF/IFAS gives a wider 65–80 °F (18–27 °C). The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists more crops, and the [pH meter guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) covers calibration.

## Step-by-step: mixing a fresh batch

1. **Clean the reservoir** and rinse it.
2. **Fill with the final volume** of water and note its starting EC.
3. **Work out each part's dose** for that volume from the label (or the table above). Weigh or measure each part separately.
4. **Add part one** and stir until dissolved.
5. **Add part two** and stir until dissolved.
6. **Add the calcium part last** and stir until dissolved.
7. **Wait a couple of minutes, then read EC.** Dilute or add concentrate to reach the crop's target.
8. **Read pH and adjust slowly**, waiting several minutes between additions.
9. **Write it down:** date, volume, doses, EC and pH.
10. **Check daily at the same time** (Oklahoma State) and top up as the plants drink. Oklahoma State advises replacing the solution completely every two weeks; the University of Kentucky replaces lettuce solution every 3–4 weeks, and Tennessee changes small recirculating tanks every 1–2 weeks. The [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains how to choose.

## Troubleshooting a new mix

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cloudy water or white sediment | Calcium met concentrated phosphate or sulfate | Mix fresh; dilute each part before adding the next; keep stocks in two tanks | Missouri G6984; Penn State |
| Crystals left at the bottom | Concentrate too strong for the salt's solubility | Stir longer, use more water, or make a weaker stock | Penn State |
| EC too high | Too much fertilizer for the volume | Add water and re-measure | Oklahoma State |
| EC too low | Underdosed or volume larger than planned | Add concentrate, in order, and re-measure | Oklahoma State |
| pH keeps rising | High-alkalinity source water (above 75 ppm) | Acidify, or switch to RO or filtered water | Oklahoma State |
| EC reads right but plants show deficiency | EC shows the total, not the balance | Check each part was added; follow the label order | Missouri G6984 |
| Yellowing between veins on new leaves | Iron unavailable, often from high pH | Bring pH down into range | Penn State |
| Meter readings jump around | Uncalibrated or dried-out probe | Recalibrate (pH 4.01 and 7.01; EC 1.41 mS/cm) | Michigan State |

## Frequently asked questions

### What order do you mix hydroponic nutrients in?

Water first, then one part at a time, dissolving each before the next. For a dry three-part mix, Masterblend's order is 4-18-38, then magnesium sulfate, then calcium nitrate. For FloraSeries liquids, General Hydroponics adds FloraMicro first, then FloraGro, then FloraBloom.

### Can I mix part A and part B together before adding water?

No. Concentrated calcium reacts with phosphates and sulfates to form solids roots cannot use (Missouri; Penn State). Add each part to the water separately.

### How much nutrient do I add per gallon?

Follow the label. As one example, Masterblend lists 2.4 g of 4-18-38, 1.5 g of magnesium sulfate and 2.4 g of calcium nitrate per gallon, at half rate for seedlings. Then check EC against your crop's target.

### Should I adjust pH before or after adding nutrients?

After. Oklahoma State says to get EC into range first and check pH afterward, because nutrients change pH.

### Can I use tap water?

Usually, if it falls inside Missouri's source-water limits (for example sodium below 50 ppm and alkalinity 40–160 ppm). Oklahoma State notes that water above 75 ppm alkalinity will keep pushing pH up.
