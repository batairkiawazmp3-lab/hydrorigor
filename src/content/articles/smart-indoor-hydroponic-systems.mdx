A smart indoor hydroponic system is a small water-culture garden with a built-in grow light, a timer and usually a water-level alert or an app. It is worth buying for **lettuce, herbs and leafy greens**, which UF/IFAS Extension says can be ready in **3–4 weeks (herbs) and within 40 days (lettuce)** in a pod-based unit. The feature that matters most is **light**. The Singapore Food Agency's buying guide asks for at least **150–200 µmol/m²/s for lettuce and 200–400 for herbs and other leafy vegetables**, and a light of **at least 200 at 10 cm (4 in)** for general edibles. Lettuce needs a **daily light integral (DLI) of 12–17 mol/m²/day** (University of Kentucky), which at the **16-hour** day a typical unit's timer runs (UF/IFAS) means about **210 µmol/m²/s or more** at the leaves.

Apps and reminders save effort but not the work: you still top up water, add nutrients at least weekly and clean the whole unit at least every three months (Singapore Food Agency). HydroRigor has not tested any smart garden; every figure comes from the sources listed at the end, and arithmetic is labelled.

**Where sources differ.** On day length, UF/IFAS describes a **16-hour** timer, the Singapore guide works from **about 12 hours** and University of Illinois Extension suggests **6–8 hours** for a home tank. ## What makes a hydroponic system "smart"

The Singapore Food Agency describes the typical kit as a water reservoir with a cover, growing cups and grow lights, with the roots in the reservoir water. Some units add a small pump or air stone. The "smart" layer sits on top:

| Feature | What it does | What it does not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Built-in light and timer | Gives a repeatable day length; UF/IFAS describes 16 hours set from when you plug it in | Guarantee enough light; that depends on intensity at the leaves |
| Adjustable light arm | Lets you keep the lamp close to small plants and raise it later | Stop leaves scorching if you forget to raise it |
| Water-level window or sensor | Shows or warns when the reservoir is low | Replace topping up; Singapore warns too little water can cut growth by 20–30% |
| App reminders | Prompts you to add nutrients or water | Measure what the plants have used |
| pH or EC sensor (some units) | Shows a reading without dipping a pen | Calibrate itself; probes drift (UMass, Michigan State) |
| Pump or air stone (some units) | Moves or aerates the solution | Keep working in a power cut |

Ask of each feature what action it prompts. A water-level alert prompts one: add water. A growth score with no unit or method prompts nothing. University of Missouri Extension places fully controlled indoor production at the complex end of growing: every sensor, pump and setting is one more thing to check.

## Light: the feature to check first

Plants grow by the total light they receive in a day, the daily light integral. Work it out from the intensity at the leaves (PPFD, in µmol/m²/s) and the hours the light is on:

**DLI = PPFD × hours on × 0.0036**

The 0.0036 converts seconds to hours and micromoles to moles. Here is what the Singapore minimums give at the two day lengths in the sources (HydroRigor's arithmetic):

| Light at the leaves | 12 hours a day | 16 hours a day | Lettuce target (Kentucky) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 150 µmol/m²/s | DLI 6.5 | DLI 8.6 | 12–17 |
| 200 µmol/m²/s | DLI 8.6 | DLI 11.5 | 12–17 |
| 210 µmol/m²/s | DLI 9.1 | DLI 12.1 | 12–17 |
| 300 µmol/m²/s | DLI 13.0 | DLI 17.3 | 12–17 |

![Bar chart of daily light integral from a smart garden light. 150 µmol per square meter per second for 12 hours gives a DLI of 6.5; 200 for 12 hours gives 8.6; 200 for 16 hours gives 11.5; 300 for 16 hours gives 17.3. 210 for 16 hours gives 12.1. A shaded band marks the lettuce target of 12 to 17 from the University of Kentucky; only the 210 and 300 bars reach it.](/images/guides/smart-garden-light-dli-check.svg)

*Light intensity times hours gives the daily total. Minimum PPFD values from the Singapore Food Agency; timer hours from UF/IFAS; lettuce DLI from the University of Kentucky. DLI values are HydroRigor's arithmetic.*

A light that only just meets the Singapore minimum for lettuce (150–200 µmol/m²/s) falls short of Kentucky's lettuce DLI at either day length. Reaching DLI 12 takes about **210 µmol/m²/s for 16 hours** or about **280 for 12 hours**. Fruiting crops are a stretch: Purdue gives tomatoes and strawberries a DLI above 20, about 350 µmol/m²/s for 16 hours, and the Singapore guide puts fruiting vegetables at 400–700. It matters for yield: in Purdue's trial, 15 lettuce varieties grown for 28 days averaged 0.90 lb/m² at a DLI of 5.6 and 6.7 lb/m² at 19.8.

Distance matters too. The Singapore guide notes that a lamp close to small plants gives them more light, leaves touching it may scorch, and weak light makes tall stems that fall over. UF/IFAS advises a few inches between plants and lamp.

**Recommendation:** ask for the PPFD at a stated distance, not just the wattage. Aim for at least 200 µmol/m²/s at 10 cm (4 in), as the Singapore guide advises, and about 210 or more over 16 hours for Kentucky's lettuce target. If a maker will not state PPFD, plan on herbs, lettuce and microgreens only.

## What grows well in a smart garden

| Crop | Fit for a countertop unit | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lettuce | Good | Within 40 days in a pod unit (UF/IFAS); pH 5.5–6.0, DLI 12–17 (Kentucky) |
| Basil and other herbs | Good | Herbs in 3–4 weeks (UF/IFAS); basil leaves from about 6 weeks after sowing indoors (Solis-Toapanta et al.) |
| [Microgreens](/crops/hydroponic-microgreens/) and seedlings | Good | 100–150 µmol/m²/s is enough (Singapore Food Agency) |
| Leafy vegetables such as pak choi | Fair to good | Need 200–400 µmol/m²/s (Singapore Food Agency) |
| Tomatoes, peppers, strawberries | Hard | 400–700 µmol/m²/s (Singapore) and DLI above 20 (Purdue) |

The best small-unit evidence is for basil. In a University of Florida study (HortScience), four basil plants grew in an opaque, aerated 7.6 L (about 2 gal) unit with a four-cup lid. Indoors at 21 °C (70 °F) and a DLI of 9, each unit produced about 344–359 g (12–13 oz), and topping up matched replacing the solution. In a greenhouse at a DLI of 26.1 the same units produced about four times as much: the light effect again. More on basil targets is in the [hydroponic basil guide](/crops/hydroponic-basil/), and lettuce targets are in the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/).

**Recommendation:** buy a smart garden for herbs, lettuce and greens. If tomatoes or strawberries are the goal, a larger system with a stronger light is the better route; the [indoor hydroponic systems comparison](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) shows which designs suit fruiting crops.

## Sensors and apps: what they can and cannot do

A built-in pH or EC probe is still a probe. UMass Extension: "Calibration is a must to get any useful information from pens and meters!", and readings drift even on expensive meters. Michigan State calibrates pH with 4.01 and 7.01 buffers and EC with a 1.41 mS/cm standard, and soaks a dried-out probe for at least an hour first. Oklahoma State advises checking pH and EC at the same time each day, EC first.

Before you pay, ask: what does the sensor measure and in what unit; how is it calibrated; can worn parts (probes, pumps, pods) be bought separately; does the light schedule keep running without Wi-Fi or the app; and can you use your own seeds and nutrients? If the sensor cannot be calibrated, a separate pen can, as the [pH meter for hydroponics guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) explains. No source here tests apps or proprietary pods, so this guide makes no claim about them.

## Upkeep a smart garden still needs

The Singapore Food Agency says even "fuss-free" kits need daily checks:

![Upkeep schedule for a smart indoor hydroponic garden in three bands. Daily: check the water level, check the light timer, look for mosquito larvae, remove dried leaves. Weekly: add nutrients as the maker directs, at least once a week. Every three months: take the unit apart and wash the reservoir, pods and cover to remove algae and disease.](/images/guides/smart-garden-upkeep-schedule.svg)

*Upkeep from the Singapore Food Agency buying guide: water and timer checks daily, nutrients at least weekly, a full clean at least every three months (more often between crops). If you have a pen, Oklahoma State advises checking pH and EC at the same time each day.*

UF/IFAS warns against well water or softened water, which can damage the system and affect growth. Keep light out of the reservoir: University of Illinois Extension covers clear tank sides to stop algae. When you shop, the Singapore guide's design checks make upkeep easier: a top-up point, a water-level window, a light that raises, and a reservoir that comes apart for washing. Tall vertical or hanging units with many pipes are harder to clean.

## Step by step: setting up a smart garden

1. **Pick a level spot** away from drafts with room for the light at full height, in a room at 65–78 °F (18–26 °C) (Nebraska Extension).
2. **Assemble** following the maker's directions; UF/IFAS notes each system is a little different.
3. **Fill with tap or filtered water** to the top of the level indicator, without overfilling (UF/IFAS).
4. **Add nutrients** at the maker's dose. If you mix your own, aim for EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm for lettuce (UF/IFAS HS1422) and 1.0–1.6 for basil (Oklahoma State), and see the [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/).
5. **Check pH** if you have a pen: 5.5–6.0 for lettuce (Kentucky) and basil (Oklahoma State). The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists other crops.
6. **Plant the pods** under their domes; remove the domes once seeds sprout, usually after 1–2 weeks (UF/IFAS).
7. **Confirm the timer** (UF/IFAS describes 16 hours a day) and raise the light as plants grow, keeping a few inches of space.
8. **Keep the routine:** water daily, nutrients weekly, a full clean at least every three months.
9. **Harvest** herbs from about 3–4 weeks and lettuce within 40 days (UF/IFAS).

## Smart garden or a simple system?

A smart garden buys convenience: a sized, timed light, a neat housing and reminders. A [Kratky bucket](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) needs no pump or electricity (UF/IFAS HS1488) but still needs a grow light indoors, and a [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tub holds more plants. If a unit has a pump, check its power-cut behavior: Oregon State says plants in pump-fed channels can wilt quickly, while roots sitting in solution keep their water (University of Missouri). See also [hydroponic systems for beginners](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/) and [indoor hydroponic system kits](/best/indoor-hydroponic-system-kits/).

**Recommendation:** choose a smart garden for a few herbs or a salad's worth of lettuce on a counter, checked daily. Choose a simple system with a measured grow light for more plants, your own seeds or fruiting crops.

## Troubleshooting smart gardens

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tall, weak, floppy stems | Too little light | Lower the light; check PPFD and DLI | Singapore Food Agency; University of Kentucky |
| Scorched top leaves | Leaves touching the lamp | Raise the light a few inches | Singapore Food Agency; UF/IFAS |
| Slow growth | Reservoir running low | Top up; too little water can cut growth 20–30% | Singapore Food Agency |
| Green film in the tank | Light reaching the solution | Close gaps; cover clear sides | University of Illinois; Singapore Food Agency |
| Brown, slimy roots | Warm solution, debris | Keep solution 65–80 °F (18–27 °C); wash the unit | UF/IFAS HS1422; Singapore Food Agency |
| Wrigglers in the water | Mosquitoes breeding | Check daily; clean stagnant corners | Singapore Food Agency |
| App pH reading looks wrong | Uncalibrated or dry probe | Calibrate with 4.01 and 7.01 buffers | Michigan State; UMass |
| Lettuce bolts | Room too warm | Keep days at or below 75 °F (24 °C) and nights at or below 65 °F (18 °C) | University of Kentucky |

## Frequently asked questions

### Are smart indoor hydroponic systems worth it?

For herbs, lettuce and greens, yes, if the light is strong enough: herbs in 3–4 weeks and lettuce within 40 days (UF/IFAS). Not for tomatoes: fruiting crops need 400–700 µmol/m²/s (Singapore Food Agency) and a DLI above 20 (Purdue).

### How many hours should a smart garden light be on?

Sources differ: UF/IFAS describes 16 hours, the Singapore guide works from about 12 and Illinois suggests 6–8 for a home tank. Use the DLI instead: for lettuce, PPFD × hours × 0.0036 should reach 12–17 (Kentucky). At 200 µmol/m²/s that takes about 17 hours; at 300 it takes about 11–16.

### How far should the light be from the plants?

A few inches above the top of the plants (UF/IFAS). The Singapore guide's benchmark is at least 200 µmol/m²/s at 10 cm (4 in). Raise the light as plants grow so leaves do not touch it.

### Do I need to check pH in a smart garden?

It is worth it: a pen tells you whether the solution suits the crop, 5.5–6.0 for lettuce (Kentucky) and basil (Oklahoma State). A built-in sensor still needs calibrating (UMass, Michigan State).

### Can I use my own seeds instead of pods?

The sources do not cover proprietary pods; check the maker's terms. If the unit takes standard net cups, see the [hydroponic lettuce seeds guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-seeds/).
