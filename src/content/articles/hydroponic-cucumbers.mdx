Hydroponic cucumbers want a solution at **pH 5.5–6.0** (UF/IFAS and the University of Tennessee), an EC of about **2.0 mS/cm** once plants are fruiting, warm days of **75–85 °F (24–29 °C)** and nights that stay above **60–65 °F (16–18 °C)**. Space plants **12–18 in (30–46 cm) apart**, give each one **3–5 gallons (11–19 L)** of root room (Alabama Extension), and grow a seedless (parthenocarpic) variety so you never have to pollinate. Expect the first fruit **50–70 days after seeding** (University of Alaska) and, in a greenhouse, **20–25 lb (9–11 kg) per plant** over a 12-week harvest (UF/IFAS; Alabama Extension).

The sources disagree on pH and water use; this guide shows where, and says what we would do.

## Pick the right cucumber type

Indoors there are no bees, so the variety matters.

| Type | Fruit at harvest | Pollination | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| European (English) seedless | 12–14 in, 300–400 g; up to 24 in in Alaska's guide | None: parthenocarpic, sets fruit without pollen | UF/IFAS CV268; Alabama ANR-2926; Alaska HGA-00434 |
| Mini or Beit Alpha | 5–8 in, 80–120 g | None: parthenocarpic | UF/IFAS CV268; Alabama ANR-2926 |
| American slicer (seeded) | 7–8 in | Needed: hand-pollinate with a soft brush | Alaska HGA-00434 |

**Our pick:** a seedless mini (Beit Alpha) or European type. UF/IFAS advises gynoecious (all-female) parthenocarpic cultivars, and Alaska notes that seedless types produce only female flowers, so pollination is "not necessary or desired". Seeded slicers need you to move pollen by hand, and Alaska warns that poor pollination gives misshapen, half-filled fruit. One caution: UF/IFAS says mini cultivars are especially prone to powdery mildew, so choose one sold as mildew-resistant if you can.

## Which hydroponic system suits cucumbers

Cucumbers are big, thirsty vines, so systems with generous root room work best.

| System | How cucumbers fare | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dutch buckets with drip | Missouri Extension calls bucket systems ideal for vines such as cucumber, with a trellis; a full-cycle trial grew cucumbers in Dutch buckets of several media | Missouri Extension; Yang et al. 2022 |
| Bags or pots of perlite, coir or pine bark | The standard greenhouse method in Florida and Alabama (3- or 5-gallon pots) | UF/IFAS HS169; Alabama ANR-2926 |
| 5-gallon deep water culture bucket | Suited to long-term crops such as cucumbers if the water is well aerated | UF/IFAS Volusia County |
| NFT channels | Used commercially, but UF/IFAS lists Pythium root rot in NFT among cucumber diseases and warns that warm solution holds less oxygen | UF/IFAS CV268 |
| Kratky (no pump) | No source we read tested cucumbers this way | — |

**For a home grower:** one plant per 5-gallon [Dutch bucket](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/) on a [drip system](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/), or one plant in an aerated [deep water culture bucket](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/). Both give the 3–5 gallons Alabama uses and leave room to run a string up to a wire.

**Which medium?** In the Dutch bucket trial, medium pine bark gave 23.9% more yield than perlite, and coir lost 24% less water to leaching. A separate greenhouse study found coir gave higher cucumber yields than rockwool. Both are single studies, but they suggest coir or bark is a sound choice. See [rockwool vs coco coir](/equipment/rockwool-vs-coco-coir/) and [hydroponic growing media compared](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/).

## Temperature: warm days, nights above 60 °F

Cucumbers are a warm crop. The two Southern guides agree: they **grow best at 80–85 °F (27–29 °C)**, and growth and yield drop **below 65 °F (18 °C)**. UF/IFAS adds that **long spells above 95 °F (35 °C)** should be avoided. Alaska's guide, written for cooler greenhouses, aims lower: **75–80 °F (24–27 °C) by day** and nights **not below 60 °F (16 °C)** for the earliest crop.

Roots feel cold too. UF/IFAS HS169 reports wilting, iron deficiency and slow growth when the growing medium drops **below 65 °F (18 °C)**, and advises raising bags 2–3 in (5–8 cm) off a cold floor.

**In practice:** indoors, aim for days in the high 70s to low 80s °F and nights of at least 65 °F. Keep the room air moving: Alabama puts ideal humidity at **60–70%** and warns that disease pressure climbs above 70%; Alaska links high humidity and excess nitrogen to powdery mildew.

## Light: as much as you can give

Michigan State's Erik Runkle names the daily light integral (DLI) as the biggest limit on vine crops such as tomato, pepper and cucumber. He suggests **at least 15, and preferably more than 20 mol/m²/day**, against 12–14 for lettuce, and notes that each 1% more light gives about 1% more yield. Alabama goes further: at its optimum of **DLI 30 or more**, fruit is ready about **10 days after it sets**.

That is a lot of light for a home setup. Runkle also suggests four to six hours of darkness each night, so plan a long day, not a 24-hour one. Seedlings need at least **14 hours of light a day** (Alaska). No source we read tested cucumbers under a home grow light, so treat these as greenhouse figures. The [indoor systems guide](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) explains how to estimate DLI from a light's rating.

## Feeding: pH, EC and nutrients by stage

**pH: aim for 5.8.** UF/IFAS and Tennessee give **5.5–6.0**, and Alabama 5.5–6.5. Oklahoma State's crop table is the odd one out at **5.0–5.5**. With three of four sources overlapping at 5.5–6.0, we recommend 5.8 and correcting anything outside 5.5–6.0. A calibrated meter matters more than its brand; see [how to choose and calibrate a pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/).

**EC: start low, settle around 2.0.** The sources broadly agree once plants fruit: **1.7–2.0** (Oklahoma State), **2.0–2.5** (Alabama) and **2.0–3.0** for fruiting crops (Tennessee). Tennessee and Alabama both start young plants weaker.

| Stage | Tennessee EC (mS/cm) | Alabama N / K (ppm) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seedlings | 0.5–1.0 | 50–100 N (half strength) | Tennessee W 844-C; Alabama ANR-2926 |
| First two weeks after transplant | 1.0–1.5 | 60–80 / 50–70 | Tennessee W 844-C; Alabama Table 1 |
| Flowering to first harvest | 2.0–3.0 | 100–120 / 140–160 | Tennessee W 844-C; Alabama Table 1 |
| After first harvest | 2.0–3.0 | 120–140 / 160–180 | Tennessee W 844-C; Alabama Table 1 |

The EC and ppm columns come from different sources, so read them as two views of the same rising curve, not as one recipe.

**What we recommend:** about **1.0–1.5** after transplant, rising to **2.0** at flowering and no higher than **2.5** while picking. That sits inside Alabama's fruiting range and overlaps Oklahoma State's, and it stays below Tennessee's 3.0 ceiling because both UF/IFAS HS1274 and HS169 say cucumbers tolerate salt less well than tomatoes. Use the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) and the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/); [hydroponic nutrients for vegetables](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-vegetables/) covers growing cucumbers beside other crops.

**Water: plan for a thirsty plant.** Alaska's guide starts small plants on about **1 quart (1 L) of solution a day** and puts a mature fruiting plant at **1–2 gallons (4–8 L) a day**. UF/IFAS HS1274 gives a lower figure, **2–2.5 quarts (about 2–2.4 L) a day** at peak harvest, for Florida's media systems. Use depends on plant size, light and heat. Size the reservoir for the high end and check it daily (see the [reservoir size guide](/equipment/hydroponic-reservoir-size/)). In media systems, UF/IFAS HS169 suggests leaching about **20%** at each irrigation for cucumbers, more than the 10–15% for tomatoes. Tennessee advises changing small recirculating tanks every one to two weeks.

## Pruning and training: the umbrella system

Cucumbers trained to a single stem up a string make the most of indoor space. All three extension guides describe the **umbrella system**:

1. Run a string from the pot to a wire **6–8 ft (1.8–2.4 m)** overhead (Alaska and Alabama).
2. Remove every side shoot on the main stem as it appears (UF/IFAS).
3. Strip all fruit, flowers and side shoots from the **bottom 24–30 in (60–75 cm)**, about the lower five to seven leaf nodes (UF/IFAS; Alabama; Alaska). Alabama lets no flowers form until the sixth or seventh true leaf.
4. Keep **one fruit per leaf node** on European seedless types and remove extras. Minis and Beit Alpha types can carry clusters, and UF/IFAS says they must not be thinned to single fruit.
5. When the stem is **one or two leaves past the wire**, pinch the tip and train the top two side shoots over the wire and down (UF/IFAS). Alaska lets them grow about two-thirds of the way back down.

![Umbrella pruning: a single main stem grows up a string to a wire 6 to 8 feet high. Fruit, flowers and side shoots are removed from the bottom 24 to 30 inches, about five to seven nodes. One fruit per node is kept on the main stem. One or two leaves past the wire the tip is pinched, and two side shoots are trained over the wire and down.](/images/guides/hydroponic-cucumbers-umbrella-pruning.svg)

*Umbrella training for seedless cucumbers (UF/IFAS CV268; Alabama ANR-2926; Alaska HGA-00434).*

Is the umbrella the best method? Not always. Alabama reports that methods keeping the growing point intact raised yields by **7–43%** over umbrella-type pruning across several studies, and a UF/IFAS trial found draping stems over the wire gave higher yield and quality than pinching in most cases. With a low ceiling the umbrella is simplest; with height, let the stem run along the wire.

## Step by step: one cucumber in a 5-gallon bucket

![Timeline for seedless cucumbers: germinate at 80 to 84 °F in 2 to 3 days; transplant 2 to 3 weeks after seeding at three or four true leaves; no fruit on the bottom 24 to 30 inches; at the wire pinch the tip and train two laterals down; first harvest 50 to 70 days from seed; pick three or four times a week over about 12 weeks.](/images/guides/hydroponic-cucumbers-timeline.svg)

*From seed to harvest (Alaska HGA-00434; UF/IFAS CV268; Alabama ANR-2926).*

1. **Choose a seedless mini or European variety**, mildew-resistant if offered. Sow 10–15% more seeds than you need (UF/IFAS).
2. **Germinate warm.** 80–82 °F (27–28 °C) for seedless types (Alaska; UF/IFAS gives 84 °F); seed is up in 2–3 days. Sow ½–1 in (1–2.5 cm) deep and give seedlings at least 14 hours of light.
3. **Set up the bucket.** A 5-gallon Dutch bucket of coir, bark or perlite on drip, or an opaque 5-gallon bucket with a 6-in net pot and an air stone for DWC (UF/IFAS Volusia County). See the [air pump sizing guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/).
4. **Mix the solution** at pH 5.8 and EC 1.0–1.5.
5. **Transplant at 2–3 weeks**, at three or four true leaves, and tie a string to an overhead support 6–8 ft up.
6. **Prune weekly.** Remove side shoots and strip the bottom 24–30 in; keep one fruit per node on European types.
7. **Raise EC to about 2.0 at flowering** and check pH, EC and the water level daily; change the solution every one to two weeks.
8. **Pick often.** Cut fruit with a knife at 12–14 in for European types or 5–8 in for minis, three or four times a week (UF/IFAS). Do not leave mature fruit on the vine: it cuts production (Alaska) and makes fruit bitter (Alabama).

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| White powder on leaves | Powdery mildew, favored by high humidity and excess nitrogen | Ventilate, keep humidity at 60–70%, do not overfeed nitrogen; grow resistant varieties | Alaska HGA-00434; Alabama ANR-2926; UF/IFAS CV268 |
| Gray fuzzy mold on stems or fruit | Gray mold (Botrytis), worse with poor ventilation and high humidity | Improve airflow; remove old lower leaves | Alaska HGA-00434 |
| Crooked or curled fruit | Lack of water, low potassium, fruit caught on clips, or too many fruit | Keep the reservoir full, feed potassium as fruit sets, thin to one fruit per node | Alabama ANR-2926 |
| Misshapen, half-filled fruit on a seeded variety | Poor pollination | Hand-pollinate, or switch to a seedless variety | Alaska HGA-00434 |
| Bitter fruit | Overripe fruit left on the plant | Pick at size, several times a week | Alabama ANR-2926 |
| Small fruit drop or abort | Over-fruiting | Remove extra fruit; strip the bottom 24–30 in | Alabama ANR-2926; Alaska HGA-00434 |
| Wilting, yellow new leaves, slow growth | Root zone below 65 °F (18 °C) | Warm the room; lift pots 2–3 in off a cold floor | UF/IFAS HS169 |
| Brown, slimy roots | Pythium root rot, especially in warm NFT or DWC with little oxygen | Keep solution cooler and well aerated; see the [DWC guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) | UF/IFAS CV268 |
| Leaf edges burn | EC too high; cucumbers tolerate salt less than tomatoes | Dilute toward EC 2.0 | UF/IFAS HS1274; UF/IFAS HS169 |
| Reservoir drops fast | Mature plants can use 2–2.5 quarts to 2 gallons a day | Check daily; use a larger tank | UF/IFAS HS1274; Alaska HGA-00434 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What pH do hydroponic cucumbers need?

pH 5.5–6.0 (UF/IFAS and the University of Tennessee). Alabama allows up to 6.5, and Oklahoma State lists 5.0–5.5. We recommend 5.8.

### What EC or ppm should I use for cucumbers?

Start around EC 1.0–1.5 mS/cm after transplant and run about 2.0–2.5 once fruiting (Alabama 2.0–2.5; Oklahoma State 1.7–2.0). Alabama feeds 150–200 ppm nitrogen. Total-dissolved-solids "ppm" depends on your meter's conversion factor, so work in EC.

### How long do hydroponic cucumbers take to grow?

First fruit comes about 50–70 days after seeding (University of Alaska), or as little as 7 weeks (Alabama). Fruit reaches size roughly 10–14 days after the flower opens. Harvest then runs for about 12 weeks in a greenhouse.

### How much does one hydroponic cucumber plant yield?

UF/IFAS and Alabama both report 20–25 lb (9–11 kg) per plant over a 12-week spring harvest in commercial greenhouses, with 1–3 lb per plant per week at peak. Under less light indoors, expect less; no source we read measured home yields.

### Do hydroponic cucumbers need to be pollinated?

Not if you grow a seedless (parthenocarpic) European or Beit Alpha variety; they set fruit without pollen. Seeded slicers need hand pollination with a soft brush indoors (Alaska).

### Can I grow cucumbers with tomatoes or peppers?

They share Tennessee's pH 5.5–6.0 and EC staging, but cucumbers tolerate salt less than tomatoes (UF/IFAS), so run a shared tank at the lower end, about EC 2.0. See the [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/) and [hydroponic peppers](/crops/hydroponic-peppers/) guides.

*Figures in this guide come from the university, extension and peer-reviewed sources listed below. Most cucumber research is from commercial greenhouses, not home grow rooms. HydroRigor has not grown cucumbers for this guide.*
