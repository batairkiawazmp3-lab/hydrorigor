**Powder and liquid hydroponic nutrients feed plants equally well when they deliver the same amount of each nutrient.** Mississippi State University Extension puts it directly: properly applied, liquid fertilizers "are no more or less efficient than dry fertilizers", so use the same rate of nutrients from either source. What changes is the work. **Powder** is weighed in grams (Masterblend's dry three-part mix is **2.4 g, 1.5 g and 2.4 g per gallon**), must be dissolved completely and kept dry. **Liquid** is measured in milliliters, is easier to dose in a small reservoir, and should be stored **above freezing**, since a solution can "salt out" when cold. Either way, finish by checking **EC first, then pH** (Oklahoma State Extension).

Our recommendation: start with a **liquid** two- or three-part product if you grow in one small reservoir and do not own a scale that reads to 0.1 g; choose **powder** once you mix 5 gallons (about 19 L) or more at a time, have an accurate scale, and want more nutrient per package. Purdue Extension's view matches: premixed fertilizer "might be perfect for beginner growers", while experienced growers can do more with their own formulations. HydroRigor has not tested any product; the reasoning and numbers below come from the university and manufacturer sources listed at the end.

## What actually differs between powder and liquid

A liquid hydroponic nutrient is, in practice, fertilizer salts that the manufacturer has already dissolved in water. A powder is the same kind of salts, dry. Once either is diluted in your reservoir, roots see a nutrient solution, and a meter sees its EC. That is why Mississippi State's fluid fertilizer guide finds "no agronomic difference" between liquid and dry when the nutrient rate is the same. (That publication is written for field crops; its point about efficiency carries over because a hydroponic reservoir is a solution either way.)

The differences are practical:

| Feature | Powder (dry salts) | Liquid concentrate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measuring | Grams on a scale | mL with a syringe or cup |
| Small reservoirs | Needs a fine scale | Easier |
| Nutrient per package | Higher (no water) | Lower (mostly water) |
| Dissolving | Your job; can leave sludge | Done at the factory |
| Storage risk | Moisture, caking | Freezing, salting out |
| Mixing order | Each part separately | Each part separately |
| Final check | EC, then pH | EC, then pH |

## Why liquids hold less nutrient per bottle

A liquid can only carry as much fertilizer as stays dissolved. Mississippi State gives a clear example from field fertilizers: "no more than 11 percent potash will go into solution, and 9 percent is more practical to manufacture." Penn State's solubility table (grams that dissolve in 100 mL of cold water) shows how much salts vary: about **121 g of calcium nitrate** but only about **13 g of potassium nitrate**. A dry product has no such ceiling, which is why dry fertilizer grades such as 4-18-38 carry far more nutrient per pound than a ready-to-use liquid.

For a home grower that means two things. A bottle of liquid concentrate contains a lot of water you pay to ship and store. And a liquid that gets cold can drop solids: Mississippi State notes one common liquid fertilizer "salts out (solids separate from the liquid) at 32°F" (0 °C), and that true solution fertilizers can be stored "as long as the temperature stays above freezing." Keep liquid nutrients in a heated room, not an unheated garage or shed over winter. If a bottle has crystals at the bottom, follow the maker's directions before using it; shaking a cloudy bottle does not prove the nutrients are back in solution.

## The real work with powder: weighing and dissolving

### Weigh, don't scoop

Masterblend publishes its rates in grams for set volumes and gives no spoon equivalents. Penn State notes that a high-precision scale is needed for micronutrients when you mix from single salts, and Purdue's mixing tips say to use accurate scales to weigh each material. A scoop is not a gram: granules pack differently from product to product.

The smallest dose decides which scale you need. In Masterblend's table, magnesium sulfate is **1.5 g per gallon**. A kitchen scale that reads in whole grams can be off by up to half a gram, which is about a third of that dose. At 5 gallons the dose is about 7 g (half the maker's 10-gallon row), so the same half-gram error shrinks to about 7%. A scale that reads to 0.1 g keeps the error near 3% even for a single gallon.

### Dissolve completely, one part at a time

Missouri Extension says to dissolve each formulation completely before combining. Purdue adds that lukewarm to hot water speeds dissolving, that stirring helps, and that it can "take a few hours for the solution to become clear". It also warns that a sludge often forms at the bottom of a stock tank from anti-caking and anti-dust additives, which needs rinsing out from time to time.

**Where sources differ:** Purdue's warm water is for dissolving concentrates. For the root zone, Oklahoma State calls 72–75 °F (22–24 °C) optimal and UF/IFAS gives 65–80 °F (18–27 °C). Dissolve powder in a little warm water if it helps, then dilute it into the full reservoir and let the solution come back into that range before roots go in.

The full order (water first, base fertilizer, then magnesium sulfate, calcium nitrate last) is in [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/).

## Both formats need separate parts

Neither format lets you put everything in one concentrated container. Missouri Extension explains that calcium reacts with phosphates and sulfates to form insoluble solids roots cannot use. Purdue lists the two usual culprits: calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate (gypsum), formed when calcium nitrate meets phosphorus or sulfate fertilizers. Ohio State's tomato fact sheet says the calcium portion "must be separated from the other macronutrients" at 100× strength.

So a dry program comes in separate bags, and a liquid program comes in separate bottles. General Hydroponics tells users of its three-part liquid: "Never combine concentrated FloraSeries components together undiluted", and add them one at a time to fresh water. Masterblend's dry mix is added in order with each part dissolved before the next. The rule is the same for both.

## Turning powder into your own liquid stock

You do not have to choose one format forever. Many growers buy powder and make their own concentrated liquid stocks, which gives powder's nutrient-per-package with liquid's easy dosing. Penn State says stock concentrations of 50, 100 or 200 times normal strength are common, kept in two tanks: calcium nitrate in one, 4-18-38 plus magnesium sulfate in the other. Purdue gives a commercial version: calcium, iron and half the potassium nitrate in one tank, everything else in the other, with most small greenhouse operations using 25–50 gallon (95–190 L) stock tanks.

A quick solubility check shows a home stock is easy for calcium nitrate. At 100× the Masterblend rate, a gallon of stock holds 240 g of calcium nitrate, about 63 g per liter, far below Penn State's figure of about 121 g per 100 mL (HydroRigor's arithmetic). Masterblend does not publish stock-solution rates or a solubility figure for its 4-18-38, so test a small jar first and watch for crystals.

Purdue's storage advice for stocks: keep the solution in a dark place and protect iron chelates from daylight. It also notes that stock solutions need a pH below 5 for everything to stay dissolved, and that chelates break down at pH 3.5 or lower. Label each tank with its contents and strength, and never pour one tank into the other.

## Step-by-step: switching formats or starting fresh

1. **Pick by batch size.** Liquid for one small reservoir; powder for 5 gallons or more, or if you will make stocks.
2. **Read the whole label.** Note how many parts, the rate (grams or mL per gallon), the order, and whether calcium and magnesium are included. Penn State notes a complete soluble fertilizer may not include them, and Purdue notes some blends assume calcium-rich water.
3. **Check your water.** Missouri gives source-water limits such as EC 0.2–0.8 dS/m, alkalinity 40–160 ppm and sodium below 50 ppm. Format does not change what is already in the tap.
4. **Get the right tool.** A 0.1 g scale for powder; a syringe or graduated cylinder for liquid.
5. **Fill the reservoir to its final volume** and note the starting EC.
6. **Add each part separately**, dissolving or stirring in fully before the next, calcium last.
7. **Wait a couple of minutes and read EC** (Oklahoma State). Aim for the crop: lettuce 1.2–1.8 mS/cm (UF/IFAS), fruiting crops 2.0–3.0 for much of the season (Tennessee).
8. **Adjust pH after EC**, slowly, waiting several minutes between additions (Oklahoma State).
9. **Write down** product, rate, EC and pH, so you can compare formats fairly if you switch.
10. **Store the rest:** powder sealed and dry; liquid and stocks dark and above freezing.

For liquid label rates in mL per gallon, the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales each part to your reservoir. It does not convert powder grams to milliliters; do not guess a density. ## Troubleshooting powder and liquid nutrients

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Powder will not fully dissolve | Cold water or too strong a mix | Use lukewarm water, stir longer, add more water | Purdue; Penn State |
| Sludge in the stock tank | Anti-caking and anti-dust additives settling | Rinse the tank out periodically | Purdue |
| White solids after mixing | Calcium met concentrated phosphate or sulfate | Mix fresh; add each part to the full volume of water | Missouri G6984; Purdue |
| Crystals in a liquid bottle | Stored cold; solids salted out | Store above 32 °F (0 °C); follow the maker's directions | Mississippi State P1466 |
| EC lower than expected | Underdosed, or powder not fully dissolved | Re-weigh with a finer scale; dissolve fully; re-measure | Oklahoma State; Penn State |
| EC right but plants show deficiency | EC shows the total, not the balance | Check every part went in, in order | Missouri G6984 |
| Stock kept in light, or system just disinfected | Iron chelates break down with daylight, UV or ozone | Keep stocks dark; replace chelates after disinfection | Purdue |
| Readings drift between batches | Uncalibrated meter | Calibrate (pH 4.01 and 7.01; EC 1.41 mS/cm) | Michigan State |

## Frequently asked questions

### Is powder or liquid hydroponic nutrient better?

Neither, for the plant. Mississippi State Extension finds no agronomic difference between liquid and dry fertilizers at the same nutrient rate. Choose by how you measure and how much you mix: liquid for small reservoirs, powder for larger batches with an accurate scale.

### Is powder cheaper than liquid?

Powder carries no water, so it holds more nutrient per package (Mississippi State; Penn State). No source used here compares the cost per gallon of solution, and HydroRigor does not publish prices; compare the labels' rates per gallon against what you pay.

### Can I use a teaspoon to measure powder nutrients?

The manufacturer's table used here gives grams only, with no spoon measures, and Purdue says to use accurate scales. For a 1-gallon batch, a 0.1 g scale keeps error near 3%; a whole-gram scale can be off by a third (HydroRigor's arithmetic).

### Do liquid nutrients go bad?

The sources here give no shelf life for hydroponic liquids. Mississippi State says true solution fertilizers store as long as they stay above freezing, and Purdue says to keep stock solutions dark and protect iron chelates from light.

### Can I turn powder nutrients into a liquid?

Yes. Penn State describes 50×, 100× or 200× stocks in two tanks, with calcium kept apart from phosphate and sulfate. Masterblend does not publish stock rates, so start with a small test jar.

### Do I still need to check EC and pH with liquid nutrients?

Yes. Missouri Extension notes EC shows the total salts, and Oklahoma State says to set EC first, then pH, for any solution. More on targets is in the [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) and the [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/); for one documented powder program, see the [Masterblend guide](/reviews/masterblend-hydroponic-nutrients/), and for choosing a product, [best hydroponic nutrients for home growers](/best/hydroponic-nutrients-for-home-growers/).
