Before you buy any hydroponic system for sale, check four numbers the listing should let you work out: **light** (lettuce needs a daily light integral, or DLI, of **12–17 mol/m²/day**, University of Kentucky; tomatoes and strawberries need **more than 20**, Purdue), **real plant capacity** (about **3–4 lettuce plants per square foot**, not the number of holes), **water per plant** (about **1–1.5 gallons per lettuce plant** over the crop in a passive system, UF/IFAS) and **what you will still have to buy**, above all a pH and EC meter, because greens grow best at about **pH 5.5–6.2** and lettuce at **EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm**. Then match the system type to your crop: water culture (Kratky, DWC, NFT) for lettuce, greens and herbs, and buckets of perlite on drip for tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers (Oregon State; Virginia Tech).

Listings sell plant sites, apps and lights; they rarely say how much light reaches the plants, what happens in a power cut, or what else you need on day one. This guide turns a product page into those answers. HydroRigor has not tested any product and does not rank sellers; every figure comes from the sources listed at the end.

## Quick answer: what to check and where the numbers come from

| Check | Target | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light for lettuce | DLI 12–17 mol/m²/day | University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| Light for leafy greens | DLI 15–20; Virginia Tech's greens and herbs yielded more at 17 or above | Purdue HO-283-W; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Light for tomatoes and strawberries | DLI more than 20 mol/m²/day | Purdue HO-283-W |
| Lettuce capacity (DWC raft) | 3–4 plants per square foot, tank at least 12 in (30 cm) deep | University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| Tower capacity | 28 plants per tower; two towers in a 5 × 5 ft (1.5 × 1.5 m) area | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Water for a passive (Kratky) lettuce | 1–1.5 gal (about 4–6 L) per plant from seed to harvest | UF/IFAS HS1488 |
| Solution pH (leafy greens) | 5.5–6.0 (Kentucky) or 5.8–6.2 (Illinois); 5.5–6.2 covers both | University of Kentucky; University of Illinois |
| EC for lettuce | 1.2–1.8 mS/cm | UF/IFAS HS1422 |
| EC for fruiting crops | 2.0–3.0 mS/cm for much of the season | University of Tennessee W 844-C |
| Solution temperature | 65–80 °F (18–27 °C) | UF/IFAS HS1422 |
| Meter calibration | Two-point with pH 4.01 and 7.01 buffers; EC standard 1.41 mS/cm | Michigan State Extension |

Where sources disagree, the page shows both. The pH ranges overlap at 5.8–6.0, so a system run at 5.5–6.2 satisfies both. The light figures differ because Kentucky's 12–17 is a lettuce production target, Purdue's 15–20 covers leafy greens as a group, and Virginia Tech's 17 is the level above which its greens and herbs yielded more.

## 1. Match the system type to your crop

Nearly every system for sale is one of six root-zone designs. Oregon State Extension sums up the choice: "the best system depends on your crops and space". Find the design in the listing's photos or manual first.

| Design | Needs power for | Best crops | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kratky (passive) | Nothing | Lettuce, leafy greens | UF/IFAS HS1488 |
| Deep water culture | Air pump, all day | Lettuce, spinach, kale, herbs | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| NFT channels | Pump, constant flow | Lettuce, herbs, small greens | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Vertical tower | Pump | Lettuce, greens, herbs, strawberries | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Ebb and flow | Pump and timer | Seedlings, herbs, peppers, greens | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Drip buckets | Pump and timer | Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers | Oregon State EM 9456 |

In Virginia Tech's side-by-side trial, greens and herbs gave their best yields in NFT and DWC, while tomatoes and peppers did best in media and worst in NFT. A countertop "smart garden" is still one of these designs underneath, so find which one before comparing features; the [smart indoor hydroponic systems guide](/systems/smart-indoor-hydroponic-systems/) covers what its automation does. For a full walk-through of each type, see [indoor hydroponic systems for home](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/).

**Recommendation:** for a first purchase, buy for lettuce, greens or herbs. Those crops forgive more and need the least light. Buy a drip-bucket system only when tomatoes or peppers are the goal.

## 2. Ask what happens when the power goes off

Listings skip this check. Oregon State warns that NFT plants can wilt quickly when the pump or power fails and calls backups crucial. Towers and drip systems also depend on a pump to wet the roots. In DWC, University of Missouri Extension notes, roots still sit in water if the pump stops; they lose aeration, not water. A Kratky system needs no power at all (UF/IFAS).

If outages are common where you live, favor Kratky or DWC, or budget for a battery backup for the pump.

## 3. Work out how much light reaches the plants

Light sets how much you harvest indoors, and a window is rarely enough in winter. Purdue measured a greenhouse in West Lafayette, Indiana, at about 5 mol/m²/day in winter and below 10 from November through February. In Purdue's trial, 15 lettuce varieties grown for 28 days averaged 0.90 lb/m² of fresh weight at a DLI of 5.6 and 6.7 lb/m² at 19.8.

A listing's wattage tells you how much power the light draws, not how much light reaches the leaves. Look instead for **PPFD** (photosynthetic photon flux density, in µmol/m²/s) measured at a stated distance or across the growing area. With it you can work out the daily total yourself:

**DLI = PPFD × hours on × 0.0036**

The 0.0036 converts seconds to hours (3,600) and micromoles to moles (÷ 1,000,000). A light giving 200 µmol/m²/s for 16 hours delivers 11.5 mol/m²/day, just short of Kentucky's 12–17 for lettuce. No PPFD figure means the light is unknown, not adequate.

## 4. Count real capacity, not holes

Plant sites are the most misleading number on a listing. Kentucky spaces lettuce at 3–4 plants per square foot in a raft. Oklahoma State's tower table spaces head lettuce 12 in (30 cm) apart and thin-leaf lettuce 6–8 in (15–20 cm). So a unit with 2 square feet of lighted growing area holds about 6–8 lettuce plants at Kentucky's spacing, whatever its hole count.

Towers deserve a second look. In a peer-reviewed study, a vertical system grew more lettuce per unit of floor area than flat hydroponics, but light and plant weight both fell from the top of the column to its base (Touliatos and colleagues, 2016). A tower sold with a single overhead light will grow smaller plants at the bottom. The [hydroponic tower garden guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) covers lighting and spacing for towers.

## 5. Check the reservoir against the plant load

A reservoir that is small for its plant count drifts fast in pH and EC. The University of Tennessee notes that larger plants cause quicker imbalances and advises changing small recirculating tanks every one to two weeks; Kentucky tops up every few days and replaces lettuce solution every 3–4 weeks.

For passive systems the arithmetic is simple. UF/IFAS gives 1–1.5 gal (about 4–6 L) per lettuce plant from seed to harvest, so a Kratky unit sold for six lettuce plants needs about 6–9 gal (23–34 L) of solution over the crop. A smaller tank means topping up.

Also check these reservoir details:

- **Opaque walls and a tight lid.** Light reaching the solution feeds algae (University of Illinois; Oregon State EM 9455).
- **A drain or easy way to empty it.** You will empty and clean it between crops.
- **Food-grade containers.** Oregon State asks for food-grade buckets in drip systems.
- **A level, solid place for big tanks.** Oregon State says DWC tanks belong on the ground with the bottom level to within an inch.

## 6. List what is not in the box

Most systems for sale leave out at least some of what you need on the first day. The cost of a system is the box plus these extras.

Often extra: pH and EC meter, calibration buffers pH 4.01 and 7.01 and EC standard 1.41, hydroponic nutrients, pH up and down, starter plugs or media, a pump backup for NFT, and a grow light if none is included.](/images/guides/hydroponic-system-in-box-vs-extras.svg)

| Extra | Why you need it | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH and EC meter | Targets are pH 5.5–6.2 and EC 1.2–1.8 for lettuce; check daily at the same time if you can | Oklahoma State HLA-6722; UF/IFAS HS1422 |
| Calibration solutions | pH 4.01 and 7.01 buffers; EC standard 1.41 mS/cm (1413 µS/cm) | Michigan State Extension |

Some extras cost as much as a small system. UMass Extension gave 2016 prices of about $50–60 for a single pH or EC pen and about $80 for a combination pen; treat those as a dated guide, not today's price. Oklahoma State put a commercial vertical tower at around $500 or more in 2021.

**On meters:** Purdue's bulletin calls portable meters affordable and very accurate, and lists automatic calibration and temperature compensation as desirable features. UMass is blunt: "Calibration is a must to get any useful information from pens and meters!" A combined pen reads the two numbers you check most. None of these sources gives a required accuracy figure, so check the stated accuracy and whether the probe can be replaced. The [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/) explains what the readings mean.

## 7. Estimate running costs from the listing

You can estimate the electricity a system uses from its rated watts without any price feed:

**kWh per day = watts × hours on ÷ 1,000**

For example, a 40 W light run 16 hours a day uses 0.64 kWh a day, about 19 kWh in a 30-day month; multiply by the rate on your power bill. Add the pump's watts: around the clock for a DWC air pump, on its schedule for NFT, towers, ebb and flow and drip. Kratky needs only the light.

Consumables make up the rest: nutrients, pH adjusters, calibration solution, starter plugs, and media for bucket systems, which Oregon State says not to reuse.

## 8. Judge convenience claims by what they measure

"Smart", "automated" and "self-watering" describe features, not results. Ask what each claim measures and what the system does with it:

- A sensor that "monitors nutrients" should say whether it reads EC, pH or only water level.
- An app that "takes care of your plants" should say which decisions it controls: the light timer, the pump or nothing.
- A crop claim ("harvest in 3 weeks") should name the crop, the light level and the method, so you can compare it with Kentucky's 21–35 days from transplant to harvest for lettuce.

HydroRigor does not count star ratings, testimonials or seller copy as product evidence.

## 9. Buying used: what to check

Inspect a used system as you would a new one: design, pump, the light's PPFD, hoses and seals. Plan to empty, scrub and rinse the reservoir and lines before use, replace any old media (Oregon State advises against reusing it in bucket systems), and calibrate any included meter before trusting it (Michigan State; UMass). None of the sources here gives a disinfectant product or strength for home systems, so follow the cleaning directions from the system's maker.

## How to choose, step by step

1. **Pick the crop.** Lettuce, greens and herbs for water culture; tomatoes and peppers for drip buckets.
2. **Measure the space.** Floor area, height for mature plants and the light, and a spill-safe spot near an outlet.
3. **Shortlist by design.** Keep only listings whose root-zone design fits the crop and your power reliability.
4. **Run the three numbers.** DLI from PPFD and hours, capacity from lighted area, solution volume per plant.
5. **Price the extras.** Meter, calibration solutions, nutrients, pH adjusters, media and a light if needed.
6. **Estimate electricity.** Watts × hours ÷ 1,000 for each powered part.
7. **Check service.** A drain, reachable roots and tubing, replaceable pumps and parts, and a return window long enough to test a full fill.
8. **After it arrives, run it with water only for a day** to find leaks and check pumps and timers, then mix nutrients and set EC first and pH second (Oklahoma State). The [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) give doses and crop numbers.

## Troubleshooting a new system

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pale, stretched seedlings | Too little light | Lower or add a light; aim for DLI 12–17 for lettuce | University of Kentucky; Purdue |
| Bottom tower plants smaller | Light falls toward the base | Add side lighting; put light-hungry crops at the top | Touliatos et al. 2016 |
| Plants wilt after a power cut | NFT or tower roots dried | Add a pump backup or choose DWC or Kratky | Oregon State EM 9457; University of Missouri |
| Green film in the reservoir | Light reaching the solution | Cover clear walls; close gaps in the lid | University of Illinois; Oregon State EM 9455 |
| pH and EC swing every few days | Small tank for the plant load | Top up more often; change small tanks every 1–2 weeks | University of Tennessee W 844-C |
| Brown, slimy roots | Warm, poorly aerated solution | Keep solution 65–80 °F (18–27 °C) and aeration running | UF/IFAS HS1422; Virginia Tech |
| Meter readings jump around | Uncalibrated or dried probe | Soak a dry probe in storage solution at least an hour, then calibrate | Michigan State Extension |
| Lettuce bolts or tipburns | Room too warm | Days at or below 75 °F (24 °C), nights at or below 65 °F (18 °C) | University of Kentucky |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best hydroponic system for sale for beginners?

One built for lettuce, greens or herbs. A Kratky unit needs no pumps or electricity (UF/IFAS) and a small DWC tub keeps roots wet if the pump stops (Missouri). [Hydroponic systems for beginners](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/) and the [best hydroponic system for home growers](/best/hydroponic-system-for-home-growers/) compare them by crop.

### Do hydroponic systems for sale include everything I need?

Rarely. Expect to add at least a pH and EC meter with calibration solutions, a hydroponic nutrient and pH adjusters, and often starter plugs and a better light. See [indoor hydroponic system kits](/best/indoor-hydroponic-system-kits/) for what kits tend to include.

### How much light should a system's grow light give?

Enough for a DLI of 12–17 mol/m²/day for lettuce (Kentucky) or more than 20 for tomatoes and strawberries (Purdue). Multiply the PPFD at plant height by hours on and 0.0036 to check.

### How many plants will a system really grow?

Count lighted area, not holes: about 3–4 lettuce plants per square foot (Kentucky). Oklahoma State gives 28 plants for a full-size tower.

### Can I grow tomatoes in a system sold for herbs?

Usually not well. Oregon State recommends buckets of perlite on drip for tomatoes, which need a DLI above 20 (Purdue) and EC around 2.0–3.0 (Tennessee). See [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).

### Is it cheaper to build a system?

Usually, and a build teaches the system. The [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) has three builds, including a [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tote.
