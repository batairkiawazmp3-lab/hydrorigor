Hydroponic basil needs a solution at **pH 5.5–6.0** and **EC 1.0–1.6 mS/cm** (Oklahoma State Extension), as much light and warmth as you can give it, and room to branch. Transplant seedlings at about **3 weeks** and you can start picking leaves from about **6 weeks after sowing** (University of Florida). In that Florida study of small home-style units, four basil plants in a 2-gallon aerated tub produced **344–359 g** of fresh basil in 8 weeks in an indoor room, and about four times as much in a brighter, warmer greenhouse.

Every number below is tied to a university or research source listed at the end. Where the sources say nothing, this guide says so.

## What a small home-style system produced

The University of Florida team grew basil the way a home grower might: four plants in a 7.6-liter (about 2-gallon) opaque tub with a lid holding four net cups, and an air pump giving continuous aeration. Seedlings went in 22 days after sowing and grew for 8 weeks. One set grew in an indoor room at a constant 21 °C (70 °F) with a DLI of 9 mol/m²/day; the other in a greenhouse at 29 °C (84 °F) by day and 24 °C (75 °F) by night, with a DLI averaging 26.1.

![Bar chart of fresh basil per four-plant, 2-gallon unit after 8 weeks. Indoors at DLI 9 and a constant 21 °C (70 °F): 344 g when the solution was replaced and 359 g when it was only topped up. Greenhouse at DLI 26.1, 29 °C (84 °F) day and 24 °C (75 °F) night: 1,417 g replaced and 1,452 g topped up. About four times the basil with more light and warmth; topping up or replacing made no difference.](/images/guides/hydroponic-basil-light-yield.svg)

*Fresh basil per four-plant unit after 8 weeks (Solis-Toapanta, Fisher and Gómez 2020, University of Florida).*

Two results matter for a home grower. First, light and warmth drive yield: the greenhouse plants produced about four times as much. Light and temperature both differed between the two sites, so the study cannot say how much of the gap each one caused. Second, how you manage the solution mattered little: replacing it and simply topping it up gave the same growth in both places. The authors conclude that indoor gardeners could grow over 340 g of basil per unit under similar conditions without ever replacing the solution.

## Which system to use

- **A small aerated tub (deep water culture).** This is what the Florida study used, and it is the simplest proven option here: an opaque container, a lid with net cups and an air pump that runs all the time. The [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) covers the build and oxygen targets.
- **NFT channels.** Oregon State lists herbs among the crops suited to nutrient film technique, which works well for rows of small plants on a rack. To grow basil alongside other herbs in one system, see the [best herbs for hydroponics](/crops/best-herbs-for-hydroponics/). Compare it with other systems in the [best hydroponic system for home growers](/best/hydroponic-system-for-home-growers/).
- **A Kratky jar or bucket (no pump).** None of the sources here tested basil in a non-aerated Kratky container; the University of Florida Kratky guidance used on this site is for lettuce. It may work, but this guide has no numbers for it. The [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) shows both a Kratky bucket and an aerated tote.
- **A countertop smart garden.** Most are small units with a reservoir and a built-in light. The same targets apply; check that the light is strong enough (see below). [Six indoor system types compared](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) explains what to look for.

## Light and temperature

**Light is the biggest lever.** In a 2023 study of sweet basil, the number of branches rose as the DLI increased from 5.5 to 13.2 mol/m²/day (Walters, Tarr and Lopez). In the Iowa State spacing work, raising the DLI from about 6 to 15 added 0.3–1.6 oz of basil per square foot, depending on spacing (Walters 2015). Indoors under a grow light, that means a brighter light or more hours of it gives you more basil. The Iowa State study ran its lights 16 hours a day; that was a study condition, not a tested optimum. The [indoor systems guide](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) explains how to work out a DLI from your light's rating.

**Basil likes it warm.** Walters and colleagues cite a base temperature of about 11 °C (52 °F), below which basil barely grows. In their model, sweet basil put on the most fresh weight at a mean daily temperature of 32.6–35.5 °C (about 91–96 °F), and the best temperature rose as light increased from DLI 6 to 11. Few homes run that hot, and they do not need to: the Florida indoor plants grew 344–359 g per unit at a constant 21 °C (70 °F). The Iowa State team kept the solution at 72 °F. In practice, keep basil out of cold rooms and away from cold windows, and put your warmest spot under your brightest light.

## Spacing: how close to plant

The Iowa State study grew sweet basil in deep-flow hydroponics at five spacings (Walters 2015):

| Spacing | Plants per sq ft |
| --- | --- |
| 4 in | 9.3 |
| 6 in | 4.1 |
| 8 in | 2.3 |
| 10 in | 1.4 |
| 12 in | 1.0 |

Spacing did not change how much each plant weighed, so closer spacing simply gave more basil per square foot. The author's recommendation: for a single harvest under lights, plant 4 inches apart. That is likely too dense if you plan to keep cutting the same plants for weeks, so give long-lived plants more room. The Florida study's tub held four plants in a footprint of about 9 by 9 inches.

## Feeding: pH, EC and when to change the solution

**Targets.** Oklahoma State lists basil at **EC 1.0–1.6 mS/cm** and **pH 5.5–6.0**. The Florida team adjusted pH to 5.5–6.5 after each fertilizer addition, and the Iowa State study ran at pH 6 and EC 1.6, so all three sit close together. Mix to the middle of the Oklahoma State range with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/), and see the [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/) for how pH and EC work.

**Where the sources differ.** The Florida paper notes that basil tolerates EC anywhere from 0.5 to 4.0 dS/m (1 dS/m equals 1 mS/cm) and that commercial growers often aim for 0.5–1.6. In its own indoor tubs, topping up without replacing let EC climb to 5.5 dS/m by week 6, with no loss of growth over 8 weeks. That is one study; Oklahoma State's 1.0–1.6 remains the safer target, and a rising EC is a sign to feed less, not a crisis.

**Top up or replace?** Commercial guidelines often say to replace the solution every one to two weeks. The Florida study found no difference in growth between replacing it and topping it up, and the authors suggest that frequent replacement may be unnecessary for indoor gardeners. If you top up, reduce the fertilizer you add indoors so EC does not keep rising.

## Step by step: basil in a 2-gallon aerated tub

1. **Sow the seed.** Start seedlings in a tray or plugs; the Florida team transplanted them 22 days after sowing.
2. **Set up the tub.** Use an opaque container of about 2 gallons with a lid that holds four net cups, plus an air pump and air line or stone that runs all the time.
3. **Mix the solution.** Aim for EC 1.0–1.6 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0. Check both against the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).
4. **Transplant.** Set four seedlings into the net cups at about 3 weeks old.
5. **Give it light.** As bright as you can manage; more light means more branches and more basil.
6. **Top up, do not panic.** Add water and nutrient as the level falls, re-check pH after each feed, and use less fertilizer if EC keeps climbing.
7. **Pinch and pick.** Start picking leaves from about 6 weeks after sowing. The Florida team pinched the plants 4 and 6 weeks after transplant.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small, slow plants indoors | Not enough light | Add light; indoors at DLI 9 gave about a quarter of the greenhouse yield | Solis-Toapanta et al. 2020 |
| Few side branches | Low light | Raise the DLI; branches rose up to DLI 13.2 | Walters et al. 2023 |
| Plants barely grow | Room too cold | Keep basil well above 11 °C (52 °F) | Walters et al. 2023 |
| EC keeps climbing | Topping up with full-strength fertilizer | Feed less when topping up | Solis-Toapanta et al. 2020 |
| pH drifts after feeding | Fertilizer additions shift pH | Re-check after each addition; hold 5.5–6.0 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Solis-Toapanta et al. 2020 |
| Little basil per square foot | Plants spaced wide for one harvest | Plant 4 in apart for a single harvest | Walters 2015 |

The sources used here do not cover basil diseases such as downy mildew, so this guide gives no disease targets or treatments.

## Frequently asked questions

### What pH does hydroponic basil need?

Oklahoma State gives pH 5.5–6.0. The University of Florida study adjusted pH to 5.5–6.5 after feeding, and the Iowa State study ran at pH 6, so anywhere in the 5.5–6.0 band is a sound target.

### What EC is best for basil?

EC 1.0–1.6 mS/cm (Oklahoma State). Basil tolerates a wider range, 0.5–4.0 in studies cited by the Florida team, so small drifts are not a problem.

### How long until I can harvest hydroponic basil?

The Florida authors say indoor gardeners could start picking leaves as early as 6 weeks after sowing. A 2023 study of sweet basil reached marketable size about three weeks after transplant.

### How much light does basil need?

The sources give no single minimum. More light gave more branches up to a DLI of 13.2 mol/m²/day and more basil per square foot at DLI 15 than at 6. Indoors at DLI 9, four plants still produced 344–359 g in 8 weeks.

### Do I need to change the water?

Not necessarily. In the Florida study, topping up the solution grew as much basil as replacing it. Feed less when you top up so EC does not keep rising.

### Can I grow basil with the Kratky method?

None of the sources here tested it, so this guide has no numbers for it. The study that did grow basil in small home-style units used an air pump. For lettuce, see the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/), where Kratky is well documented.
