**Dutch bucket hydroponics (also called Bato buckets) grows one large plant, or a pair, in each bucket of perlite or another coarse medium, fed by drip lines from a shared reservoir, with the extra solution draining back to the tank.** In Virginia Tech's trial each bucket had **two drip stakes delivering 0.5 gal (1.9 L) per hour each**. Run the drip so about **10–15% of what goes in drains out** for tomatoes (about 20% for cucumbers), which works out to roughly **2–5 pints (1–2.4 L) per full-grown tomato plant per day** (UF/IFAS). For tomatoes keep the solution at **pH 5.8–6.0** and **EC 2.0–3.0 mS/cm** once plants are fruiting.

Oregon State's bucket publication (EM 9456) describes the system and its crops: tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, zucchini, eggplant, pole beans and small melons. Its advantage over channel systems is root room; Oregon State says buckets give **a larger rooting volume and greater buffering capacity than NFT**, and in Virginia Tech's trial tomatoes and peppers yielded best in media and worst in NFT. Its weak points are clogged drip lines and outlets, and a pump that has to keep running.

HydroRigor has not built or tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## How a Dutch bucket system works

Each plant grows in its own bucket of coarse, free-draining media. A pump in the reservoir sends solution through a drip line to emitters in every bucket. The solution soaks through the media, and whatever the roots don't hold drains out of an outlet near the bottom into a shared drain line that runs back to the reservoir. Kentucky's illustrated overview shows the buckets sitting on a common gutter with holes at the bottom, and Missouri Extension calls the setup a **closed, recirculating system**. Oregon State's diagram shows the same loop: reservoir to buckets and back again.

The outlet is the part that makes a Dutch bucket a Dutch bucket. Oregon State notes that outlets **can be raised above the bottom of the bucket** to leave a small pool of solution in the base; purpose-made buckets do this with a short elbow or standpipe. That pool is a safety margin if a drip cycle is missed. Oregon State gives no height for it, and neither do our other sources.

Because the roots sit in media rather than water, no air pump is needed. Oregon State says porous media such as perlite, fed by drip, supply oxygen to the roots **without extra aeration equipment**, which is the main difference from [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

Missouri sorts hydroponic systems into water-based and media-based, and names Dutch buckets as the system for tomatoes, cucumbers and vining crops. See our [types of hydroponic systems guide](/systems/types-of-hydroponic-systems/) for how it compares with the other seven.

## Drip rate and timing: how much water each bucket needs

This is the question most Dutch bucket pages skip. None of our sources gives a minutes-per-cycle schedule for home buckets, but three figures pin it down.

**1. Flow.** Virginia Tech's trial used **two drip stakes per bucket at 0.5 gal (1.9 L) per hour each**, so each bucket received 1 gal (3.8 L) per hour while the pump ran. UF/IFAS advises a **fairly high flow rate**, which shortens each run and reduces clogging, and emitter openings of **at least 0.05 in (1.3 mm)**.

**2. Daily water.** UF/IFAS gives a full-grown tomato plant in perlite **2–3 pints (0.9–1.4 L) a day in winter and 3–5 pints (1.4–2.4 L) on warm spring days**, including the drain. Alabama Extension gives **about 2 quarts (1.9 L) per plant per day**, which sits within the warm-day range. Young plants use far less, but UF/IFAS still irrigates them **at least three times a day** with enough to wet the root ball.

**3. Drain fraction.** UF/IFAS says to leach **about 10–15% of each irrigation for tomato** and **about 20% for cucumber**, because cucumbers tolerate salts in the root zone less well. In a recirculating Dutch bucket system that drain goes back to the tank rather than to waste, so it costs nothing; its job is to keep salts from building up in the media.

![Bar chart of water per full-grown tomato plant per day, in pints. UF/IFAS winter: 2 to 3 pints, 0.9 to 1.4 liters. Alabama Extension: about 2 quarts, which is 4 pints or 1.9 liters. UF/IFAS warm spring days: 3 to 5 pints, 1.4 to 2.4 liters. At two stakes of 0.5 gallons per hour, 4 pints takes about 30 minutes of drip a day, split into several short runs.](/images/guides/dutch-bucket-water-per-plant.svg)

*Daily water per full-grown tomato plant from UF/IFAS HS169 and Alabama ANR-2955. The run-time line is HydroRigor arithmetic, not a source figure.*

**Putting it together (our arithmetic).** At 1 gal (3.8 L) per hour per bucket, the pump delivers about 2 fl oz (63 mL) a minute to each bucket. A full-grown tomato drinking 4 pints (½ gal, 1.9 L) a day, plus a 15% drain, needs a little over half a gallon: about **35 minutes of drip a day**. Split that into several short runs through the light period rather than one long one, so the media stays evenly moist. Start young plants with three short runs a day, as UF/IFAS does, and lengthen or add runs as the plants grow and the weather warms.

**Check, don't guess.** UF/IFAS's method: time how much one emitter delivers in 15 seconds, check that emitters are within **10–15% of each other**, and catch the drain from a bucket during a run. If less than 10% comes out, run longer; if far more than 15–20% drains, shorten the runs. UF/IFAS's "slap test" also works on buckets: a bucket that feels light is short of water or has a clogged emitter.

## Buckets, media and spacing

| Part | What to use | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bucket | Food-grade plastic such as HDPE, never one that held paint or pesticide; avoid polystyrene and basic PVC | Chemicals and breakdown | Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Outlet | A high-quality screen on every outlet, raised slightly off the floor | Stops media clogging the drain; leaves a small reserve | Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Media | Perlite (most common), expanded clay, coir, washed river rock or gravel | Coarse and porous, so roots get air | Oregon State EM 9456; UF/IFAS blog 2025 |
| Emitters | Two stakes or pressure-compensating emitters, opening 0.05 in or more | Even flow; fewer clogs | Virginia Tech SPES-466; UF/IFAS HS169 |
| Filter | About 200 mesh on the supply line | Keeps particles out of the emitters | UF/IFAS HS169 |
| Support | A string to an overhead wire or a stake for each plant | Vining crops need it | Oregon State EM 9456; Missouri Extension |

**Bucket size.** None of our sources gives a bucket volume for Dutch buckets; Oregon State says various household containers and bucket sizes work. For comparison only, Utah State suggests 2–3 gallon (8–11 L) pots for the larger indoor tomato varieties grown in pots, and says a pot too large is better than one too small.

**Which medium?** UF/IFAS found **negligible differences between perlite grades**: coarse and medium horticultural perlite grow tomatoes and cucumbers equally well. A 2022 cucumber trial in Dutch buckets (Yang, Altland and Samarakoon) found medium-grade **pine bark gave 23.9% more yield than perlite**, coarse bark matched perlite, and bark, coir and peat drained 18–38% less solution than perlite because they hold more water. So perlite is the safe default, and a water-holding medium such as coir or bark needs shorter or fewer runs. Oregon State notes each bucket can even hold a different medium for a different crop.

**Don't reuse media.** Oregon State calls recycling and reusing materials **the primary cause of disease spread and pest problems in home systems**, and UF/IFAS calls reusing unsterilized perlite risky because old roots may carry disease and make the media hold more water.

**Spacing.** For single-stem tomatoes, a University of Tennessee trial of 'Trust' found **16–20 in (41–51 cm) between plants in the row, 18 in (46 cm) ideal**, with rows 4 ft (1.2 m) apart; closer spacing raised disease and pest risk. Alabama Extension gives **3.5–5 sq ft (0.33–0.46 m²) per plant**: 16 in in the row and 5 ft (1.5 m) between rows. If you put two plants in one bucket, give the bucket twice that floor area (our arithmetic). Our sources give no Dutch bucket spacing for peppers or cucumbers.

## The nutrient solution: pH, EC and temperature

| Crop | pH | EC (mS/cm) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tomato (fruiting) | 5.5–6.0; 5.6–5.8; 5.8–6.2; 6.0–6.5 | 2.0–3.0; 2.0–2.5; 2.0–4.0 | Tennessee; Mississippi State; UF/IFAS HS796; Oklahoma State |
| Tomato seedlings | — | 0.5–1.0, then 1.0–1.5 at transplant | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Cucumber | 5.0–5.5 | 1.7–2.0 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Pepper | 5.5–6.0 | 0.8–1.8 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Eggplant | 6.0 | 2.5–3.5 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Zucchini | 6.0 | 1.8–2.4 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Bean | 6.0 | 2.0–4.0 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |

**pH: aim for 5.8–6.0 for tomatoes.** The four tomato ranges disagree at the edges: Tennessee 5.5–6.0, Mississippi State 5.6–5.8 for media systems, UF/IFAS 5.8–6.2 and Oklahoma State 6.0–6.5. The span from 5.8 to 6.0 sits inside three of them, so it is the safest target. A tank shared by tomatoes and peppers fits there too; cucumbers prefer lower (Oklahoma State 5.0–5.5), so give them their own reservoir if you can.

**EC: 2.0–3.0 once fruiting.** Tennessee runs seedlings at 0.5–1.0, raises the solution to 1.0–1.5 at transplant, then holds **2.0–3.0 for much of the season**; Alabama gives 2.0–2.5 and Oklahoma State 2.0–4.0. Note the pepper conflict: Oklahoma State lists peppers at 0.8–1.8, while Tennessee puts tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers together at 2.0–3.0 for small soilless systems. If peppers share a tank with tomatoes, start near 2.0 and watch for leaf-edge burn. For peppers on their own, see the [hydroponic peppers guide](/crops/hydroponic-peppers/). UF/IFAS adds a check on the media: with a 2.0 EC solution, the media should read no more than **2.5–2.8**; if it climbs, drain more.

**Temperature and replacing.** Virginia Tech gives tomatoes **80 °F (27 °C) days and 60 °F (16 °C) nights**; Missouri gives warm-season crops 70–80 °F (21–27 °C) by day and 60–70 °F (16–21 °C) at night. UF/IFAS warns that wilting, iron deficiency and slow growth appear when the **media falls below 65 °F (18 °C)**; raise buckets 2–3 in (5–8 cm) off a cold floor. Tennessee says to change small recirculating tanks **every one to two weeks**, because large fruiting plants shift the balance quickly. The [tomato nutrients by stage guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-tomatoes/) gives ppm targets, and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) works out amounts for a target EC.

## Which crops suit Dutch buckets

| Crop | Fit | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Indeterminate tomatoes | Best | Long season on one stem; best tomato yields in media in the trial | Oregon State EM 9456; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Cucumbers | Best | Vining, needs root room; drain about 20% | Oregon State EM 9456; UF/IFAS HS169 |
| Bell and hot peppers | Good | Best pepper yields in media in the trial | Oregon State EM 9456; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Eggplant, zucchini | Good | Bushy plants that need root volume | Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Pole beans, small melons | Good | With a trellis or support | Oregon State EM 9456; Missouri Extension |
| Lettuce and herbs | Poor fit | Small plants grow better in NFT or DWC | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |

Oregon State says buckets are best for large plants because, unlike towers and channels, they cannot be stacked. For tomatoes, Purdue puts the light need at a **daily light integral above 20 mol/m²/day**, more than leafy greens, so indoor Dutch buckets need a strong light. Tennessee gives about **7–10 weeks from transplant to harvest** and 90–120 days from seed to first ripe fruit; the Tennessee 'Trust' trial began picking about 10 weeks after transplant. The [hydroponic tomatoes guide](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/) covers pruning and pollination, and the [tomato seeds guide](/crops/hydroponic-tomato-seeds/) helps you pick a variety that fits.

## How to set up Dutch buckets, step by step

1. **Choose the place.** Put the buckets on a level bench or blocks, a few inches above the floor (UF/IFAS), with the reservoir lower so the return drains by gravity. Plan 16–20 in between tomato plants (Tennessee) and an overhead wire for strings.
2. **Prepare the buckets.** Use food-grade buckets (Oregon State). Fit a screened outlet near the bottom of each, slightly raised to leave a small pool.
3. **Run the return line.** Connect every outlet to a shared drain line sloping back to an opaque, covered reservoir (Kentucky CCD-SP-20).
4. **Fit the pump, filter and drip line.** Submersible pump in the tank, a filter of about 200 mesh, then a supply line with two stakes or emitters per bucket (Virginia Tech; UF/IFAS).
5. **Fill with fresh media.** Perlite is the usual choice; never reuse media from an earlier crop (Oregon State; UF/IFAS).
6. **Test with plain water.** Time an emitter for 15 seconds, check emitters are within 10–15% of each other, and make sure every bucket drains (UF/IFAS).
7. **Mix the solution.** EC 1.0–1.5 at transplant (Tennessee), pH 5.8–6.0.
8. **Plant.** Set a transplant in each bucket and tie it to its string or stake.
9. **Set the timer.** Start with three short runs a day for young plants (UF/IFAS). Add runs as plants grow, aiming for 10–15% drain.
10. **Run the season.** Raise EC to 2.0–3.0 as fruit sets, check pH and EC regularly, change the tank every 1–2 weeks, and check drain from one bucket each week.

| Pump | Water pump on a timer | Water pump on a timer | Water pump, always on | Air pump, always on |

| Main risk | Clogged emitters or outlets | Missed floods | Pump stops, roots dry | Air pump stops |
| Source | Oregon State EM 9456 | Oregon State EM 9458 | Missouri G6986 | Missouri G6986 |

Choose Dutch buckets for big fruiting plants; for run-to-waste bags and slabs and how to read the drain, see the [drip hydroponic system guide](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/). For leafy greens, [NFT](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) and deep water culture use space better; for seedlings and mixed pots, use [ebb and flow](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/). Missouri Extension's beginners guide notes that in any pumped system **no power means no water**, and crops can wilt quickly, so check the pump and timer every day.

## Troubleshooting Dutch buckets

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One bucket dry, plant wilting | Clogged emitter | Clean or replace it; filter about 200 mesh | UF/IFAS HS169 |
| Bucket stays flooded | Media in the outlet | Fit a screen on every outlet | Oregon State EM 9456 |
| White crust on media; leaf edges burn | Salts building up | Increase drain to 10–20%; check media EC | UF/IFAS HS169; UF/IFAS blog 2025 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a Dutch bucket hydroponic system?

A recirculating system in which each large plant grows in its own bucket of media, fed by drip lines from a reservoir, with the extra solution draining back to the tank (Oregon State EM 9456; Missouri Extension). It is also called a Bato bucket system.

### How often should Dutch buckets be watered?

Our sources give no fixed schedule. UF/IFAS irrigates young plants at least three times a day and gives a full-grown tomato 2–5 pints (1–2.4 L) a day, adjusted so 10–15% drains out each time. Add runs as plants grow and the weather warms.

### What is the best medium for Dutch buckets?

Perlite is the standard, and UF/IFAS found grade makes little difference. Oregon State also lists expanded clay, coir, washed river rock and gravel; in a 2022 cucumber trial, medium pine bark yielded 23.9% more than perlite.

### What drip rate should I use?

Virginia Tech's trial used two drip stakes per bucket at 0.5 gal (1.9 L) per hour each. UF/IFAS recommends a fairly high flow rate and emitter openings of at least 0.05 in (1.3 mm).

### Do Dutch buckets need an air pump?

No. Oregon State says porous media fed by drip supply oxygen to the roots without extra aeration equipment.

### How far apart should Dutch buckets be?

For single-stem tomatoes, 16–20 in (41–51 cm) between plants, 18 in ideal, in a University of Tennessee trial; Alabama gives 3.5–5 sq ft (0.33–0.46 m²) per plant.

### Can I reuse perlite in Dutch buckets?

Oregon State advises against reusing media, calling it the main cause of disease and pest problems in home systems, and UF/IFAS calls reusing unsterilized perlite risky.
