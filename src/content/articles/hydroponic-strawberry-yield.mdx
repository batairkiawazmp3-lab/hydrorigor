In university trials, a hydroponic strawberry plant gave about **0.2–0.5 kg (½–1 lb) of fruit** over a five- to six-month harvest when it was a day-neutral variety under good light. The full range was wider: **0.12 kg** for the weakest cultivar in a University of Nebraska winter greenhouse and **0.74 kg (about 1⅝ lb)** for the best. A University of Illinois tunnel crop gave 0.48–0.76 lb per plant, and a Cornell trial under LED light gave the equivalent of about 340–530 g per plant.

The biggest levers, in order of the effect the trials measured: **day length** (16-hour days gave 372–989% more fruit than 12-hour days for 'Albion'), **variety** (two- to threefold gaps between cultivars in the same room), **light in a dim greenhouse** (LED supplement roughly doubled 'Albion'), **growing in substrate rather than water** and **keeping the air under 86 °F (30 °C)**.

Nebraska is the most useful single source: 13 cultivars grew side by side. In 2010–11, fruit per plant ran from **0.12 kg** ('Darselect') and **0.13 kg** ('Honeoye') to **0.46 kg** ('Chandler'), with berries per plant from 5 to 53. Two lots of 'Albion' plants in the same trial gave 0.43 and 0.21 kg, a reminder that plant stock matters as well as the cultivar name.

**What a home grower can plan on.** Take 0.2–0.5 kg per plant over about 22 weeks of picking. That is roughly 9–23 g per plant per week on average (our arithmetic), so a pound (454 g) a week takes somewhere between 20 and 50 plants. Weekly picks are uneven: Nebraska plants peaked at about 70–98 g per plant in their best weeks, and the Illinois crop fell off from late May to mid-June before climbing again from mid-August.

**About the Delaware estimate.** The SARE report projects about 4 lb of marketable fruit per plant over a 10-month cycle. It is an estimate, not a harvest: heat cut the measured crop to one month, and no trial we read picked that much from one plant.

## Factor 1: variety

![Card of the factors that changed strawberry yield in trials: 16-hour days 372 to 989 percent more fruit than 12-hour days; LED light in a dim winter greenhouse about twice the yield of daylight alone; substrate 144 percent more than water culture; far-red light plus 48 percent in Albion only; and above 86 degrees Fahrenheit no new flowers.](/images/guides/hydroponic-strawberry-yield-factors.svg)

*What moved yield in the trials. Day length: Park et al. 2023. LED light: Cornell (Allred) 2020. Substrate: Hutchinson et al. 2025. Far-red: Ries and Park 2024. Heat: Delaware SARE ONE22-417.*

Every trial that compared cultivars found big gaps, and 'Albion' was rarely on top for yield:

- **Illinois:** 'Monterey' 0.76 lb, 'San Andreas' 0.54 lb, 'Albion' 0.48 lb per plant; the author says Albion's low yield "was expected."
- **Cornell:** 'Cabrillo' out-yielded 'Albion' under daylight, HPS and LED. Cornell's growers describe 'Albion' as sweeter but lower yielding (Greenhouse Grower 2021).
- **Delaware:** in one month, 'Royal Royce' (7,567 g from 48 plants) and 'Cabrillo' (7,465 g) gave about three times the fruit of 'Albion' (2,518 g).

**Day-neutral or June-bearing?** Day-neutral types flower whenever temperatures are moderate, and University of Minnesota Extension lists 'Albion', 'Evie-II', 'Monterey', 'Portola', 'San Andreas' and 'Seascape'. In the Delaware rooms, June-bearing cultivars did not flower well under a 14-hour day; the report says they need a specific lighting regime. 'Chandler' did well in Nebraska's winter greenhouse, so short-day plants can work under natural winter days, but for a lit indoor setup we recommend day-neutral plants.

**Our pick:** 'Monterey' or 'San Andreas' for yield, 'Cabrillo' where you can find it, and 'Albion' when flavor matters more than weight. Our [hydroponic strawberries guide](/crops/hydroponic-strawberries/) covers buying certified plants.

## Factor 2: day length and light

**Day length came first.** Arizona State grew 'Albion' in deep water culture under LEDs at three intensities and two day lengths. A 16-hour day raised fruit production by **372–989%** over a 12-hour day and gave **365–1,220% more fruit**; at the highest intensity it brought the first harvest **17 days** sooner. Raising intensity from 200 to 450 µmol/m²/s grew bigger plants but had little effect on fruit, and berry weight did not change (Park et al. 2023). The daily light integral (DLI) in that trial ran from 8.6 to 25.9 mol/m²/day.

**Light amount mattered where it was scarce.** In Cornell's winter greenhouse, natural DLI fell as low as 1–2 mol/m²/day. Topping it up to a DLI of 15 (which the researchers call the low end for strawberries) raised 'Albion' from **1.77 kg/m²** on daylight alone to **2.72 kg/m²** under HPS and **3.68 kg/m²** under LED; 'Cabrillo' went from **3.03** to **4.9** under HPS, and Cornell reported **5.67 kg** under LED (printed without the per-area unit). "More light almost always means higher yields," the researcher said.

**Reading the two together.** The results do not clash as much as they seem. Cornell's gain came from lifting a very dark winter crop to DLI 15; Arizona State's lowest treatment already had a DLI of 8.6–11.5 and its plants were limited by day length, not light. Our recommendation: give a **16-hour day** first, then enough intensity to reach a DLI of about 15. At 16 hours, that needs about **260 µmol/m²/s** at the leaves (15 × 1,000,000 ÷ 57,600 s; our arithmetic).

**Far-red light.** Adding far-red LEDs (about 12% of the light) raised fruit fresh mass per plant by **48%** and fruit number by **36%** in 'Albion', but not in 'Monterey' (Ries and Park 2024). Treat it as a bonus for some cultivars, not a fix for a short day or a dim lamp.

## Factor 3: system and root zone

A University of Georgia greenhouse trial grew 'Florida Brilliance' and 'Florida Beauty' in four systems for 129 days. Substrate troughs (perlite and peat mix, drip-fed) gave **144% more fruit** than the average of three water-culture systems for 'Florida Brilliance', and **613% more** than NFT for 'Florida Beauty'. Vertical towers beat NFT by 71% for 'Florida Brilliance' (Hutchinson et al. 2025). All four systems ran at DLI 17.5 and EC near 1, so the root zone, not light or feeding, made the difference.

Cornell's indoor guide says that "research and practice have not yet shown effective deep-water culture or NFT strawberry production." Yet both Arizona State trials grew fruiting strawberries in deep water culture rafts. **Our reading:** water culture can work under tight control, but for the most fruit per plant, grow in a free-draining substrate fed by drip, as Nebraska, Cornell, Illinois and Georgia did. See [hydroponic growing media](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/), the [Dutch bucket guide](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/) and the [drip system guide](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/). If you already run channels, our [NFT strawberries guide](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/) covers how to set them up.

## Factor 4: temperature

- **Heat stops flowers.** In the Delaware rooms, temperatures above **86 °F (30 °C)** in late July "essentially stopped the production of new flowers," ending the measured crop. In Illinois, summer heat in the tunnel cut yields until mid-August.
- **Cool nights grow bigger berries.** Cornell runs **72/58 °F (22/14 °C)** day and night, and notes that nights of 50–54 °F (10–12 °C) give larger berries.
- **Crown temperature.** The University of Arizona reports that holding the crown near 18 °C (64 °F) raised winter yields by about 10%.

**Our pick:** aim for about 72 °F (22 °C) by day, 58 °F (14 °C) at night, and never let the room sit above 86 °F (30 °C).

## Factor 5: pollination, crowns and runners

Indoor flowers need help to set full-sized, even fruit. Nebraska released bumblebees once flowers opened. Georgia counted a berry as marketable only if it weighed at least **8 g** and was evenly pollinated, so poor pollination costs yield twice: fewer berries and more culls. At home, brush each open flower with a soft paintbrush or shake the trusses gently every day or two.

Crowded plants yield small fruit. Cornell limits each plant to **three crowns** and **four flower stalks** and removes runners. Delaware found plants overcrowded after about three months, thinned crowns every 12 weeks and removed runners weekly; fruit after the thinning was small.

## Step by step: set up for the most fruit per plant

1. **Choose day-neutral plants.** 'Monterey', 'San Andreas' or 'Cabrillo' for yield; buy named, certified plants.
2. **Plant into substrate.** A perlite-based or coir mix in buckets, troughs or a gutter, fed by drip.
3. **Space them.** About 1 plant per sq ft (Cornell) or 0.12 m² per plant (Nebraska).
4. **Set a 16-hour day** and enough light for a DLI near 15, about 260 µmol/m²/s at 16 hours.
5. **Hold the temperature** near 72/58 °F (22/14 °C), below 86 °F (30 °C).
6. **Feed at EC 1.2, pH 5.5–6.0**, and check both two or three times a week.
7. **Pollinate** every open flower every day or two with a soft brush.
8. **Thin** to three crowns and four flower stalks per plant; pull runners weekly.
9. **Pick** at least three times a week once fruit ripens, as Cornell advises for production; expect the first fruit about 7 weeks after potting crowns.
10. **Weigh your harvest.** Your own grams per plant is the only number that tells you whether a change worked.

## Troubleshooting low yield

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lots of leaves, few flowers | Day too short | Move to a 16-hour day | Park et al. 2023 |
| Flowers stop in summer | Room above 86 °F (30 °C) | Cool the room or shade; expect a pause | Delaware SARE; Illinois Extension |
| Small berries | Crowded crowns, warm nights | Thin to three crowns; cool nights toward 58 °F | Cornell 2021; Delaware SARE |
| Misshapen or uneven berries | Poor pollination | Brush flowers every day or two | Hutchinson et al. 2025; Paparozzi 2018 |

## Frequently asked questions

### How many strawberries does one hydroponic plant produce?

In the Nebraska winter trial, between 5 and 58 berries per plant depending on cultivar and season, and 0.12–0.74 kg of fruit. Most well-lit day-neutral plants in the trials we read gave 0.2–0.5 kg (½–1 lb) over five to six months.

### How long until a hydroponic strawberry plant fruits?

About 7 weeks from potting dormant crowns in Nebraska, with 3–4 weeks from flower to ripe fruit. Indoors under LEDs, harvest began 66 days after transplant and averaged 74–79 days by cultivar (Ries and Park 2024).

### Is NFT or a tower good for yield?

They work, but in the Georgia comparison, drip-fed substrate gave the most fruit per plant, towers came second and NFT last.

### How many plants for a family?

At 9–23 g per plant per week (our arithmetic from 0.2–0.5 kg over 22 weeks), 20–50 plants give about a pound a week. Our [tower garden guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) covers fitting many plants in a small space.
