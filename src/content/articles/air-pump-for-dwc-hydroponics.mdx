**A DWC air pump has one job: keep the water around the roots at 7–10 ppm dissolved oxygen, never below 4 ppm** (Virginia Tech). Run it **24 hours a day**, feed **one or two air stones in a 5-gallon bucket** (UF/IFAS), fit a **check valve** in the airline (University of Illinois) and keep the solution at **65–75 °F (18–24 °C)** (South Dakota State). Temperature matters as much as the pump: water at **90 °F (32 °C) holds only 7.4 mg/L of oxygen** even when fully saturated, against **11.9 mg/L at 45 °F (7 °C)** (UF/IFAS), and no air pump can push water past that ceiling.

No university or extension source we found gives an air-per-gallon rule for hydroponics, and the "per gallon" figures on shop pages could not be traced to one. Below are the real setups sources describe, one fish-tank rule of thumb with its limits, and how to check your pump.

HydroRigor has not tested air pumps; every figure comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end, and where we did arithmetic, we say so.

ppm and mg/L are the same unit for dissolved oxygen, so the two are used interchangeably here.

## Why deep water culture needs an air pump

In DWC the roots hang in the nutrient solution all the time. Roots breathe, and still water runs short of oxygen. Oregon State puts it plainly: with minimal water movement and low air exchange, **DWC systems require effective aeration**, through air pumps and air stones or, in larger setups, ozone injection. South Dakota State explains the mechanism: the pump makes bubbles, which increase the water's surface exposure and agitation and let more oxygen dissolve. Without enough oxygen, it warns, submerged roots can suffer **root rot**, growth slows and plants become more prone to disease.

Not every water culture system needs one. Kratky leaves about **1 inch of air space** between the lid and a falling water level and uses **no pumps or electricity** (UF/IFAS HS1488); NFT gets oxygen from the thin moving film and uses a water pump instead (see our [water pump guide](/equipment/hydroponic-water-pumps/)). If you want to skip the air pump altogether, start with the [Kratky method](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/).

## How much oxygen is enough? A source conflict

The sources agree that more oxygen is better up to a point, but not on the number:

| Source | Dissolved oxygen | Context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virginia Tech SPES-466 | 7–10 ppm; minimum 4 | Extension, DWC systems |
| University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 | 6–8 ppm | Lettuce in DWC |
| Ohio State (Gillespie, 2019) | 6 ppm or more | Leafy greens, short-course handout |
| South Dakota State P-00337 | Above 5–8 mg/L | Most hydroponic systems |
| UF/IFAS HS1422 | 5 mg/L | Lettuce, floating raft |
| Qin and Ferrarezi 2025 | Below about 3–4 mg/L inhibits growth | Literature summary in a research paper |

Our reading: the figures overlap at **6–8 ppm**, and Virginia Tech's **7–10 ppm with a 4 ppm floor** is the safest target because it is the highest and comes with a hard minimum. If you can only remember one number, remember 4: below it, you are inside the range where the research Qin and Ferrarezi summarize found root and shoot growth inhibited.

### Does extra oxygen help? Only for some crops

The best data on this comes from a 2025 University of Georgia greenhouse study (Qin and Ferrarezi, HortScience). Their control trays used a plain air pump feeding **four 2-inch (5.1 cm) air stones in each 50-gallon (190 L), 4 × 4 ft tray**, which held **6.5–8 mg/L**. Other trays were enriched with an oxygen generator to about 11, 16 and 20 mg/L.

- **Arugula** responded strongly: one variety yielded **103% more at about 16 mg/L and 191% more at about 20 mg/L** than the air-pump control.
- **Kale** showed **no significant difference**. The authors concluded that for kale "a standard air pump-based aeration would meet the root respiration demand."
- Enrichment raised electricity use by about **143–145%**, and only one arugula treatment paid for itself.
- They cite earlier work where **lettuce at 16.8 mg/L grew no better than at 8.4 mg/L** (Goto and colleagues, 1996).

So for lettuce, kale and most home DWC crops, an ordinary air pump is enough. Pure-oxygen setups are a commercial experiment, not a home upgrade.

## Temperature: the limit your air pump can't beat

Warm water holds less oxygen. UF/IFAS gives the endpoints: fully saturated water holds **11.9 mg/L at 45 °F (7 °C)** but only **7.4 mg/L at 90 °F (32 °C)**. Qin and Ferrarezi note that air-stone aeration keeps water near **9 mg/L at 68 °F (20 °C)**, citing Kurashina and colleagues (2019). An air pump can bring water up to saturation; it cannot push it above, which is why the Georgia team needed an oxygen generator to reach 15–20 mg/L.

That means a bigger pump does not fix a hot reservoir. At 90 °F, even perfect aeration leaves you at the bottom of Virginia Tech's 7–10 ppm range. UF/IFAS calls this a double squeeze for fish: warm water holds less oxygen just as their need for it rises. Keep the solution at **65–75 °F (18–24 °C)** (South Dakota State); Ohio State's tighter target for leafy greens is **68–72 °F (20–22 °C)**, and UF/IFAS allows up to **80 °F** for lettuce.

Practical ways to keep it cool: a larger volume of water, which Oregon State says acts as a thermal buffer against temperature swings, and a cooler room for the tank. Our [reservoir size guide](/equipment/hydroponic-reservoir-size/) covers volume.

## How big an air pump? What the sources actually give

There is no sourced gallons-to-pump formula for hydroponics. Here is everything we found, from most to least relevant:

| Setup | Air supply | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One 5-gal bucket, one plant | Aquarium pump, 1–2 air stones | Roots can stay submerged if well oxygenated | UF/IFAS Volusia County |
| 50-gal (190 L), 4 × 4 ft tray | Four 2-in (5.1 cm) stones from one pump | 6.5–8 mg/L | Qin and Ferrarezi 2025 |
| Fish tank (aquaponics) | 1 cfm per 300 gal, "simple rule of thumb" | Not measured | Sawyer presentation, Texas A&M-hosted |

Two notes on the last row. It is a rule for **fish tanks**, where the fish use oxygen too, and it comes from a grower's presentation hosted on Texas A&M's extension site, not from a university study. By our arithmetic it works out to about **0.5 L/min for a 5-gallon bucket** and about **4.7 L/min for a 50-gallon tank** (1 cfm is about 28.3 L/min). Treat it as a rough floor for comparing pumps, not a target.

The Georgia tray gives a more useful home benchmark. By our arithmetic, four stones in 50 gallons is **one 2-inch stone per about 12 gallons, or per 4 square feet of tank floor**, and that held dissolved oxygen inside the 6–8 ppm range the sources overlap on. The paper printed its pump's rating, but we could not confirm the units, so we don't quote it.

### How to choose a pump, step by step

These steps combine the sources above. They are a planning method, not a tested HydroRigor build.

1. **Count the air stones.** One or two per 5-gallon bucket (UF/IFAS Volusia County); for a larger tub or raft tank, spread stones so bubbles rise across the whole floor, roughly one 2-inch stone per 4 square feet as in the Georgia trays.
2. **Give each stone its own outlet** or a manifold with a valve per line, so one clogged stone doesn't starve the others.
3. **Check the pump's rated output against the stone count,** not just the tank size. A small single-outlet aquarium pump suits one bucket; several buckets or a raft tank need a multi-outlet pump.
4. **Compare with the fish-tank floor.** If the pump gives less than about 1 cfm per 300 gallons (about 0.1 L/min per gallon by our arithmetic), it is undersized even by a rule written for fish.
5. **Fit a check valve** in each line so water can't run back to the pump when it stops (University of Illinois).
6. **Plug into a GFCI outlet** and avoid extension cords where you can (University of Maine).
7. **Verify.** Even bubbles across the floor, white roots and no smell are the everyday checks; a dissolved oxygen meter, listed as optional by South Dakota State, gives the actual number.

## Setting up the air line

1. **Place the stone on the tank floor,** under the plants. Bubbles rise and mix the solution on the way up.
2. **Run the tubing over the rim** or through a small side hole. UF/IFAS Volusia County drills a **1-inch hole** in the side of the bucket for the air tube and topping up.
3. **Put the pump outside the tank,** on a shelf. If the pump sits below the water line, water can siphon back down the tube when it stops; the check valve is the backstop (University of Illinois).
4. **Keep plugs and power strips above and away from any spill path,** and use a GFCI outlet wherever water is present (University of Maine).
5. **Cover the tank.** Oregon State says to cover the water surface with opaque materials, which cuts evaporation and algae.
6. **Leave it running all the time.** None of the sources describes a timer for DWC aeration; Oregon State calls aeration a requirement of the system, and the Georgia air-pump control ran continuously. Pond advice to aerate only at night (UF/IFAS FA27) is for ponds where algae make oxygen by day, not for a dark, covered tank.
7. **Check it as part of routine maintenance.** Oregon State lists aeration equipment alongside pH and nutrient checks; see our [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) for the full schedule and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) for crop ranges.

## If the air pump stops

DWC is more forgiving than NFT here. University of Missouri Extension says DWC plants **don't wilt immediately** if the air pump fails, because the roots are still in water. But no source we found gives a number of hours before damage, so we don't guess one.

| What to do | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Check bubbles daily | A dead pump is silent; roots slowly run out of oxygen | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Keep a spare pump or a battery aerator | Battery aerators exist for power outages | Sawyer presentation (aquaponics) |
| Plan backup power for pumps and aerators | Same presentation's advice for fish systems | Sawyer presentation (aquaponics) |
| Keep the water cool | Cooler water starts with more oxygen in it | UF/IFAS FA27 |
| Watch roots after an outage | Browning, a foul smell or slowed growth signal low oxygen | South Dakota State P-00337 |

## Hydrogen peroxide instead of an air pump?

South Dakota State notes that some growers add diluted hydrogen peroxide, which can raise oxygen and also disinfects. It gives **no dose**, nor does any other source we read, so we don't give one. It doesn't replace continuous aeration.

## Troubleshooting DWC aeration

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brown, slimy or smelly roots | Low oxygen, root rot | Check pump and stones; cool the water; change the solution | South Dakota State P-00337 |
| Slow growth, healthy-looking tops | Oxygen running low | Add a stone or a bigger pump; keep water at 65–75 °F | South Dakota State P-00337 |
| Few or uneven bubbles | Clogged stone or kinked tubing | Clean or replace the stone; straighten the line | Oregon State EM 9455 (check equipment) |
| Water in the airline, or a dead pump after an outage | No check valve | Fit a check valve; pump outside the tank | University of Illinois |
| Roots fine in winter, rotting in summer | Warm water holds less oxygen | Bigger volume; cooler room | UF/IFAS FA27; Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Algae in the tank | Light reaching the water | Opaque lid and walls | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Tripped outlet | Water reached a plug | Dry everything; use a GFCI outlet | University of Maine 2311 |

For nutrient-side causes of poor roots, see the [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/); South Dakota State suggests changing the solution every one to two weeks in small systems.

## Frequently asked questions

### What size air pump do I need for DWC?

No university source gives a per-gallon rule. Real setups: one or two air stones per 5-gallon bucket on an aquarium pump (UF/IFAS Volusia County), and four 2-inch stones per 50-gallon tray, which held 6.5–8 mg/L (Qin and Ferrarezi 2025). A fish-tank rule of 1 cfm per 300 gallons is a rough minimum.

### Should the air pump run 24/7?

Yes. DWC needs continuous aeration because the water barely moves (Oregon State), and none of the sources gives a DWC timer schedule.

### What dissolved oxygen level should DWC have?

Aim for 7–10 ppm and never let it fall below 4 ppm (Virginia Tech). Kentucky gives 6–8 ppm for lettuce.

### Can an air pump give too much oxygen?

Not in practice: an air pump only brings water toward saturation. Going above it took an oxygen generator in the Georgia study, which helped arugula but not kale, at about 143–145% more electricity.

### Do I need an air pump for Kratky?

No. Kratky uses no pumps; roots get air from the gap of about 1 inch above the falling water (UF/IFAS HS1488).

### How long can DWC plants survive without air?

Missouri says they don't wilt immediately, but no source gives a number of hours. Treat a pump failure as urgent and keep a spare.

### Why do I need a check valve?

So water can't flow back down the airline to the pump when it stops (University of Illinois).
