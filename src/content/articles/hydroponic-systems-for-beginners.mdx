The best hydroponic system for a beginner is a **Kratky bucket growing lettuce**: a food-grade 5-gallon bucket with three 2-inch net cups, no pump and no electricity, and about **1–1.5 gallons of nutrient solution per lettuce plant** for the whole crop (UF/IFAS). Mix the solution to about **EC 1.25 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0**, leave about an inch of air under the lid, and don't top it up. Lettuce from seed is ready in about **6–7 weeks** (Kratky, University of Hawaii). Once that works, move to a small **deep water culture (DWC) tub** with an air pump for more plants. Leave NFT channels and fruiting crops like tomatoes for your second or third grow.

Why this order: a beginner's first system should be the one with the least that can go wrong. A Kratky bucket has nothing to switch off. NFT is the opposite: Oregon State warns that if the pump or power fails, plants can wilt quickly. Every figure below is tied to a university or extension source listed at the end. If the idea of growing without soil is new to you, [what hydroponics is and how it works](/systems/what-is-hydroponics/) covers the basics first.

## Rank systems by what happens when something goes wrong

Most "best system for beginners" lists rank by features. A better test for a first grow is what happens on the day something goes wrong, because something will.

![Four beginner systems ranked from most to least forgiving. Kratky bucket: no pump or power; the mistake to avoid is topping up over the air roots. Deep water culture: the air pump runs all the time; if the air stops, oxygen falls; keep 7–10 ppm with 4 ppm the minimum. Dutch buckets on drip: the pump runs on a timer; a clogged outlet dries the media, so screen every outlet. NFT channels: the pump runs nonstop; if the pump or power fails, plants can wilt quickly.](/images/guides/beginner-hydroponic-systems-forgiveness.svg)

*The fewer moving parts, the more a first grow forgives. From Oregon State EM 9455, EM 9456 and EM 9457, Virginia Tech SPES-466, UF/IFAS HS1488 and Kratky.*

- **Kratky bucket: most forgiving.** All of the solution goes in before planting and nothing is added afterwards. As the plants drink, the level drops and roots in the moist air gap take in oxygen, so no pump or air stone is needed (Kratky). UF/IFAS says a well-lit Kratky bucket rarely needs pH or nutrient adjustments.
- **Deep water culture: forgiving if the air keeps running.** Roots hang in an aerated tank. Virginia Tech keeps dissolved oxygen at 7–10 ppm with 4 ppm the minimum, and met Pythium root rot in its DWC spinach; raising oxygen helps control it. Oregon State notes the tank is heavy and must sit on the ground.
- **Dutch buckets: right for tomatoes, more to manage.** Buckets of media are fed by drip lines on a timer. Oregon State says to screen each outlet so media cannot clog the drain. Virginia Tech's trial got its best tomato and pepper yields from media systems like these, so they are the right second step for fruiting crops.
- **NFT channels: least forgiving.** A thin film of solution runs down sloped channels and back to a tank, with a pump that runs nonstop. Oregon State calls power and pump backups crucial because plants can wilt quickly when the film stops.

Oklahoma State calls the wick system the simplest of all. It is passive too, but none of the sources here gives crop guidance for it, so this guide starts with Kratky instead.

## Buy a kit or build your own?

Both work. The decision is about what you want to learn first.

| You want | Choose | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The cheapest, most certain first harvest | Build a Kratky bucket | Food-grade bucket, opaque or covered so light can't reach the solution |
| A tidy unit on the kitchen counter | A small countertop kit | Its light: lettuce needs a DLI of 12–17 mol/m²/day (about 250 µmol/m²/s for 14–16 hours) |
| More salad every week | A DWC tub or kit | An air pump with a check valve, and a lid that covers the tank fully |
| Tomatoes or peppers | Dutch buckets, after a first leafy crop | Screened outlets and fresh media |

**Recommendation:** build the Kratky bucket first even if you plan to buy a kit later. It costs little, teaches you pH, EC and how roots behave, and a kit makes more sense once you know what you are looking at. Before buying any system, run through the [9 checks before you buy a hydroponic system](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-sale/). To build, follow the [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/), which has step-by-step instructions for a Kratky bucket, a DWC tote and Dutch buckets.

Kit lights are where most small units fall short. The University of Kentucky gives lettuce an optimal daily light integral of 12–17 mol/m²/day and reaches it with about 250 µmol/m²/s for 14–16 hours a day. If a listing doesn't say what intensity its light gives at the leaves, you can't tell whether it will grow full heads. Illinois Extension's simpler rule for a first grow is 6–8 hours a day from a sunny spot or a grow light.

## Your first grow, step by step

![Timeline of a first grow. Day 0: sow lettuce in plugs and keep them near 68 °F (20 °C). About day 3: seedlings come up. Days 14–21: transplant three seedlings with 3–4 true leaves into a 5-gallon Kratky bucket with three 2-inch net cups, solution at EC 1.25 and pH 5.5–6.0, filled to about 1 inch below the lid. After transplant: leave the level alone and never top up over the air roots. Harvest about 3–4 weeks after transplant, or 6–7 weeks from seeding.](/images/guides/first-hydroponic-grow-timeline.svg)

*A first lettuce crop from seed to harvest. Timings from the University of Kentucky (germination and transplant), UF/IFAS HS1488 (bucket and harvest) and Kratky (seed to harvest).*

1. **Sow.** Put a couple of lettuce seeds in each rockwool cube or similar plug. Keep them near 68 °F (20 °C); the University of Kentucky reports germination in about 3 days.
2. **Grow the seedlings.** Give them gentle light. Kentucky transplants 14–21 days after seeding, when plants have 3–4 true leaves and roots show through the plug. UF/IFAS transplants seedlings one to three weeks old.
3. **Prepare the bucket.** Drill three 2-inch holes in the lid. Cover the bucket if light can get through it: Illinois Extension says light reaching the solution grows algae.
4. **Mix and fill.** Mix the nutrient to about EC 1.25 mS/cm and adjust pH to 5.5–6.0 (UF/IFAS). Fill until the solution reaches about halfway up the net cups, leaving about an inch of air below the lid.
5. **Transplant.** Set one seedling in each cup, with the plug touching the solution.
6. **Leave the level alone.** The first fill can carry the whole crop. As the plants drink, the level falls and the upper roots get air. Kratky's one rule: never top the container back up over the air-exposed roots, because submerging them can drown the plant.
7. **Check weekly.** Look at pH and the solution level. Check the plants for color and for algae where light gets in.
8. **Harvest.** UF/IFAS gives about three to four weeks after transplanting for its Florida cool-season crop; in Kratky's trials a lettuce grown in a 4-liter bottle took about 6–7 weeks from seeding. Indoors, your light and temperature decide the timing.

For the full lettuce targets, including temperature and light by the hour, see the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/).

## Five beginner mistakes and how to avoid them

| Mistake | What happens | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Topping up a Kratky bucket | Air roots are submerged and the plant can drown | Leave the level alone after the first fill | Kratky |
| Using soil fertilizer | The solution lacks what soil would supply | Use a complete nutrient made for hydroponics | University of Illinois Extension |
| Clear or uncovered containers | Algae grows where light reaches the solution | Use opaque containers or cover the sides | University of Illinois Extension |
| Trusting an uncalibrated pH meter | Readings drift and you chase the wrong number | Calibrate weekly with pH 4 and pH 7 buffers | University of Missouri |
| Starting with NFT or tomatoes | A pump stop wilts plants fast; fruiting crops need more support and root room | Grow a leafy crop in Kratky or DWC first | Oregon State EM 9457; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |

Two more tips from the sources. The University of Missouri says to store a pH probe in storage solution or pH 4 buffer, never plain water, and to expect a probe to last 1–2 years. Kratky notes that poor water can concentrate salts and cause tipburn, so use clean water where your tap water is hard.

## When to move up

Move to a second system when the first has worked at least once. Oregon State's short version of which system suits what: NFT for short-lived crops or vertical gardens, buckets for long-lived or vining plants, deep water culture for large spaces and ebb and flow for seedlings.

- **Want more salad?** Build or buy a DWC tub. Keep the air stone running all the time; Virginia Tech keeps oxygen at 7–10 ppm. The [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) covers the setup.
- **Want tomatoes or peppers?** Use Dutch buckets: in Virginia Tech's trial, media systems gave the highest tomato and pepper yields and NFT the lowest. See [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).
- **Short on floor space?** A vertical tower is another option once you are comfortable with pumps; see the [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the easiest hydroponic system for beginners?

A Kratky bucket. UF/IFAS describes it as needing no pumps or electricity, and a well-lit bucket rarely needs pH or nutrient adjustments. Oklahoma State calls the wick system the simplest, but the sources here give no crop guidance for wicks.

### What should a beginner grow first?

Lettuce or another short-cycle leafy green. UF/IFAS's Kratky bucket is built for lettuce, and in Kratky's trials one lettuce plant grew in a 4-liter (about 1-gallon) bottle in about 6–7 weeks from seeding. Fruiting crops do better in other systems.

### Do I need an air pump?

Not for Kratky: the air gap that forms as the plants drink supplies the roots with oxygen. For deep water culture, yes. The pump runs all the time to keep dissolved oxygen at 7–10 ppm (Virginia Tech).

### What pH and EC should a beginner use?

For a Kratky lettuce crop, UF/IFAS uses pH 5.5–6.0 and EC about 1.25 mS/cm. Oklahoma State gives a general range of pH 5.0–6.0 and EC 1.5–3 mS/cm, adjusted by crop. Check your crop in the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).

### Is NFT good for beginners?

Not as a first system. Oregon State warns that if the power or pump fails, NFT plants can wilt quickly, so it needs backups. Try it once you have grown a crop in Kratky or DWC.

### Is a hydroponic kit worth it for a beginner?

It can be, if its light is strong enough and it includes or leaves room for a pH and EC meter.
