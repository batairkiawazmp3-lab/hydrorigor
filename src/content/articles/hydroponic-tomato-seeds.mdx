The best tomato seed for hydroponics is the one whose **mature size fits your system**. For a countertop unit or a small bucket, choose a **micro-dwarf such as 'Micro-Tom' or 'Micro-Tina'**: in University of Florida trials they stayed about **4–10 inches (10–25 cm) tall in a 1-liter pot** and showed first fruit color **71–100 days** after seeding. For Dutch buckets with room for a string and wire, choose an **indeterminate greenhouse cultivar**: most greenhouse tomatoes are indeterminate (University of Tennessee), and 'Trust' has been the most common greenhouse cultivar in north Florida for years (UF/IFAS). Germinate any of them at **75–85 °F (24–29 °C)** in cubes at least 1.5 inches across (Tennessee).

Seed packets sell flavor and color. Indoors, the first question is height. A full-size vine doesn't become compact because it grows in water, and Utah State advises smaller varieties indoors because root and shoot space are limited. This guide sorts tomato seed by the space you have, explains the labels on a packet, and covers starting the seed for a hydroponic system. Every figure comes from a university or extension source listed at the end.

## Choose by mature size, not by the picture

![Four sizes of tomato for hydroponics, smallest first. Micro-dwarf such as Micro-Tom or Micro-Tina: 4–10 inches tall in a 1-liter pot, first fruit color 71–100 days; fits countertop units. Compact indoor such as Tiny Tim, Terenzo or Lizzano: 1–2 feet across in a 1–3 gallon container; fits a windowsill or small buckets. Determinate bush, common in home gardens: stops at its own height and fruits over a short period; fits buckets with a cage. Indeterminate greenhouse types such as Trust: keep growing on one stem and need a string and wire; fit Dutch buckets in a tall room.](/images/guides/hydroponic-tomato-seed-by-space.svg)

*Sort seed by the space it will need when mature. Heights and timings from UF/IFAS HS987 and HortScience 35(4); container sizes from Utah State; growth habits from UF/IFAS HS1273 and Tennessee W 844-C. The "fits" lines are HydroRigor's.*

Tomatoes come in two growth habits, and dwarfs are a third size class:

- **Determinate** plants are bushier and shorter. UF/IFAS says they stop growing in height once flowers appear at the ends of their stems, and their fruit matures over a shorter period. Tennessee notes determinates are more common in home gardens and field production.
- **Indeterminate** plants keep growing and setting fruit along the stem, with flower clusters only on the sides (UF/IFAS). Most greenhouse cultivars are indeterminate and fruit for several months (Tennessee). In a north Florida greenhouse, indeterminate plants can reach 20–30 feet of stem between September and June, lowered along the wire every two weeks (UF/IFAS). At home you'd top the plant long before that.
- **Micro-dwarfs** are genetically small in plant, leaf, stem and fruit. The University of Florida bred 'Micro-Tom' (1989), then 'Micro-Gold', 'Micro-Tina' and 'Micro-Gemma'. Ordinary dwarf cultivars can outgrow small containers, the Florida authors note, while these stay small because the trait is genetic.

The practical rule: measure the height between your reservoir lid and the light, and pick seed that fits under it with room to spare. The [hydroponic tomato guide](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/) covers training an indeterminate plant to a string when you have the height.

## Micro-dwarfs: the countertop option

The University of Florida trials are the best numbers available for truly small tomatoes. Single plants grew in 13-cm (about 5-inch), 1-liter pots in a Bradenton greenhouse.

| Cultivar | Height, spring / fall | Fruit weight | Fruit per pot | Days to first fruit color | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 'Micro-Tom' | 10–23 cm (4–9 in) | 4.5–7.9 g | 27–48 | 73–100 | HS987; HortScience 2000 |
| 'Micro-Tina' | 11–23 cm (4–9 in) | 5.5–6.9 g | 42–45 | 71–91 | HortScience 2000 |
| 'Micro-Gemma' (gold) | 12–19 cm (5–8 in) | 5.9–6.2 g | 34–37 | 75–98 | HortScience 2000 |
| 'Micro-Gold' (gold) | 12–25 cm (5–10 in) | 7.8–9.8 g | 19–37 | 74–96 | UF/IFAS HS987 |

Ranges span the spring and fall trials (1993 for 'Micro-Gold'; 1998 for the others), so they come from different years. Three things stand out:

- **Season changes everything.** The same cultivar colored about 20 days sooner in fall than in spring, and mostly grew taller. Your light and temperature will move these numbers too.
- **'Micro-Tina' was the earliest** (71 days to first color in fall pots) and was bred to be sweeter than 'Micro-Tom'. 'Micro-Tina' and 'Micro-Gemma' are resistant to Fusarium wilt race 1 and gray leaf spot; the papers give no resistance for 'Micro-Tom'.
- **Fruit are cherry-sized:** 20–27 mm (about 1 in) across.

Days to first fruit color is not the same as harvest: the trials counted from seeding to the first fruit showing color. Expect to pick a few days later.

## Compact indoor cultivars

Utah State Extension names 'Tiny Tim', 'Micro Tom', 'Terenzo' and 'Lizzano' for indoor growing: small plants, typically 1–2 feet across, sold mostly as seed. Its container guidance is for potting mix, but the volumes are a useful guide to root space: a 1-gallon pot for the smallest varieties and 2–3 gallons for larger ones, and a pot that's too big beats one that's too small.

Utah State also gives the indoor temperature limits: below 50 °F (10 °C) can harm the plants, and above 90 °F (32 °C) may prevent fruit from setting.

## Indeterminate greenhouse cultivars for buckets

If you have Dutch buckets, a string and an overhead wire, the cultivars bred for greenhouses make sense:

- **'Trust'** has been the most commonly used greenhouse cultivar in north Florida hydroponics for several years (UF/IFAS), and it is the cultivar the University of Tennessee used in its perlite-bag spacing trial: first harvest about 10 weeks after transplant.
- **'Geronimo' and 'Big Dena'** have had some success in the Southeast, with limited Florida experience (UF/IFAS).
- **Greenhouse-bred cultivars resist leaf mold and powdery mildew** (Tennessee), the diseases of humid, enclosed spaces.

Greenhouse cultivars need height and training. Alabama spaces greenhouse plants 16 in apart in the row and hangs its wires 8–10 ft up. Few homes have that, which is why most indoor growers do better with compact seed. Light is the other limit: Purdue puts tomatoes at a daily light integral above 20 mol/m²/day, more than most small countertop lights give.

## Reading the seed packet

**Hybrid, open-pollinated or heirloom.** UF/IFAS defines hybrids (F1) as first-generation crosses bred for traits such as disease resistance and yield; they are the most widely grown type. Open-pollinated seed breeds true, so you can save it. Heirlooms are open-pollinated types with typically 50+ years of selection; UF/IFAS says they are usually indeterminate, have little or no disease resistance, crack easily and often fail to set fruit in extreme heat. Protected culture makes them easier, but they're a harder first crop.

**Fruit type.** Tennessee lists beefsteak (the most common), cherry, grape, plum, roma or paste, and cluster or tomato-on-the-vine. Cluster is a way of harvesting, not a growth habit (UF/IFAS).

**Disease-resistance codes.** These name specific diseases, not general toughness. The UF/IFAS table uses:

| Code | Means |
| --- | --- |
| F1, F2, F3 | Fusarium wilt races 1, 2 and 3 |
| V | Verticillium wilt |
| ASC | Alternaria stem canker |
| St | Gray leaf spot |
| N | Nematodes |
| ToMV | Tomato mosaic virus |
| TYLCV | Tomato yellow leaf curl virus |
| TSWV | Tomato spotted wilt virus |

A code covers only the pathogen (and race) it names. Keep clean tools, clean water and good airflow either way. Seed companies print their own legends, so check the one on your packet.

## How to start tomato seed for hydroponics

![Timeline from sowing to first fruit. Day 0: sow in cubes at least 1.5 inches across at 75–85 °F (24–29 °C), sowing 10–15% extra. When the seed leaves open: feed half strength at EC 0.5–1.0 mS/cm. At 2–6 weeks: transplant, raising EC to 1.0–1.5; Alabama transplants at 4–6 inches tall. Micro-dwarfs: first fruit color 71–100 days from seeding. Standard tomatoes: first ripe fruit about 90–120 days from seed. Above 90 °F (32 °C) germination falls.](/images/guides/hydroponic-tomato-seed-timeline.svg)

*From seed to first fruit. Seed-starting figures from Tennessee W 844-C, transplant size from Alabama ANR-2955, micro-dwarf timings from the University of Florida trials.*

1. **Pick cubes.** Peat, coir, rockwool or foam, at least 1.5 × 1.5 in (Tennessee). Rockwool and foam move straight into net cups and buckets without disturbing the roots.
2. **Sow extra.** Fill 10–15% more cubes than the plants you need, because germination is uneven (Tennessee). Thin to one seedling per cube.
3. **Keep them warm and moist.** Hold 75–85 °F (24–29 °C); above 90 °F (32 °C) germination drops (Tennessee). A heat mat helps in a cool room; Alabama raises transplants with the root zone at 84 °F (29 °C). Keep about ¼ inch of water in the tray so cubes are evenly moist, not soaked, and keep trays out of hot direct sun.
4. **Feed once the seed leaves open.** Start at half strength, EC 0.5–1.0 mS/cm, and raise it to 1.0–1.5 by transplant (Tennessee). Scale your nutrient label with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/).
5. **Give strong light** as soon as they come up. Tennessee says fruiting crops need consistently strong light and that winter daylight is often not enough.
6. **Transplant.** Tennessee gives 2–4 weeks from seed (its text also says about 3–4); Alabama transplants at 4–6 weeks, at 4–6 in (10–15 cm) tall. The Tennessee trial moved plants at the fourth to fifth true leaf, about 6 weeks after sowing. Choose the strongest seedlings.
7. **Plan for pollination.** Indoors, tomatoes need help: Tennessee recommends hand pollination three to four times a week, ideally at midday, with an electric toothbrush or a commercial pollinator.

For pH, EC by stage and the full feeding plan after transplant, see [hydroponic nutrients for tomatoes](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-tomatoes/).

## Troubleshooting seed and seedlings

| Problem | Check first | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Few seeds come up | Temperature | Hold 75–85 °F; above 90 °F germination falls | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Uneven germination | Normal variation | Sow 10–15% extra and thin | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Cubes stay soggy, seedlings weak | Too much water | Keep about ¼ in in the tray; evenly moist, not saturated | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Pale seedlings after the seed leaves | No feed yet | Start EC 0.5–1.0 mS/cm | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Tall, stretched seedlings | Light | Give strong light from emergence | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Plant outgrows the light | Wrong size class | Grow a micro-dwarf or compact cultivar next time | Utah State; UF/IFAS HS987 |
| Flowers but no fruit | Pollination or heat | Hand-pollinate 3–4 times a week; keep below 90 °F | Tennessee W 844-C; Utah State |
| Disease despite a "resistant" packet | Code covers one pathogen | Check which codes the packet lists | UF/IFAS HS1273 |

## What the sources don't say

- **Named compact cultivars beyond these.** The extension sources name the cultivars above; HydroRigor doesn't add others from seed catalogs, whose sizes and timings are the sellers' claims.
- **Days to germination.** Tennessee gives the temperature but not the number of days.
- **Micro-dwarf resistance.** The Florida papers list Fusarium race 1 and gray leaf spot resistance for 'Micro-Tina', 'Micro-Gemma' and 'Micro-Gold', but nothing for 'Micro-Tom'.
- **Results under LEDs at home.** The micro-dwarf figures come from Florida greenhouses; expect different timings under your light.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the best tomato seeds for hydroponics?

The ones that fit your space. For countertop units, micro-dwarfs such as 'Micro-Tom' and 'Micro-Tina' (about 4–10 in tall in a 1-liter pot in University of Florida trials). For Dutch buckets with a trellis, an indeterminate greenhouse cultivar such as 'Trust', the most common greenhouse cultivar in north Florida (UF/IFAS).

### Can I use ordinary garden tomato seed?

Yes, but check its mature size first. A full-size vine still needs height and support in hydroponics, and Utah State recommends smaller varieties indoors. Greenhouse-bred cultivars add resistance to leaf mold and powdery mildew (Tennessee).

### What temperature do tomato seeds need to germinate?

75–85 °F (24–29 °C), according to the University of Tennessee. Above 90 °F (32 °C) germination falls. A heat mat helps in a cool room.

### How long until my tomatoes fruit?

About 90–120 days from seed to first ripe fruit for standard tomatoes (Tennessee). Micro-dwarfs showed first fruit color 71–100 days after seeding in Florida greenhouse trials, faster in fall than in spring.

### Should I buy hybrid or heirloom seed?

For a first crop, hybrid. UF/IFAS says hybrids are bred for traits such as disease resistance, while heirlooms usually have little or no resistance, crack easily and often fail to set fruit in extreme heat. Heirlooms are worth trying once you have a system that works.

### What do the letters on a tomato seed packet mean?

They are disease-resistance codes: F for Fusarium wilt (with the race number), V for Verticillium wilt, ToMV for tomato mosaic virus, TYLCV for tomato yellow leaf curl virus and so on. Each covers only the disease it names. See the table above, or the [indoor hydroponic systems guide](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) to choose a system for your seed.
