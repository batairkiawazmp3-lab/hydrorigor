**Size a hydroponic reservoir by the system and the number of plants: about ¼–1 gallon (1–3.8 L) per plant for NFT, more than 1 gallon (4 L) per lettuce plant for deep water culture, 1–1.5 gallons (3.8–5.7 L) per lettuce plant for a Kratky tank, and about ½ gallon per square foot of tray for ebb and flow.** Those are the rules of thumb from Virginia Tech, the University of Kentucky, UF/IFAS and UMass Extension. Then check the tank against daily use: UF/IFAS says a mature tomato or cucumber drinks **2–2.5 quarts (1.9–2.4 L) a day** at peak harvest, so fruiting crops empty a small tank fast.

Bigger is safer. Virginia Tech advises **oversizing the reservoir** to buffer pH and nutrient swings, and Oregon State says a large volume **"acts as a thermal buffer."** The limit is space, weight and the cost of replacing the solution.

HydroRigor has not built or tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university or extension source listed at the end. Where we do arithmetic with those figures, we say so.

## Gallons per plant, system by system

No single figure fits every hydroponic system, because each holds a different share of its solution in the tank. Here is what each source gives.

**NFT.** Virginia Tech says **"¼ to 1 gallon of capacity per plant in the container is adequate,"** and gives a 50-plant system with a **25 gallon (95 L)** reservoir as an example, which is ½ gallon per plant. NFT channels hold only a thin film, so nearly all the solution sits in the tank. Our [NFT guide](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) covers slope and flow.

**Deep water culture.** Here the tank *is* the root zone. Kentucky's lettuce guide lists DWC as **high volume, more than 4 L (1 gal) per plant**, in a frame at least **12 in (30 cm)** deep. Virginia Tech gives pond depths of **4–12 in (10–30 cm)**, set by how much volume you want. See [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

**Kratky.** A Kratky tank is never topped up during the crop, so it must hold the plant's whole water use from transplant to harvest. UF/IFAS gives **1–1.5 gallons (3.8–5.7 L) per lettuce plant** from seed to harvest; Kratky's own CTAHR method fills tanks with **1.5–2 gallons (5.7–7.6 L) per plant** before planting. The [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) explains the air gap.

**Ebb and flow.** UMass says the holding tank should hold **about ½ gallon per square foot of bench area** (about 20 L per m², our conversion), because the tank must refill the tray every flood. Our [ebb and flow guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) gives flood timing.

**Drip and Dutch buckets.** None of our sources gives a gallons-per-plant reservoir figure for drip-fed tomatoes. Size these from daily use instead (next section).

## Check the size against daily water use

A tank sized by plant count can still run low if the plants are large or the room is warm. Two figures help.

**Per plant.** UF/IFAS says a mature fruiting tomato or cucumber **"will use 2–2.5 quarts of water per day during peak harvest"** (1.9–2.4 L); Missouri gives the same **2 to 2½ quarts** for high-tunnel tomatoes as the fruit grows. Young transplants use far less: UF/IFAS waters them with an ounce or two per event.

**Per area.** UMass gives a peak rule of **0.3–0.4 gallons per square foot of growing area per day** (12–16 L/m², our conversion) on the warmest day, and notes there have not been many studies of plant water use. A breeze of 5 mph can raise it by 20%.

**Leafy greens.** None of our sources gives a daily water figure for lettuce or basil. The Kratky figures are the nearest: 1–1.5 gallons over a whole lettuce crop.

**Putting numbers on it (our arithmetic).** Four mature tomatoes at 2–2.5 quarts each drink **2–2.5 gallons (7.6–9.5 L) a day**. Kentucky suggests topping up **every few days**; to go three days between top-ups, the tank must give up 6–7.5 gallons (23–28 L) and still keep the pump covered. A 10 gallon (38 L) tank would be nearly empty, so a fruiting system wants a larger tank or a daily top-up. The same four plants in their first weeks use only a fraction of that.

## Leave room for drain-back

In recirculating systems, part of the solution is always out in the channels, trays or lines. When the pump stops, all of it runs back. UMass says a flood-floor tank **"has to be large enough to hold the liquid from several bays"** run as one zone, and Virginia Tech slopes NFT benches so solution drains back to the reservoir. Neither gives a home-scale figure, so check it yourself: fill the system, switch the pump off, and mark the tank level once everything has drained. The tank must hold that without overflowing, and must still cover the pump intake while the system is running.

## Why a bigger tank is easier to manage

**pH and EC move more slowly.** Virginia Tech advises oversizing the reservoir **"to better buffer against pH and nutrient changes,"** and checking EC and pH **two to three times a week**. Alabama adds the other side: recirculating systems allow a smaller working volume, at the cost of closer watching.

**Temperature moves more slowly.** Oregon State says a large volume acts as a thermal buffer, and Virginia Tech insulates under DWC pond liners against temperature swings. Ohio State gives **68–72 °F (20–22 °C)** for leafy greens.

**Warm water holds less oxygen.** Ohio State and Missouri both say warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, and both put the optimum **over 6 ppm**. Kentucky gives **6–8 ppm** for DWC, while Alabama notes little research on optimum levels and recommendations of **more than 4 ppm**. Aiming above 6 ppm meets them all. A tank that stays cooler is the easiest way to keep oxygen up.

**Is there a maximum?** Oregon State says there is **"no strict limit to reservoir size."** The practical limits are floor strength, space and the solution you discard at each change.

## Topping up and changing the solution

| Source | Top up | Change the solution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kentucky CCD-CP-63 | Every few days | Full or partial change every 3–4 weeks |
| Minnesota | Keep NFT at capacity; check twice a week | Greens rarely; tomatoes once or twice a crop; NFT after every crop |
| UNH | With mixed solution, not plain water | Once or twice a year for a recirculating system; every couple of months for a very small raft |
| Ohio State | — | Keep for many crops; replace when problems arise |

The sources disagree widely on how often to change the solution. The common ground: **top up often, with nutrient solution at the normal strength** (Minnesota; UNH), and change the whole tank when EC and pH become hard to hold. For a small home system, Kentucky's 3–4 weeks is a cautious, easy schedule. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) covers when to change it, and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a recipe to your tank volume.

## Choosing the container

| Need | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opaque, with a lid | Algae grow in wet, light-exposed water | Minnesota; Oregon State EM 9455; Oklahoma State |
| Food-safe plastic | Holds the solution your food grows in | Minnesota; Kentucky |
| Not metal | Can react with the fertilizer and corrode | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Insulated or on the floor | Steadier temperature | Virginia Tech SPES-464; Oregon State |

Oklahoma State says to cover hydroponic tanks with lids or black plastic, and advises against algicides in hydroponics because they swing the pH. UMass encloses tanks and pipes in black plastic or paints them black.

## How to size and set up a reservoir, step by step

1. **Pick the rule for your system.** NFT ¼–1 gal per plant; DWC over 1 gal per lettuce; Kratky 1–1.5 gal; ebb and flow ½ gal per sq ft (Virginia Tech; Kentucky; UF/IFAS; UMass).
2. **Multiply by your plant count**, using the upper end for larger plants (Minnesota: plant size should dictate container size).
3. **Add daily use for fruiting crops.** 2–2.5 qt per mature tomato or cucumber a day, times the days between top-ups (UF/IFAS HS1274; Kentucky).
4. **Measure drain-back.** Fill, switch off the pump, mark the level; the tank must hold it (UMass).
5. **Round up.** A larger tank buffers pH, EC and temperature (Virginia Tech; Oregon State).
6. **Choose an opaque, lidded, food-safe container**, not metal (Minnesota; Virginia Tech).
7. **Mark the working level** on the side so top-ups are quick.
8. **Mix and check.** EC and pH two to three times a week (Virginia Tech); the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) gives crop ranges.
9. **Top up with full-strength solution** every few days (Kentucky; Minnesota).
10. **Change the solution** on a schedule, every 3–4 weeks to start (Kentucky).

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tank runs low every day | Large plants, warm room, tank too small | Bigger tank or daily top-ups; 2–2.5 qt per mature tomato | UF/IFAS HS1274; UMass |
| pH and EC swing between checks | Small volume | Oversize the reservoir; check 2–3 times a week | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Tank overflows when the pump stops | Not enough room for drain-back | Larger tank or lower working level | UMass |
| Green water, slime | Light reaching the solution | Opaque tank and lid; no algicides | Minnesota; Oklahoma State |
| Warm solution, limp roots | Small tank in a warm room; low oxygen | Larger volume, insulate; keep 68–72 °F | Oregon State; Ohio State |
| EC and pH hard to hold after many top-ups | Solution out of balance | Change the whole tank | Kentucky; Ohio State |

## Frequently asked questions

### How big should a hydroponic reservoir be?

About ¼–1 gallon per plant for NFT (Virginia Tech), more than 1 gallon per lettuce for DWC (Kentucky), 1–1.5 gallons per lettuce for Kratky (UF/IFAS) and ½ gallon per square foot of tray for ebb and flow (UMass).

### How many gallons per plant for tomatoes?

None of our sources gives a reservoir figure for drip tomatoes. A mature plant uses 2–2.5 quarts a day at peak (UF/IFAS; Missouri), so size the tank for several days of use.

### Is a bigger reservoir better?

Yes, within your space. Virginia Tech says it buffers pH and nutrient changes, and Oregon State says it steadies temperature. Oregon State sets no upper limit.

### How often should I top up the reservoir?

Every few days (Kentucky), using nutrient solution at the normal strength rather than plain water (Minnesota; UNH).

### How often should I change the solution?

Sources range from every 3–4 weeks (Kentucky) to once or twice a year (UNH). Every 3–4 weeks is a safe start for a small system.

### What should the reservoir be made of?

Opaque, food-safe plastic with a lid (Minnesota; Oklahoma State). Virginia Tech warns that metal can corrode.
