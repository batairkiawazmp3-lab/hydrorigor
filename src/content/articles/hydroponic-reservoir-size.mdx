**Size a hydroponic reservoir by the number of plants and the system you run.** Virginia Tech gives **¼ to 1 gallon (about 1–4 L) of reservoir per plant** for a recirculating NFT system, so 50 lettuce plants need about 25 gallons. A Kratky container holds all the water the crop will use, so it needs more: UF/IFAS puts it at **1–1.5 gallons per lettuce plant** from seed to harvest, and the University of Hawai‘i at **1.5–2 gallons**. A tomato needs its own **5-gallon (19 L) bucket** in deep water culture (DWC), and drinks **2–2½ quarts (about 2–2.4 L) a day** once fruit is growing, according to Missouri and Alabama Extension.

Bigger is safer, within reason. Virginia Tech says a larger reservoir buffers pH and nutrient swings, and Oregon State calls a large volume of water a thermal buffer. The cost is weight, floor space and more nutrient to mix at every change.

HydroRigor has not built or tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end, and where we did the arithmetic ourselves, we say so.

## Why reservoir size matters

The reservoir is the plants' whole supply of water and nutrients between your checks. Three things depend on its size.

**How long it lasts.** Plants drink from it every day. Missouri Extension says a tomato needs **2 to 2½ quarts of water a day** as its fruit grows, and Alabama gives **about 2 quarts**. A small tank under a big plant can run low in days.

**How steady it stays.** Virginia Tech's NFT guide says a larger reservoir **buffers pH and nutrient changes**: the same uptake by the roots shifts a big volume less than a small one. Oregon State says the same about heat in DWC: a large water volume is a **thermal buffer**, which is also why it has to sit on the ground.

**How much work it is.** Every full change means mixing a full tank. Minnesota Extension notes that how often you add water "will depend on how many plants you have", so more plants on the same tank means more frequent top-ups.

There is no single "best" size. Recirculating systems, where the solution flows past the roots and back, can run on a modest tank that you top up. Static systems, where the roots sit in the container, need the whole crop's water in the container itself.

## Reservoir size by system

### NFT and other recirculating channels

Virginia Tech's rule is **¼ to 1 gallon of capacity per plant**, with the example of a **50-plant system on a 25-gallon reservoir**, which works out to half a gallon each. Minnesota Extension's table-top NFT unit, six 4-foot channels holding **36 plants**, ran on a **25-gallon** reservoir, about 0.7 gallon per plant by our arithmetic, inside the same range. The solution is pumped up the channels and drains back, so the tank only has to hold enough to fill the channels and pipes, keep the pump covered and ride out a few days of uptake. Our [NFT system guide](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) covers flow and slope.

### Kratky (no pump)

In the Kratky method the container is the reservoir, and you add all the solution before planting. Three sources from the method's home institutions give overlapping amounts per lettuce plant:

| Source | Solution per lettuce plant | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UF/IFAS HS1488 | 1–1.5 gal (3.8–5.7 L) | Seed to harvest; 5-gal bucket with three plants |
| Hawai‘i CTAHR VC-1 | 1.5–2 gal (5.7–7.6 L) | Commercial tanks; 50 heads in 75–100 gal |
| Kratky, Acta Horticulturae 843 | 3–6 L (0.8–1.6 gal) used | Measured use per plant |

The figures differ because they measure different things: Kratky's 3–6 L is what plants **used**, while the other two are what to **put in**, with some margin. A 5-gallon bucket with three plants (UF/IFAS) gives about 1.7 gallons each, which fits both extension ranges. Minnesota Extension adds that this set-and-forget approach suits **short-lived crops such as lettuce**, harvested in 6 to 7 weeks. For the air gap and setup, see our [Kratky hydroponics guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/).

### Deep water culture

For a single large plant, UF/IFAS Volusia County's bucket design is **one 5-gallon bucket per plant**, with an air pump, for long-term crops such as tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers. For leafy greens on a floating raft, Kentucky gives a tank **at least 12 inches (30 cm) deep** with **3–4 plants per square foot**. By our arithmetic, a 12-inch-deep square foot holds about 7.5 gallons, so 2–2.5 gallons per lettuce plant. In a University of Florida basil trial, **four plants shared a 7.6-liter (2-gallon) unit** for eight weeks and still produced over 340 g per unit indoors, about half a gallon per plant. Our [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) covers aeration.

### Towers and ebb and flow

Oklahoma State's tower build uses a **45-gallon tank for 28 net pots**, about 1.6 gallons per plant by our arithmetic. Tower pumps run for long cycles (continuously for lettuce in Oklahoma State's build), so the tank must keep the pump covered while solution is up in the column; see our [hydroponic tower guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/). For flood-and-drain trays, UMass Extension gives a holding tank of **about ½ gallon per square foot of bench**, so a 2 × 4 ft (8 sq ft) tray needs about 4 gallons (15 L). The tank must hold the whole flood volume when it drains back; our [ebb and flow guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) has the flood timing.

## How to size a reservoir, step by step

These steps combine the figures above. They are a planning method, not a tested HydroRigor build.

1. **Count the plants** the system will hold when full.
2. **Pick the per-plant figure for your system** from the quick-answer table: ¼–1 gal for NFT, 1–2 gal for Kratky lettuce, 5 gal per tomato in DWC.
3. **Multiply.** Plants × gallons per plant is your minimum. Twenty NFT lettuce plants × ½ gal = 10 gal.
4. **Check how fast it will drain.** For tomatoes, multiply plants by 2–2½ quarts a day (Missouri). Four tomatoes use about 8–10 quarts, or 2–2½ gallons, a day by our arithmetic.
5. **Check the pump.** In a recirculating system, the pump must stay under water when the channels are full; Virginia Tech sizes the pump for head and friction.
6. **Round up to the next container size** you can actually lift, move or drain. Water weighs about 8.3 lb a gallon, so a full 25-gallon tank weighs over 200 lb (about 95 kg) by our arithmetic.
7. **Choose an opaque container with a lid.** Minnesota Extension says to use opaque materials and a lid on the reservoir to keep out light and algae.
8. **Mark the full line** so you can see the drop and top up before the pump or roots are exposed.

## Bigger vs smaller: the trade-off

| | Larger reservoir | Smaller reservoir | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH and nutrient drift | Slower | Faster | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Temperature swings | Slower | Faster | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Top-ups | Less often | More often | Minnesota Extension |
| Nutrient per change | More | Less | Arithmetic |
| Weight and floor space | More | Less | Arithmetic |

A small reservoir is not wrong; it just needs closer watching. Virginia Tech says to check EC and pH **two to three times a week** in NFT, whatever the size. Minnesota Extension says to check the NFT reservoir level **twice a week** and keep it at capacity.

## Topping up and changing the solution

Once the reservoir is the right size, how you maintain it matters as much. The sources give different change intervals, and this is the clearest source conflict on the page:

| Source | Advice | Context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General Hydroponics (manufacturer) | Change every 7–10 days; top off with fresh water between | Feedchart usage guide |
| University of Illinois | Replace every 2 to 3 weeks | Home hydroponics handouts |
| University of Kentucky | Top up every few days; replace every 3–4 weeks | Lettuce |
| Minnesota Extension | Leafy greens rarely need a change; tomatoes one or two | NFT; change after every crop |
| Solis-Toapanta et al. 2020 | Topping up grew as much basil as replacing | Eight-week DWC trial |

Our reading: the manufacturer's 7–10 days is the most cautious, the university advice runs from 2 weeks to a whole lettuce crop, and the Florida basil trial found no growth difference between replacing and topping up. For a small home system, top up as the level drops and replace the solution every 2 to 4 weeks, or sooner if EC or pH drifts out of range and won't come back. Minnesota Extension says water you add should carry nutrients **at the label strength**; General Hydroponics says to top off with **fresh water**. If EC is rising, top up with water; if it is falling, top up with solution. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) covers testing and [how to mix nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) covers the order.

## Keeping the reservoir cool, dark and oxygenated

Size affects temperature, and temperature affects oxygen and disease.

| Measure | Target | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solution temperature | 65–80 °F (18–27 °C) for small lettuce systems | UF/IFAS HS1422 |
| Solution temperature | 72–75 °F (22–24 °C) | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Solution temperature | Below 75 °F (24 °C) | General Hydroponics |
| Lettuce, cool room | Most growth at 71.4 °F (22 °C) solution | Purdue, 2019 |
| DWC dissolved oxygen | 7–10 ppm, at least 4 ppm | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Lettuce DWC oxygen | 6–8 ppm | Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| Lettuce pH and EC | pH 5.5–6.0; EC about 1.5 | Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| Leafy greens in NFT | pH 5.5–6.2; EC 1.2–2.0 | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |

The temperature figures overlap at **72–75 °F (22–24 °C)**, which is a sensible aim. In Purdue's trial, lettuce on **10-gallon** reservoirs in a 60 °F room grew most with the solution heated to **71.4 °F**, while heating was most efficient at **66.3 °F**. Minnesota Extension warns that higher temperatures speed evaporation and support algae and bacteria. Keep the tank out of direct light and away from heaters, and use white tubing where you can: Virginia Tech says white reflects heat and keeps solution cooler. For pH and EC ranges by crop, see the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).

## Troubleshooting reservoir problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Level drops fast | Too many plants for the tank, warm room | Top up more often or move to a larger tank | Minnesota Extension; Missouri G6462 |
| pH or EC swings day to day | Small volume | Larger reservoir; check 2–3 times a week | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Pump sucks air | Level below the intake | Keep the tank at capacity; mark the full line | Minnesota Extension |
| Green film or slime | Light reaching the solution | Opaque tank and a lid | Minnesota Extension |
| Warm solution | Small tank, lights or a warm room | Larger volume on the floor, shade, white tubing | Oregon State EM 9455; Virginia Tech |
| Kratky tank runs dry before harvest | Too little solution per plant | Use 1–2 gal per lettuce; add ½–1 in of water if needed | UF/IFAS; Hawai‘i VC-1 |
| Roots fully submerged in Kratky | Level raised after planting | Never raise the level; keep roots ⅓–½ in solution | Hawai‘i VC-1; Minnesota Extension |

## Frequently asked questions

### How many gallons of reservoir per plant?

For recirculating NFT, Virginia Tech gives ¼ to 1 gallon per plant. For Kratky lettuce, UF/IFAS gives 1–1.5 gallons and Hawai‘i 1.5–2 gallons, because the container holds all the crop's water. For a tomato in DWC, use one 5-gallon bucket per plant (UF/IFAS Volusia).

### Is a bigger hydroponic reservoir better?

Usually, up to what you can handle. Virginia Tech says a larger reservoir buffers pH and nutrient changes, and Oregon State calls a large volume a thermal buffer. It also costs more nutrient at every change and is heavier to move.

### What size reservoir for 10 plants?

For NFT leafy greens, 2.5–10 gallons by Virginia Tech's rule; for Kratky lettuce, 10–20 gallons; for tomatoes in DWC, ten 5-gallon buckets. These are our arithmetic from each source's per-plant figure.

### How much water does a hydroponic tomato use per day?

About 2 to 2½ quarts a day while fruit is growing, according to Missouri Extension; Alabama gives about 2 quarts. Both are greenhouse figures; a cooler room will use less.

### How often should I change the reservoir?

Sources range from every 7–10 days (General Hydroponics) to every 2–3 weeks (Illinois), 3–4 weeks (Kentucky) or once per lettuce crop (Minnesota). Top up between changes and replace sooner if pH or EC won't stay in range.

### Can a reservoir be too big?

Not for the plants, but a big tank is heavy and slow to change. At about 8.3 lb a gallon, a full 25-gallon tank weighs over 200 lb, so make sure the floor and any stand can take it and that you can drain it.

### Does the reservoir need to be opaque?

Yes. Minnesota Extension says to use opaque materials and a lid on the reservoir to prevent algae, and nutrient-rich water is prone to algal and bacterial growth.
