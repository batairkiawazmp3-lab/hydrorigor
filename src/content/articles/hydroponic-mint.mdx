Hydroponic mint grows well at a solution **pH of 5.5–6.5** and an **EC of 1.4–2.5 mS/cm**. In a University of Tabriz peppermint trial, EC 1.4 gave the most leaf and 40.5% more essential oil than EC 5.6, and a 2024 University of Turin trial grew three commercial mints in NFT-style channels at EC 2.0–2.5 and pH near 5.5. Keep the solution at **68–77 °F (20–25 °C)** with **7–9 ppm** of dissolved oxygen, as that trial did, and give the plants a daily light integral (DLI) of **at least 17 mol/m²/day**.

Start from rooted cuttings, not seed. The first cut comes **3 weeks after transplant** in a 2023 flood-table trial and **37–49 days** after transplant in the Turin channels; after that, mint regrows and can be cut again every **30–46 days**. Cut before it flowers, because bloom lowers the oil and flavor in the leaves (Utah State Extension).

## Quick answer: hydroponic mint targets

| Measure | Target | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH | 5.5–6.5 for most hydroponic crops; the NFT mint trial held about 5.5 | Missouri G6984; Hazrati et al. 2024 |
| EC | 1.4–2.5 mS/cm: 1.4 best for peppermint, 2.0–2.5 in the NFT trial | Tabatabaie and Nazari 2007; Hazrati et al. 2024 |

| Dissolved oxygen | 7–9 ppm in the NFT trial; 7–10 ppm for DWC | Hazrati et al. 2024; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |

The 6 in spacing is our conversion of the trial's 40 plants per square meter to a square grid.

## Which mint to grow

Peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*) and spearmint (*M. spicata*) have the most hydroponic research behind them. The Turin trial also grew a red-stemmed spearmint (*M. spicata* var. *rubra*), and a Chiang Mai University study grew Japanese mint (*M. arvensis*) in deep water culture. Utah State Extension lists many more garden types, including pineapple, ginger and water mint, but none has published hydroponic targets.

Mint is also one of the seven herbs Iowa State Extension says do best indoors, alongside basil, parsley, chives, oregano, cilantro and thyme. If you want to grow it with others, our [best herbs for hydroponics](/crops/best-herbs-for-hydroponics/) guide covers a shared solution.

**Grow it from cuttings.** North Dakota State University Extension says a mint stem roots when placed in a container of water until roots appear, and also roots easily in sand or potting mix. The research crops all started this way: the Greek floating-raft trials transplanted rooted shoots about 4 in (10 cm) long (Kasampalis et al. 2021), and the Chiang Mai study used 3 in (8 cm) cuttings rooted for two months in coconut coir dust (Janpen et al. 2019).

## Feeding: EC and pH

![Bar chart of peppermint leaf fresh weight per plant by solution EC: 715 grams at 0.7, 864 grams at 1.4, 498 grams at 2.8, 396 grams at 5.6 and 548 grams at 5.6 with added salt.](/images/guides/hydroponic-mint-ec-yield.svg)

*Peppermint leaf fresh weight per plant at five solution strengths, from Table 1 of Tabatabaie and Nazari (2007). The plants grew in perlite and vermiculite bags fed by timer, in a glasshouse at 82 °F (28 °C) by day and 68 °F (20 °C) by night.*

**EC.** The clearest mint EC trial comes from the University of Tabriz. Peppermint fed at EC 1.4 mS/cm produced **864 g** of leaf per plant, against 715 g at EC 0.7, 498 g at 2.8 and 396 g at 5.6 (Tabatabaie and Nazari 2007, Table 1). Essential oil content at 1.4 was 40.5% higher than at 5.6. The authors conclude 1.4 is the optimum for peppermint; their conference paper on the same work says the same (Tabatabaie et al. 2007).

**A conflict worth knowing.** Other trials grew mint well at higher EC. The Turin NFT trial held **2.0–2.5**, and the Chiang Mai DWC study ran Japanese mint at **1.9–2.0**. In the Greek floating-raft trials the solution started at **3.5** and, without a refill, drifted up to **6.2** within about 20 days (Aktsoglou et al. 2021); adding 20 mM of salt to that solution did not significantly change plant weight or height (Kasampalis et al. 2021). Those were short crops harvested after 24–42 days, while the Tabriz plants grew through a full season to flowering, which may explain why high EC cost so much more there.

**pH.** The trials do not agree. The Turin channels held pH **close to 5.5**; the Chiang Mai DWC study ran **6.5–7.0**; the Greek rafts were left at **7.2** and drifted to 7.5–7.7. None of them compared pH levels, so there is no mint-specific optimum. University of Missouri Extension's rule of thumb for most hydroponic crops, **5.5–6.5**, covers the two adjusted trials.

| Setting | Our pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EC, new cuttings | 1.4 mS/cm | Best peppermint yield and oil in the Tabriz trial |
| EC, established plants | 1.4–2.0 mS/cm | Between the Tabriz optimum and the Turin trial's lower bound |
| pH | 5.8–6.2 | Middle of Missouri's 5.5–6.5, between the NFT and DWC trials |
| Checks | Two to three times a week | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |

The Turin trial adds one more point: solutions with doubled phosphorus and potassium (4 and 12 mmol/L) gave about **803–813 g/m²** at the second cut, against about **1,200 g/m²** with 2 and 6 mmol/L, whatever the nitrogen level (Hazrati et al. 2024, Table 2). More fertilizer did not mean more mint. A general leafy-greens or herb formula is enough; see the [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/), the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).

## Temperature and oxygen

The Turin trial kept the nutrient solution at **20–25 °C (68–77 °F)** and dissolved oxygen at about **7–9 ppm**. Virginia Tech gives **7–10 ppm** for deep water culture, with 4 ppm as the minimum. Stirred-only Greek rafts swung from 65 to 83 °F (18.4–28.1 °C) over a 6-week crop (Kasampalis et al. 2021).

For air, the best mint-specific figure is from the Walters, Tarr and Lopez study: spearmint fresh mass rose by **60 g** as DLI rose from 6.1 to 19.4 mol/m²/day at **73 °F (23.0 °C)**, but by only **32 g** at **95 °F (35.1 °C)**. The North Carolina State spearmint study grew plants at 73 °F (22.8 °C) by day and 72 °F (22.0 °C) by night.

**What we recommend:** keep the room around 68–75 °F (20–24 °C) and the reservoir under 77 °F (25 °C). In DWC, run an air pump and stone around the clock; the [air pump guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/) covers sizing.

## Light

Mint responds strongly to light (see the Walters result above), and Virginia Tech gives **17 mol/m²/day** as the minimum for greens and herbs. Iowa State Extension's indoor herb advice is a timer at **12–16 hours** with lights **6–12 in (15–30 cm)** from the leaves. Utah State says garden mint grows best in full sun to partial shade.

To reach DLI 17 under a 16-hour day, the lamp needs to deliver about **295 µmol/m²/s** at leaf level; at 14 hours, about **337**. (DLI × 1,000,000 ÷ hours ÷ 3,600; our arithmetic.) None of the sources we read tested how day length affects mint flowering, so we cannot give a day length that holds off bloom; cutting regularly does that job instead.

## Spacing and system choice

The Turin trial planted about **40 plants per square meter**, which works out to roughly 6 in (16 cm) between plants on a square grid. Virginia Tech's NFT guide puts leafy greens on **8 in (20 cm)** centers.

| System | Fit | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFT channels | Very good | The Turin mint trial used a closed, recirculating channel system with three cuts; greens and herbs did best in NFT and DWC (Virginia Tech SPES-466) |
| Deep water culture | Very good | Japanese mint grew to harvest in aerated DWC (Janpen et al. 2019); Greek peppermint and spearmint in floating rafts |
| Ebb and flow | Good | Oregon State EM 9458 lists mint among common crops; the Walters spearmint trial used flood tables |
| Kratky jar | Unknown | No source we read tested mint without aeration |

**Our pick:** a small [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tub for one or two plants, or an [NFT system](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) if you grow several herbs. Utah State warns that mint spreads quickly and out-competes most garden plants. In a shared system, give mint its own end of the channel or its own tub, so its roots do not crowd slower herbs such as basil.

## Harvest: cut and regrow

![Timeline of hydroponic mint: root a cutting in water, transplant a 4 inch rooted shoot, first cut 3 weeks or 37 to 49 days after transplant, regrowth cuts every 30 to 46 days, and cut before it flowers.](/images/guides/hydroponic-mint-harvest-cycle.svg)

*Mint harvest cycle. Rooting: NDSU Extension. Transplant size: Kasampalis et al. (2021). First cut: Walters et al. (2023) and Hazrati et al. (2024). Regrowth interval: Hazrati et al. (2024). Flowering: Utah State Extension.*

Mint is a perennial, so one plant gives many harvests. In the Turin trial, the first cut came **37 days** after transplant for spearmint, **42** for red spearmint and **49** for peppermint. The plants regrew and were cut a second time **37–46 days** later and a third time **30–37 days** after that. The second cut was the largest in every treatment: about 1,200 g/m² in the best solutions, against 719–767 g/m² at the first cut in the same solutions (Hazrati et al. 2024, Table 2). The authors note that conventional practice is two harvests a year.

Faster crops are possible. Spearmint in the Walters flood-table trial was transplanted two weeks after cuttings were stuck and was ready **3 weeks** later. The Chiang Mai DWC mint was harvested at **60 days**, which the authors call the commercial harvest time for that crop.

**How much to cut.** Advice differs by setting. Utah State's garden advice is to start once plants are 3–4 in (8–10 cm) tall and cut stems to within 1 in (2.5 cm) of the soil, about three times a season, just before bloom. Iowa State's indoor advice for perennial herbs such as mint is gentler: take the top one-third. For a hydroponic plant you want to keep producing, we follow Iowa State between full cuts, and cut harder only when the plant is crowded or starting to flower. NDSU adds that the youngest, smallest leaves have the most flavor, and that removing flowers and pinching back stems keeps plants bushy.

## Step by step: set up a mint system

1. **Take cuttings.** Cut a stem from a healthy peppermint or spearmint plant, remove any leaves that would sit underwater, and stand it in water until roots appear (NDSU). The Greek trials transplanted rooted shoots about 4 in (10 cm) long.
2. **Move rooted cuttings into net pots.** Use clay pebbles or a rockwool cube; see [hydroponic growing media](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/).
3. **Mix the solution.** Bring EC to about 1.4 mS/cm and pH to 5.8–6.2.
4. **Space the plants.** About 6–8 in (16–20 cm) apart, at the end of a shared channel or in their own tub.
5. **Aerate and hold temperature.** Keep solution at 68–77 °F (20–25 °C) and, in DWC, run the air pump all the time.
6. **Set the light.** 12–16 hours a day, aiming for DLI 17; lamp 6–12 in (15–30 cm) above the leaves if the maker gives no intensity figure.
7. **Check pH and EC two to three times a week.** Top up with mixed solution; change it when EC creeps up, as it did from 3.5 to 6.2 in the unrefilled Greek rafts.
8. **First cut at 3–7 weeks.** Take the top one-third, or cut harder just before flowering.
9. **Cut again every 30–46 days.** The second cut is often the biggest.
10. **Store it.** Utah State and NDSU both give about a week in a glass of water in the refrigerator.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stunted plant, light green all over, yellowing between veins on lower leaves | Nitrogen shortage (seen within 2 weeks) | Check EC and refresh the solution; N-deficient spearmint had 78% less dry mass | Clade et al. 2025 |
| Purple stems, purpling and dead spots on lower leaves | Phosphorus shortage (seen after 2 weeks) | Refresh the solution with a complete formula | Clade et al. 2025 |
| Brown spots on lower leaves, yellow edges and downward curl above | Potassium shortage (seen at 2 weeks) | Refresh the solution; K-deficient plants had 55% less dry mass | Clade et al. 2025 |
| Small new leaves with inward-curled edges and fine dead specks | Calcium shortage (seen at 3 weeks) | Check that your formula supplies calcium | Clade et al. 2025 |
| Glossy yellowing of middle and lower leaves | Magnesium shortage (seen at 4 weeks) | Check that your formula supplies magnesium | Clade et al. 2025 |
| Newest leaves pale yellow-green, lower leaves stay green | Sulfur shortage | Refresh the solution; S-deficient plants had 40% less dry mass | Clade et al. 2025 |
| Slow growth, weak aroma | EC too high | Dilute toward EC 1.4 | Tabatabaie and Nazari 2007 |
| Leggy, pale stems | Too little light | Raise intensity toward DLI 17 | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Walters et al. 2023 |
| Orange or brown spots under the leaves | Mint rust | Remove affected stems; Utah State links rust to overwatering and overfeeding in soil | Utah State Extension |
| Flowers forming, flavor fading | Plant left too long | Cut now; bloom lowers oil content | Utah State Extension |
| Brown, slimy roots | Warm solution, low oxygen | Keep solution under 77 °F (25 °C) and oxygen at 7–9 ppm, the Turin trial's levels | Hazrati et al. 2024; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |

The symptom timings come from the North Carolina State spearmint study, which grew plants in sand culture with one nutrient left out at a time. Its photos are worth a look if you suspect a deficiency; our [nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/) covers the elements.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is mint easy to grow hydroponically?

Yes. Iowa State lists it among the best indoor herbs, cuttings root in water (NDSU) and it regrows after cutting. Keep EC moderate and cut before it flowers.

### What pH and EC does hydroponic mint need?

pH 5.5–6.5 (University of Missouri's general range; the Turin mint trial held about 5.5) and EC 1.4–2.5 mS/cm. Peppermint yielded most at EC 1.4 in the Tabriz trial; the Turin trial grew three mints well at 2.0–2.5.

### Can I grow mint from a supermarket sprig?

It may work: NDSU says mint cuttings root when stood in water until roots appear. None of the sources we read tested bought sprigs, so a cutting from a living plant, the method NDSU describes, is the safer start.

### How often can I harvest hydroponic mint?

In the Turin trial, every 30–46 days after the first cut, with three cuts over the season and the second the largest. Light trims of the top one-third (Iowa State) can be taken in between.

### Can mint share a system with basil?

Yes, but give it room. Basil prefers a warmer room (see our [hydroponic basil](/crops/hydroponic-basil/) guide), and mint spreads fast (Utah State), so put it at one end or in its own tub.
