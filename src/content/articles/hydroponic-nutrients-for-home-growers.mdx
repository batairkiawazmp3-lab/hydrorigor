The best hydroponic nutrient for a home grower is a **complete hydroponic formula** (one that supplies calcium, magnesium, iron and the other micronutrients, not just N-P-K), mixed to the **EC your crop needs** and held at **pH 5.5–6.5**. For most home growers that means a premixed one-part nutrient for lettuce and herbs, and a two- or three-part program for tomatoes and peppers, where the ratio changes as plants flower. Oklahoma State puts **lettuce at EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm and tomato at 2.0–4.0**, so a bottle that only gives a dose for "plants" is missing the most important number.

No brand wins on evidence. HydroRigor found no university trial that compares commercial hydroponic nutrients head to head for home growers, and we have not tested any. What the research does show is which features matter: completeness, strength, how the product suits your water, and whether you can mix it correctly. This guide turns those findings into a buying checklist. Every figure comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## Quick answer: what to look for

| Check | What good looks like | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complete formula | Lists calcium, magnesium, sulfur, iron and other micronutrients, not only N-P-K | Penn State Extension |
| Grade | Greenhouse-grade salts: higher purity and better solubility | Penn State Extension |
| Strength for leafy greens | Lettuce EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm; lettuce 100–150 ppm nitrogen | Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Penn State |
| Strength for fruiting crops | Tomato EC 2.0–4.0 mS/cm; tomato, pepper, cucumber 2.0–3.0 for much of the season | Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Univ. of Tennessee W 844-C |
| pH it will run at | 5.5–6.5 for most hydroponic crops | University of Missouri Extension |

| Mixing directions | Separate parts, an order, and "never combine concentrates" | Penn State; General Hydroponics |
| Seedling rate | Half rate until seedlings are established (one maker's directions) | Masterblend mixing instructions |

## The five types of hydroponic nutrient

Penn State describes three ways to feed a hydroponic crop: a single complete soluble fertilizer, a premixed program of several parts, or your own recipe from individual salts. Organic liquids are a fourth route, and the format (powder or liquid) cuts across all of them.

| Type | Example | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-part complete | A single bottle or bag with calcium and micronutrients | Greens and herbs, first grows | Check it really has calcium and magnesium |
| Two- or three-part liquid | General Hydroponics Flora series (Micro, Gro, Bloom) | Changing the ratio by stage | Mix each part into water separately |
| Dry base plus calcium | Masterblend 4-18-38 + calcium nitrate + Epsom salt | Larger batches, tomatoes | Needs a scale; dissolve each in turn |
| Your own recipe | Calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, MKP and micronutrients | Experienced growers | High-precision scale, water analysis |
| Organic liquid | Fish, seaweed or amino-acid based | Growers who must stay organic | Smaller yields in trials |

**Why multi-part products exist.** Calcium reacts with phosphates and sulfates to form solids roots can't use (University of Missouri), so concentrated calcium has to be kept apart from the rest. That is why Penn State's tomato program keeps calcium nitrate in one stock tank and 4-18-38 with magnesium sulfate in another, and why General Hydroponics tells users never to combine its concentrates undiluted.

**Why premixed is the beginner's choice.** Purdue Extension says a premixed fertilizer "might be perfect for beginner growers", while experienced growers can calculate their own formulations. A recipe gives more control, but Penn State notes it needs a high-precision scale for the micronutrients and a water test to subtract what your tap already supplies.

## What "complete" means on a label

Penn State lists 17 elements plants need. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come from air and water; the other 14 must come from the nutrient, because in hydroponics there is no soil to fill gaps. The three numbers on the front of the pack cover only nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.

- **Calcium and magnesium.** Penn State warns that magnesium and calcium may not be included in complete soluble fertilizers. That is why its tomato program adds calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate to a 4-18-38 base, and why Masterblend's directions use three components.
- **Iron and micronutrients, preferably chelated.** Penn State explains that chelates keep metals such as iron available to plants even when pH changes; iron, manganese and zinc are easily made unavailable. Iron shortage shows as yellowing between the veins of new leaves, often caused by high pH.
- **Greenhouse grade.** Penn State recommends greenhouse-grade fertilizers for their higher purity and greater solubility than standard grades.
- **Nitrogen form.** Most hydroponic nitrogen is nitrate. Penn State notes ammonium can cause excessive leafy growth, especially in poor light, and ammonium toxicity; indoor growers under modest lights have reason to prefer a nitrate-based product.
- **Balance, not just quantity.** Too much of one nutrient blocks another: Penn State gives excess potassium reducing calcium or magnesium uptake. An EC meter cannot catch this, because EC shows the total salt, not the balance (University of Missouri).

## Strength matters as much as the brand

The same nutrient can grow small or large plants depending on how strong you mix it. In a University of Nevada, Reno trial, lettuce and bok choy were grown in float beds on General Hydroponics' Flora series at 400 ppm and at 1,200 ppm, the manufacturer's commercial rate, alongside a home-made organic solution.

| Crop | Full rate (1,200 ppm) | One-third rate (400 ppm) | Organic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bok choy | 619.1 g | 384.7 g | 239.8 g |
| Butterhead lettuce | 150.9 g | 131.3 g | 73.8 g |
| Nevada lettuce | 197.1 g | 133.1 g | 96.2 g |

*Mean wet weight per plant, University of Nevada, Reno (Ferguson et al., 2014), second crop cycle. pH 6.0–6.4.*

![Bar chart of mean weight per plant: bok choy 619.1 g at full rate, 384.7 g at one-third rate and 239.8 g organic; butterhead lettuce 150.9, 131.3 and 73.8 g; Nevada lettuce 197.1, 133.1 and 96.2 g.](/images/guides/hydroponic-nutrient-strength-yield.svg)

*The full label rate grew the largest plants of every crop; the organic solution grew the smallest. In the first cycle, 300, 400 and 500 ppm gave no significant difference.*

More is not always better, though. Purdue grew 24 lettuce varieties at EC 1.3, 2.0, 2.9 and 3.9 dS/m: growth was higher at 1.3 and 2.0 and lower at 2.9 and 3.9, so Purdue recommends EC 1.3 for lettuce. Nebraska Extension's simple lettuce kit says never to use more than the labeled fertilizer rate. The practical answer is to follow the label, then check the result with an EC meter against your crop's range:

| Crop group | EC (mS/cm) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lettuce | 1.2–1.8 (Purdue recommends 1.3; Kentucky about 1.5, 1.0 for seedlings) | Oklahoma State; Purdue HO-309; Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| Basil | 1.0–1.6 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Strawberry | 1.8–2.2 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Tomato | 2.0–4.0 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Tomato, pepper, cucumber | 2.0–3.0 for much of the season; seedlings 0.5–1.0 | Univ. of Tennessee W 844-C |

Oklahoma State and Tennessee differ on tomato (up to 4.0 versus up to 3.0). HydroRigor's reading: stay at 2.0–3.0 in a small home reservoir, where Tennessee notes large plants throw the solution out of balance quickly. For more crops, see [hydroponic nutrients for vegetables](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-vegetables/) and, for feeding by stage, [hydroponic nutrients for tomatoes](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-tomatoes/).

## Your water decides more than the label

A nutrient is designed around an assumed water. The Ohio State tomato recipes are written for reverse osmosis water, and Purdue notes that some compound blends were developed for calcium-rich water and may need calcium nitrate or magnesium sulfate added when your water is softer. Test your tap before you buy. The University of Missouri gives these limits for source water:

| Property | Suitable range | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EC | 0.2–0.8 dS/m, ideally below 1 | University of Missouri Extension |
| pH | 5.5–7.0 | University of Missouri Extension |
| Alkalinity | 40–160 ppm as CaCO3 | University of Missouri Extension |
| Sodium | Below 50 ppm | University of Missouri Extension |
| Calcium | Below 150 ppm | University of Missouri Extension |
| Boron | Below 0.5 ppm | University of Missouri Extension |

Oklahoma State adds that alkalinity above 75 ppm pushes pH up, which can be countered with an acidic fertilizer, phosphoric or citric acid, or reverse osmosis. If your tap is hard, a nutrient made for soft or RO water may oversupply calcium; if you use RO or rainwater, choose a complete formula that includes calcium and magnesium rather than one that relies on the water for them.

## Powder or liquid?

Mississippi State Extension found "no agronomic difference" in the efficiency of liquid and dry fertilizers at the same nutrient rate. The choice is about handling: liquids are measured by volume and suit one small reservoir; powders store compactly and cost less to ship but need a scale. Masterblend's directions, for example, give 2.4 g of 4-18-38, 1.5 g of Epsom salt and 2.4 g of calcium nitrate per gallon (3.8 L), each dissolved in turn. The [powder versus liquid guide](/nutrients-water/powder-vs-liquid-hydroponic-nutrients/) covers storage and scale accuracy.

## Organic nutrients: expect smaller harvests

Organic hydroponic nutrients work, but every trial HydroRigor found grew less than mineral nutrients. In a 2024 Dutch bucket trial (Alneyadi et al.), romaine averaged 175.1 g with mineral fertilizer against 159.8 g and 157.9 g for the best two organic products. In a 2025 Texas A&M NFT trial, the best organic scheme reached 40.3% of the mineral yield. In the Nevada trial above, the home-made organic solution yielded less than even the one-third-strength mineral solution, partly because rock phosphate barely dissolved. Choose organic for its certification, not its yield, and read [organic hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/organic-hydroponic-nutrients/) for the fixes.

## How to choose and start a nutrient, step by step

1. **Decide your crop group.** Greens and herbs, or fruiting crops. This sets the EC you need and whether a multi-part program is worth it.
2. **Test your water** for EC, pH and alkalinity (many water utilities publish a report). Compare with the Missouri limits above.
3. **Pick the type** from the decision card: one-part for greens, two- or three-part or a dry base plus calcium for fruiting crops and larger batches.
4. **Check the label** for the six items on the checklist, especially calcium, magnesium and chelated iron.
5. **Buy a pH meter and an EC meter.** You cannot judge strength without one; see [how to choose a pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/). Calibrate EC with the 1.41 mS/cm standard (Michigan State).
6. **Mix at the label rate,** scaled to your reservoir with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/). Add parts to the water one at a time in the maker's order; use half rate for seedlings if the directions say so. The [mixing guide](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) shows each step.
7. **Read EC first, then adjust pH** to 5.5–6.5 (Oklahoma State adjusts pH only after EC is in range).
8. **Check daily and replace on schedule:** Oklahoma State suggests replacing the solution about every two weeks; Tennessee says one to two weeks for fruiting crops in small tanks; Kentucky three to four weeks for lettuce.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cloudy solution or white sludge | Calcium mixed with phosphate or sulfate concentrate | Remix: add each part to the water separately; keep calcium apart in stocks |
| Yellowing between veins of new leaves | Iron unavailable, often high pH | Bring pH to 5.5–6.5; choose a product with chelated iron |
| Tipburn on lettuce, blossom-end rot on tomato | Calcium not reaching growing tissue | Check the formula includes calcium; check EC and airflow |
| Plants small and pale at "correct" dose | Mixed too weak for the crop | Measure EC and bring it into the crop range |
| Leaf edges burn, plants wilt | Too strong (excess salts) | Dilute with water; follow the label rate |
| pH climbs every day | Alkaline tap water (above 75 ppm) | Acidify, or use RO water with a complete formula |
| Soft, leafy growth under weak lights | High ammonium share | Prefer a nitrate-based formula |

## FAQ

**What is the best hydroponic nutrient for beginners?**
A premixed, complete one-part nutrient for greens and herbs, or a premixed multi-part program for fruiting crops. Purdue says a premixed fertilizer "might be perfect for beginner growers." Check it lists calcium, magnesium and iron.

**Can I use regular garden fertilizer in hydroponics?**
Only if it is complete. Penn State notes that some complete soluble fertilizers leave out calcium and magnesium, which hydroponic plants cannot get from soil, and recommends greenhouse-grade salts for purity and solubility. A product made for hydroponics is the safer choice.

**Are General Hydroponics and Masterblend good?**
Both are complete programs whose documentation gives parts, rates and a mixing order. No university trial HydroRigor found compares them, so we do not rank them. The Nevada trial used General Hydroponics' Flora series and found the full label rate grew the largest plants.

**Do I need Cal-Mag?**
Only if your base nutrient or water falls short. Penn State's tomato program adds calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate to a base without them; a complete formula used with RO water may already include both. Read the label before adding anything.

**How strong should I mix it?**
Follow the label, then check EC: lettuce 1.2–1.8 mS/cm, basil 1.0–1.6, tomato 2.0–4.0 (Oklahoma State). Purdue found lettuce grew less at EC 2.9 and 3.9 than at 1.3 and 2.0.

**How long does a mixed solution last?**
Replace it about every two weeks (Oklahoma State), one to two weeks for fruiting crops in small tanks (Tennessee), or three to four weeks for lettuce (Kentucky). Top up between changes. See [hydroponic nutrient solution](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) for when to change it.
