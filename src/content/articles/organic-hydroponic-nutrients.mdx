Yes, you can grow hydroponically with organic nutrients, but expect a smaller harvest unless you manage the solution differently. In university trials, the best organic solutions gave lettuce **about 9–10% less fresh weight** than a mineral fertilizer in a perlite Dutch bucket system (Alneyadi et al. 2024), strawberries in vertical towers yielded **about 15% less** (Wortman et al. 2016), and lettuce in NFT channels reached only **23.5–40.3% of the mineral yield** (Liu et al. 2025). The reasons are known: nitrogen arrives in a form plants cannot use yet, microbes and biofilm compete with roots for oxygen, and most organic products supply far too little **calcium (target 200–300 mg/L)** and **magnesium (30–50 mg/L)**.

The fixes are also known. **Aerate hard, run pumps on a timer with long rest periods, add calcium and magnesium, and choose a system with growing media** rather than bare roots in a thin film. Below: what "organic" does and does not mean, what each study found, a step-by-step setup and a troubleshooting table.

## What "organic hydroponic nutrients" means

The label describes where the ingredients come from: fish, seaweed, humic material, plant extracts and similar sources, rather than mined or manufactured salts. It does not mean the crop is certified organic. USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service explains that operations selling products as organic generally must be certified by an accredited certifier, with limited exemptions. If you plan to sell, talk to a certifier before you buy inputs. For a home grower, the question is simpler: will this bottle feed my plants as well as a mineral nutrient?

Plants answer that the same way in any system. They absorb nutrients as dissolved mineral ions, such as nitrate, potassium and calcium. A mineral hydroponic fertilizer supplies those ions directly; that is why it works the moment it dissolves (see the [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/) for what each element does). Organic fertilizers supply many nutrients locked in proteins and other compounds, and microbes have to break them down first. Alneyadi and colleagues put it plainly: organic solutions are less available, and the release of nutrients has to match what the crop needs. In soil, a huge microbial community does that work slowly and buffers the results. In a reservoir, it happens in the same water the roots are breathing.

## Why organic solutions struggle in water

The Texas A&M NFT lettuce study (Liu, Masabni and Niu 2025) measured what goes wrong, and the problems line up with the yield losses.

![Four problems with organic hydroponic solutions and their fixes: nitrogen arrives as organic N that microbes turn into ammonium and nitrite, harmful from 5 mg/L, fixed by aeration and rest between flows; biofilm takes oxygen, fixed by intermittent flow and cleaning; calcium and magnesium run at about 3 mg/L against targets of 200–300 and 30–50 mg/L, fixed with a supplement; a bottled inoculant at 3.13 mL/L reduced growth, so skip it.](/images/guides/organic-nutrient-problems-fixes.svg)

*Four problems and their fixes, all from Liu, Masabni and Niu (2025). The calcium and magnesium targets are the ones the authors cite from earlier research.*

**1. Nitrogen in the wrong form.** The organic solutions held 91–214 mg/L of ammonium and 43–103 mg/L of nitrite, against about 1 mg/L of each in the mineral (Hoagland) solution. The authors cite research showing nitrite as low as 5 mg/L can harm lettuce. Microbes convert organic nitrogen to ammonium, then to nitrite, then to nitrate. That last step needs oxygen and time, and in the trial it did not keep up. A side effect showed in the Dutch bucket study: leaf nitrate was only 3.2–8.7 mg/kg dry weight with organic solutions versus 259.8 with the mineral one (Alneyadi et al. 2024). Low leaf nitrate is a plus for a leafy green, but it also tells you how little nitrate the plants were getting.

**2. Biofilm and oxygen.** Slime formed in the organic reservoirs and channels and coated the roots, most heavily under continuous flow. The authors suggest it competed with roots for oxygen. Microbes eating organic matter use oxygen too.

**3. Too little calcium and magnesium.** The organic fertilizer in the NFT trial supplied only about 3.18 mg/L each of calcium and magnesium. Lettuce shoots ended at 0.29% calcium versus 1.23% with mineral nutrients, and 0.17% magnesium versus 0.35%. The Dutch bucket study found the same pattern: leaf calcium of 4.58–6.87 mg/g with organic solutions against 11.90 with the mineral control, and magnesium of 1.43–2.01 versus 2.88 mg/g. Those authors blamed the lower growth on poor availability of calcium, potassium and magnesium.

**4. Added microbes did not rescue it.** A commercial microbial inoculant at 3.13 mL/L did not help in the NFT trial; it reduced growth compared with the same flow scheme without it.

## What the research shows

Three studies, three systems, one pattern: the more the roots depend on the water alone, the bigger the gap.

| Study | Crop and system | Organic result vs mineral |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alneyadi et al. 2024 | Romaine lettuce, zero-runoff Dutch buckets with perlite, no pumps, 60 days | 159.8 g (fish-based) and 157.9 g (alginic-based) vs 175.1 g; two other organic products lower |
| Wortman et al. 2016 | Strawberries, vertical towers in a high tunnel, two years | About 15% lower yield |
| Liu et al. 2025 | Lettuce, NFT channels | 23.5% (continuous flow), 32% (15 min on, 45 off), 40.3% (15 on, 105 off) |

![Bar chart: with organic nutrients, Dutch bucket lettuce reached about 91% and 90% of the mineral yield, tower strawberries about 85%, and NFT lettuce 40.3%, 32% and 23.5% depending on flow.](/images/guides/organic-hydroponic-yield-vs-mineral.svg)

*Organic yield as a share of the mineral control. The 91% and 90% are our arithmetic from Alneyadi et al. (159.8 g and 157.9 g against 175.1 g); "about 85%" restates Wortman et al.'s "about 15% lower".*

The Dutch bucket result is the encouraging one. It used 40 cm (16 in) pots holding 25 L (about 6.6 gal) of perlite, fed from a 100 L (about 26 gal) tank by gravity, with no pump. Perlite gives roots air and gives microbes surface to live on, away from the roots' oxygen supply. The Wortman strawberry towers also used media, and the authors concluded organic hydroponic production appears feasible. NFT, where bare roots sit in a thin, moving film, did worst.

There are trade-offs beyond weight. In the Dutch bucket trial, every organic solution produced fewer leaves, smaller leaf area and shorter plants. Two organic treatments did produce lettuce with more phenolic compounds (3.22–3.36 g per 100 g dry weight versus 2.28), which the authors count as a nutritional plus.

**What we did not find:** no study here tested organic nutrients in home deep water culture or Kratky. We cannot give you a yield figure for those systems, and we would treat any product claim for them with caution until a trial exists.

## Organic or mineral: how they compare

| | Mineral hydroponic nutrient | Organic hydroponic nutrient |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nutrients ready to use | Yes, as soon as they dissolve | After microbes break them down |
| Yield in trials | Reference (100%) | About 90% in media; 23.5–40.3% in NFT |
| Calcium and magnesium | Included in complete products | Often far too low; supplement |
| Oxygen demand | Roots only | Roots plus microbes and biofilm |
| Reservoir cleanliness | Clear water | Biofilm, sediment, smell possible |
| What EC tells you | Total dissolved salts | Only the dissolved share; organic nitrogen not yet released does not register |
| Sell as organic | No | Only with certification |

**Our recommendation:** if you want the most food from a home system, use a complete mineral hydroponic nutrient and follow the [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/). If organic inputs matter to you, they can work: choose a media-based system, plan for roughly 10% less, and follow the steps below. This rests on the three trials above; none of them found an organic solution that matched mineral yield.

## How to set up an organic hydroponic system

1. **Pick a system with growing media.** Dutch buckets or other containers of perlite came closest to mineral yields (Alneyadi et al. 2024). Avoid NFT for organic nutrients unless you can run it on a timer.
2. **Choose a liquid organic product that lists calcium and magnesium.** In the Dutch bucket trial, fish-based and alginic-based liquids did best, but the study tested regional products, so no brand recommendation follows from it. Check the label for calcium; most organic products in the studies supplied very little.
3. **Plan a calcium and magnesium supplement.** Liu and colleagues advise supplementing, citing targets of 200–300 mg/L calcium and 30–50 mg/L magnesium. If you want to stay organic, ask your certifier or the product maker which calcium and magnesium sources qualify; we have not found a trial that tested specific ones in hydroponics.
4. **Mix in clean water and record the reading.** Start with water low in salts; the University of Missouri suggests source water EC of 0.2–0.8 dS/m. The Dutch bucket trial set every solution to EC 2.0 mS/cm and checked it every two days. No source gives an organic-specific EC target, and EC only counts what has dissolved, so treat EC as a consistency check rather than a measure of feeding. For comparison, Purdue recommends EC 1.3 for lettuce with mineral nutrients.
5. **Set pH.** The trials used 5.5–5.8 (Dutch bucket, adjusted three times a week) and 6.5 (NFT, set weekly). Both sit inside the 5.5–6.5 range the University of Missouri gives for most hydroponic crops. Organic solutions can drift as microbes work, so check more often than you would with mineral nutrients. The [pH meter guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) covers calibration.
6. **Aerate hard.** Run an air pump and stone in every reservoir. Liu and colleagues recommend aerating organic solutions, and the oxygen demand of microbes is on top of what roots need.
7. **Run the pump on a timer with long rests.** In the NFT trial, 15 minutes on and 105 minutes off raised fresh weight 71% over continuous flow and saved pump energy.
8. **Skip bottled microbe additives** unless a trial in a system like yours supports them; one reduced growth in the NFT study.
9. **Keep the system clean.** Wipe out biofilm between crops and check roots weekly: white and firm is good, brown and slimy is not.

Liu and colleagues also suggest a biofilter, as aquaponic systems use, to let microbes convert ammonium and nitrite to nitrate before the solution reaches the roots. That is a bigger project than most home growers want, but it addresses the nitrogen problem at its source.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow growth, small leaves | Nutrients not yet released; low calcium, potassium, magnesium | Supplement calcium and magnesium; expect some yield gap (Alneyadi et al. 2024) |
| Brown leaf edges or tipburn on new leaves | Calcium shortage, measured in both lettuce trials | Add a calcium supplement (Liu et al. 2025) |
| Slime on roots, channels or reservoir walls | Biofilm from organic matter | Intermittent flow, more aeration, clean between crops |
| Brown, smelly roots | Low oxygen from microbes and biofilm | Aerate, shorten pump run times, rinse system |
| Plants worse after adding a microbe product | Inoculant competing or adding load | Stop the additive (Liu et al. 2025) |
| pH drifting between checks | Microbial activity changing the solution | Check at least three times a week, as the Dutch bucket trial did (Alneyadi et al. 2024) |
| EC steady but plants hungry | EC misses organic nitrogen not yet released | Judge by growth and leaf color, not EC alone |
| Clogged emitters or pump intake | Sediment from organic product | Filter the intake; prefer fully soluble liquids |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you use organic nutrients in hydroponics?

Yes. Lettuce in a perlite Dutch bucket system reached about 90–91% of mineral yield with the best organic solutions (Alneyadi et al. 2024), and strawberries in towers about 85% (Wortman et al. 2016). Results in bare-root NFT were much worse.

### What is the best organic hydroponic nutrient?

No trial we found compares widely sold brands. In the Dutch bucket study, fish-based and alginic-based liquids outperformed humic-based ones. Whatever you choose, check calcium and magnesium and plan to supplement them.

### Do organic hydroponic nutrients clog systems?

They can. Organic solutions grew biofilm in reservoirs and channels in the NFT study, and particles can block small emitters. Filter pump intakes and clean between crops.

### Can I use organic nutrients in DWC or Kratky?

We found no published trial for home deep water culture or Kratky with organic nutrients. Both keep roots in still solution with no media, which is close to the conditions that did worst in research, so we suggest mineral nutrients for them. See the [DWC guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) and [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/).

### Is hydroponic produce grown with organic nutrients certified organic?

Not automatically. Certification applies to the operation, through an accredited certifier (USDA AMS). Organic inputs alone do not let you label a crop organic.

### What EC should an organic nutrient solution be?

No source gives an organic-specific target. The Dutch bucket trial used 2.0 mS/cm for lettuce. Because organic nitrogen does not register until microbes release it, EC under-reads how much food is in the tank.
