**The best hydroponic growing medium depends on the system.** For ebb and flow and other flood beds, use **expanded clay pebbles** or gravel: Illinois calls clay "great" for ebb and flow, and Oklahoma State gives it a near-neutral pH (about **7.0**), fast drainage and years of reuse. In deep water culture, Kratky and NFT, plants usually need no loose medium at all, only the plug they started in. To start seeds and cuttings, use **rockwool cubes, foam or peat/coir plugs** (Virginia Tech). For drip buckets and bags, use **perlite, coco coir or a blend**: fine coir holds **at least twice the water** of perlite (UF/IFAS), so it needs fewer, longer waterings.

Two media need preparing first. **Rockwool** starts at **pH 7–8** (Oklahoma State) or **7–8.5** (UF/IFAS), so soak it in solution adjusted to **pH 5.5–6.0** before use. **Coco coir** carries sodium and other salts, so rinse it or buy it buffered. UF/IFAS adds that loose, fine media such as coir should stay out of recirculating systems, where particles clog pumps and lines.

HydroRigor has not tested these media; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## What makes a good hydroponic medium

A medium in hydroponics does not feed the plant; the nutrient solution does. Its jobs are to hold the plant up, hold some water, and let air reach the roots. UF/IFAS (EP623) and Kentucky (CCD-TA-02) judge media on the same handful of properties:

- **Water-holding capacity.** How much solution the medium keeps after draining. Kentucky defines it as field capacity minus the permanent wilting point.
- **Aeration.** How much air the pores hold once drained. Roots need oxygen; a waterlogged medium suffocates them.
- **pH.** The medium can shift the solution's pH, which Kentucky puts at **5.5–6.5** for hydroponic production.
- **Cation exchange capacity (CEC).** How strongly the medium holds nutrients. Most hydroponic media are close to inert, which is what you want: the solution stays in control.
- **Reuse and disposal.** Some media last years; others break down or can't be recycled.

The trade-off runs through every comparison below: the more water a medium holds, the less air it has, and the less often you need to water. Oklahoma State's figure for rockwool is **18–25% air**; Illinois gives it **95% pore space and 80% water-holding capacity**, so most of its pores hold water.

### Expanded clay pebbles (LECA)

Kiln-fired clay balls, **1–18 mm** across (UF/IFAS). Oklahoma State gives a pH **about 7.0**, says they release almost no nutrients, drain and dry fast, and last **many years** if washed and sterilized between crops. They suit ebb and flow, where Illinois calls them "great", and Oregon State lists them for Dutch buckets. They can also fill a net pot around a plug, though no source we read tested that use. The downsides: UF/IFAS calls them **very expensive**, mostly a hobby medium, and Oklahoma State notes they **float for the first few months**. Rinse the dust off before use. Our [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) and [ebb and flow guide](/systems/ebb-and-flow-hydroponics/) show where they sit, and our [LECA guide](/equipment/leca-hydroponics/) covers clay pebbles in detail.

### Rockwool

Spun from molten rock (Kentucky). It holds a lot of water and is sterile and uniform, which is why it is the standard seed-starting cube. Its catch is pH: **7.0–8.0** at the start in Oklahoma State's figures, **7–8.5** in UF/IFAS's. Both say to bring it down: Oklahoma State to **5.5–6.0**, by soaking in low-pH solution. It isn't biodegradable, and Kentucky lists skin irritation, so wear gloves when cutting it. On reuse the sources disagree: UF/IFAS says rockwool **cannot be reused**, while Oklahoma State says slabs can be **steam sterilized** between crops and last **three to four years**. For a home grower without a steamer, treat cubes as single-use.

### Coco coir

Made from coconut husks; sold as compressed bricks that **expand up to six times** when soaked (Oklahoma State). UF/IFAS gives a pH of **5.5–6.8**. It holds a lot of water: UF/IFAS HS1274 says fine coconut fiber holds **at least twice the water** of standard perlite or pine bark, and a **3-gallon bag holds about 1 gallon** of water. Oklahoma State warns it is rich in **sodium and chlorine**; Kentucky says it may need **buffering** to remove excess salts. Rinse it well, or buy pre-washed, buffered coir. UF/IFAS says neither fine nor chunky coir belongs in **recirculating** systems.

### Perlite

Heat-expanded volcanic glass. Oklahoma State says it holds **three to four times its weight** in water and has some of the best oxygen levels of any medium. It is sterile and neutral, with no CEC (UF/IFAS). Two cautions: the dust is a respiratory hazard, so wet it and wear a mask; and it **floats and washes away** in flood-and-drain systems (Oklahoma State, Kentucky). It is the usual fill for Dutch buckets (UF/IFAS 2025). UF/IFAS HS169 warns that reusing **unsterilized** perlite is risky, because old roots can carry disease. See our [Dutch bucket guide](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/).

### Vermiculite, peat and foam

**Vermiculite** holds water and nutrients well (pH **7–7.5**, UF/IFAS) but can waterlog and compact when used alone (Oklahoma State, Kentucky); it works best mixed in. **Peat** is acidic (**pH 3.8–4.5**) and holds **up to 10 times its dry weight** in water (Oklahoma State); growers add lime to raise the pH, and Kentucky notes it is non-renewable. **Foam (Oasis) cubes** are neutral and need no pre-soaking (Oklahoma State), but UF/IFAS says they are not ideal for slow-growing crops and Oklahoma State says loose foam can clog pump filters.

## Media in trials: what grew best

Most media pages rank by opinion. Two trials compared media directly:

| Trial | Crop and system | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yang et al. 2022 | Cucumber, Dutch buckets | Medium pine bark yielded 23.9% more than perlite; coir and peat leached less | Yang et al. 2022 |
| Wortman et al. 2016 | Strawberry, vertical towers | Highest yield in perlite mixed with coco coir or vermiculite | Wortman et al. 2016 |

In the cucumber trial, leachate from coir was **24.0%** lower than from perlite and from peat **38.1%** lower, because they hold more water; the authors concluded organic substrates can give equal or higher yield than perlite. Neither trial tested lettuce in net pots, so they say nothing about clay pebbles.

## How to prepare and use your medium, step by step

These steps follow the sources above. They are a starting point, not a tested HydroRigor method.

1. **Match the medium to the system** using the quick-answer table. Keep loose, fine media out of NFT and other recirculating systems (UF/IFAS).
2. **Rinse clay pebbles, gravel and perlite** until the water runs clear, to remove dust, and wear a mask when pouring perlite (UF/IFAS; Oklahoma State).
3. **Soak rockwool** in water or solution adjusted to **pH 5.5–6.0** before sowing (Oklahoma State).
4. **Wash or buy buffered coir.** Rinse compressed bricks thoroughly; Oklahoma State adds calcium and magnesium to make up for what the salts displace.
5. **Check pH and EC of the runoff** in the first days, then the solution as usual. Aim for **pH 5.5–6.5** (Kentucky); the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) has crop ranges.
6. **Set watering to the medium.** UF/IFAS HS1274: fine coir may need **6–8 waterings a day**, horticultural perlite **a dozen or more** shorter ones. Mix solution with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/).
7. **Sterilize before reuse.** Oklahoma State: **180 °F (82 °C) for 30 minutes**, or a **10% bleach soak for 20 minutes** followed by several rinses. UF/IFAS lists clay, gravel, sand and pumice as reusable this way.

## Troubleshooting growing media

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH climbs after planting in rockwool | Rockwool starts at pH 7–8.5 | Soak cubes at pH 5.5–6.0 before use | Oklahoma State; UF/IFAS EP623 |
| Pump or filter clogged | Loose fine media or foam crumbs | Use plugs or clay; keep coir out of recirculating systems | UF/IFAS EP623; Oklahoma State |
| Perlite floating off the tray | Perlite floats in flood systems | Use clay or gravel for ebb and flow | Oklahoma State; Kentucky |
| Clay pebbles floating | New pebbles float for months | Expected; weigh down or wait | Oklahoma State |
| Medium stays soggy, roots brown | Too much water held (vermiculite, fine coir) | Mix in perlite; water less often | Oklahoma State; UF/IFAS HS1274 |
| High EC or sodium in runoff from coir | Unwashed coir | Rinse or use buffered coir | Oklahoma State; Kentucky |
| Disease after reusing media | Old roots carry pathogens | Sterilize or replace | UF/IFAS HS169; Oklahoma State |
| Itchy skin handling rockwool | Fibers | Wear gloves | Kentucky CCD-TA-02 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best growing medium for hydroponics?

It depends on the system. Clay pebbles or gravel for ebb and flow, rockwool or foam cubes to start seeds, perlite or coir for drip buckets (Oklahoma State; UF/IFAS; Virginia Tech).

### Is rockwool or coco coir better?

Both hold a lot of water. Rockwool is sterile and uniform but starts at pH 7–8.5 and isn't biodegradable; coir is renewable but may carry salts that need rinsing (UF/IFAS; Kentucky). For seed starting, rockwool cubes are the standard; for buckets and bags, coir. Our [rockwool vs coco coir guide](/equipment/rockwool-vs-coco-coir/) compares the two in trials.

### Can I reuse clay pebbles?

Yes. Oklahoma State says expanded clay lasts many years if washed and sterilized; it gives 180 °F for 30 minutes or a 10% bleach soak for 20 minutes, then rinsing.

### Do I need to soak rockwool before use?

Yes. Oklahoma State gives a starting pH of 7.0–8.0 and says to bring it to 5.5–6.0 with a low-pH soak; UF/IFAS also says soaking may be required.

### Can I use soil or potting mix in hydroponics?

Not in a recirculating system. Potting mixes contain peat, bark and fine particles that UF/IFAS says to keep out of recirculating systems. Peat and bark do work in drip buckets and bags (Yang et al. 2022).

### What medium does Kratky use?

Usually just a net pot holding the seedling's starting cube or plug, which the roots grow out of into the solution. No source we read sets a fill medium for Kratky net pots. See our [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) and [net pots guide](/equipment/hydroponic-net-pots/).

### Is perlite safe?

Yes for plants, but the dust is a respiratory hazard: wet it and wear a mask (Oklahoma State; UF/IFAS).
