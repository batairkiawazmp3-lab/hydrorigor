A **hydroponic tower** is a vertical, gravity-fed system: a pump lifts nutrient solution from a tank to the top of a column, the solution trickles down inside past the plant roots, and it drains back into the tank. Oklahoma State Extension's DIY design is a **5–6 ft tower holding 28 plants** in 3-inch net pots, fed by a **400 gallon-per-hour pump** from a **45-gallon tank**; two towers fit in a 5 ft by 5 ft space. Towers suit lettuce, other leafy greens, herbs and strawberries. Indoors they need lights above the tower, and research on lettuce towers found that light and plant size fall from the top of the tower to the base.

This guide explains how towers work, what grows well on them, how far apart to plant, how to run the pump, and how a DIY build compares with a kit. Every figure comes from a university or extension source listed at the end; HydroRigor has not built or tested a tower for this guide.

## How a hydroponic tower works

Oklahoma State Extension describes vertical hydroponics as "conventional hydroponic techniques in a vertical, gravity fed system": the nutrient-rich water is fed from the top and collected at the bottom. UF/IFAS Extension in St. Lucie County describes the same loop for home towers. A pump moves water from the reservoir to the top, it trickles down past the roots, and the closed loop recirculates the water rather than wasting it.

Plants sit in net pots set into holes along the column. Their roots grow into the column, where the falling solution wets them. This is why a tower behaves like a pump-fed channel system rather than a still-water system: when the pump stops, the roots are not sitting in a reservoir. Oregon State Extension's advice for nutrient film technique, where plants also depend on a pumped flow, applies here too. Plan for power and pump failures, because plants can wilt quickly.

The benefits Oklahoma State lists for vertical hydroponics are high-density yield per unit area, a good fit for small sunny places such as balconies, patios and rooftops, year-round production indoors, "more than 90 percent efficiency in water use" and no soil-borne diseases. The fact sheet lists no disadvantages, so here are the trade-offs the other sources point to: towers depend on a pump, and the plants lower down get less light.

## Towers compared with other home systems

| System | Power | What the sources say it suits | Main watch-out | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tower (vertical) | Submersible pump on a timer | Lettuce, greens, herbs, strawberries; 28 plants on 5–6 ft | Pump failure; less light at the base | Oklahoma State HLA-6724; Touliatos et al. 2016 |
| NFT channels | Pump | Short-lived or small-rooted crops: lettuce, herbs, spinach, kale, chard | Minimum 2% slope; plants wilt quickly if the pump stops | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Deep water culture | Air pump | Lettuce, spinach, kale, herbs | Needs effective aeration; heavy tank must sit level on the ground | Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Kratky bucket | None | Lettuce and short-cycle greens; 1–1.5 gal per lettuce plant | Not for long-season crops | UF/IFAS HS1488 |

A tower's advantage is plant count per square foot of floor. Touliatos and colleagues (Food and Energy Security, 2016) compared lettuce grown in vertical towers with horizontal hydroponics and found the vertical system "produced more crop per unit of growing floor area". If floor space is your limit, a tower makes sense. If you only want a few heads of lettuce, a [Kratky bucket or a DIY deep water tote](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) costs less and needs no pump. ## What grows well on a tower

Oklahoma State lists strawberry, lettuce, Swiss chard, herbs, spinach, kale and broccoli for towers. UF/IFAS St. Lucie County lists lettuce, spinach, kale, basil, cilantro, strawberries and cherry tomatoes. Leafy greens and herbs are the safest start: they are short-lived and small-rooted, the same profile Oregon State recommends for channel systems.

A tower holds one solution, so grow crops with similar targets together. For example, lettuce and basil overlap at EC 1.2–1.6 and pH 6.0 in the Oklahoma State table. Strawberries and broccoli want a stronger solution in that table.

## How far apart to plant

![Bar chart of the spacing Oklahoma State recommends on a vertical tower: thin leaf lettuce 6 to 8 inches; head lettuce, spinach, kale and strawberry 12 inches; Swiss chard 12 to 14 inches; broccoli 18 inches. Pump schedule: lettuce can run continuously, 24 hours a day; strawberries 7 to 10 times a day for 1 hour each. Stagger the holes so pots on opposite sides do not touch inside.](/images/guides/hydroponic-tower-spacing-by-crop.svg)

*Spacing between plants on a tower, from Oklahoma State Extension HLA-6724, Table 1, with the pump schedules from the same fact sheet.*

Spacing on a tower is the vertical and around-the-column distance between net pots. Oklahoma State's Table 1 gives 6–8 inches for thin leaf lettuce, 12 inches for head lettuce, spinach, kale and strawberry, 12–14 inches for Swiss chard and 18 inches for broccoli. A 28-site tower is therefore best for leafy greens; large crops such as broccoli need fewer sites.

## Light: the tower's weak point indoors

Oklahoma State notes that a tower "can also be used for growing plants indoors if lights are provided above the tower", but gives no light intensity or hours. The research shows why lighting a tower is harder than lighting a flat tray. In Touliatos and colleagues' lettuce study, light and shoot fresh weight "decreased significantly ... from top to base" of the towers. The plants at the bottom received less light and grew less.

For lettuce, University of Kentucky gives a daily light integral (DLI) of 12–17 mol/m²/day. A light above the tower delivers the most to the top sites. Practical consequences:

- Expect smaller plants low on the tower unless you add light at the sides.
- Consider putting crops you harvest young at the lower sites and the crops that need the most light at the top.
- Outdoors, Oklahoma State recommends towers for sunny spots such as balconies, patios and rooftops.

## Building a DIY tower

Oklahoma State Extension's fact sheet HLA-6724 is a complete DIY design. Its materials:

| Part | Specification in HLA-6724 |
| --- | --- |
| Tower column | White vinyl fence post, 5 in x 5 in x 8 ft |
| Pipe | 3-inch PVC pipe, 10 ft |
| Reservoir | 45-gallon tank |
| Pump | Submersible, 400 gallons per hour, with attachments |
| Timer | Light timer, to switch the pump |
| Plant holders | 28 net pots, 3-inch |

The key steps from the fact sheet, in short:

1. **Cut the column to height.** The recommended height for a vertical tower is 5–6 ft.
2. **Cut the planting holes, staggered.** Stagger the holes on each side so the pots do not touch each other inside the tower.
3. **Make the top distribute solution.** The top has about 10 holes to spread the solution down the inside of the tower.
4. **Set the tower over the tank** so solution drains back, and run the pump line to the top.
5. **Put the pump on the timer.** Lettuce can be watered continuously for 24 hours; strawberries seven to 10 times a day for one hour each.
6. **Test for splashing.** If water splashes out of a pot it can wet the leaves, which can lead to fungal disease and kill the plant. Adjust before planting.
7. **Fill, check pH and EC for the crop, and plant** into the net pots at the Table 1 spacing.

For mixing the solution itself, follow [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) and size each part with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/).

## DIY tower or a kit?

Oklahoma State notes that commercial tower systems "can cost around $500 or more" (2021). A kit saves the cutting and fitting, while a DIY build lets you choose the parts. Whichever you choose, check the same things the DIY design specifies:

- **Plant sites and spacing:** enough sites for your crop at the Table 1 spacing (more sites help only with small leafy crops).
- **Pump and timer:** the DIY design uses a 400 gal/hr submersible pump and a timer that can run it continuously or in cycles.
- **Reservoir volume:** the DIY design uses a 45-gallon tank for 28 plants.
- **Light:** whether the kit includes lights for indoor use, since a tower needs light from above at least.
- **Cleaning:** the tower needs cleaning with bleach between crops, so check you can reach the inside.

## Running the tower

- **Check pH and EC regularly.** Oklahoma State's tower fact sheet says to monitor pH and EC for the crop grown. Its companion guide, HLA-6722, advises checking both daily at the same time of day and adjusting pH only after EC is in range.
- **Keep the tank topped up.** The water level in the tank should be maintained (HLA-6724).
- **Watch for splashing** and correct it before leaves stay wet.
- **Clean between crops.** Clean the tower with bleach after each round of crop (HLA-6724).
- **Plan for outages.** As with NFT, plants can wilt quickly if the pump stops (Oregon State EM 9457).

### Strawberries on towers

Researchers in Illinois grew strawberries in vertical hydroponic towers in a high tunnel for two years at two sites (Wortman and colleagues, HortTechnology, 2016). Yield was highest in perlite mixed with coco coir or vermiculite with a synthetic fertilizer. An organic nutrient source cut yield by about 15%, and the best cultivar changed from site to site and year to year, so the authors advise local cultivar testing. Oklahoma State waters tower strawberries seven to 10 times a day for an hour. The University of Arizona keeps hydroponic strawberry solution at EC 1.0 dS/m or below, while Oklahoma State's table lists 1.8–2.2, so start low and watch the plants. Our [NFT strawberry guide](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/) covers channel growing.

## Troubleshooting a hydroponic tower

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plants at the bottom smaller than at the top | Light falls toward the base | Add side light, or put low-light or harvest-young crops at the bottom | Touliatos et al. 2016 |
| Leaves spotted or rotting near the pots | Solution splashing onto leaves | Adjust the flow or pot fit; splashing can lead to fungal disease | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Plants wilting suddenly | Pump or power failure | Restore flow quickly; plan a backup | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Pots touching inside, uneven wetting | Holes not staggered | Stagger holes on opposite sides | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Crowded, small plants | Spacing tighter than the crop needs | Use Table 1 spacing (for example 12 in for head lettuce) | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| pH or EC off target | Normal drift as plants feed | Check daily; fix EC first, then pH | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Disease carried into the next crop | Residue in the tower | Clean with bleach between crops | Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |

## Frequently asked questions

### How many plants does a hydroponic tower hold?

Oklahoma State's DIY tower holds 28 plants in 3-inch net pots, and two towers fit in a 5 ft by 5 ft space for 56 plants at a time. Commercial towers vary.

### What is the best crop for a hydroponic tower?

Leafy greens and herbs: lettuce, spinach, kale, Swiss chard and basil all appear on extension lists for towers. Strawberries also work, with more frequent pumping. Large plants such as broccoli need 18-inch spacing, so fewer fit.

### How often should a hydroponic tower pump run?

Oklahoma State runs lettuce continuously, 24 hours a day, and strawberries seven to 10 times a day for one hour each, with a timer switching the pump.

### Do hydroponic towers work indoors?

Yes, if lights are provided above the tower (Oklahoma State). Expect less light and smaller plants toward the base (Touliatos et al. 2016); side lighting or careful crop placement helps.

### How much does a hydroponic tower cost?

Oklahoma State noted in 2021 that commercial tower systems "can cost around $500 or more". A DIY tower uses a fence post, PVC pipe, a 45-gallon tank, a 400 gal/hr pump, a timer and 28 net pots.

### Is a tower better than NFT or deep water culture?

A tower fits more plants into a small floor area; the Touliatos study found more lettuce per unit of floor area than horizontal hydroponics. Deep water culture and Kratky are simpler and cheaper for a few plants. Oregon State notes that stacking channel levels adds shading too, so any vertical design needs light planning.
