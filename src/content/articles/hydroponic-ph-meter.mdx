The right pH meter for hydroponics is a pen-style digital meter with **two-point calibration, automatic temperature compensation and a cap that keeps the probe wet**. Purdue University calls automatic calibration and temperature compensation the features worth having, and says portable, affordable units are very accurate. Calibrate it with **pH 7.01 and pH 4.01 buffers** (Michigan State), at the start of every testing session until you know how far your meter drifts (UMass Extension). Then use it to keep the nutrient solution at **pH 5.5–6.5** (University of Kentucky), checking every day at the same time (Oklahoma State).

The meter matters less than how you treat it. UMass Extension puts it bluntly: "Calibration is a must to get any useful information from pens and meters!" A cheap pen that is calibrated beats an expensive one that isn't. This guide covers what to look for, what to buy with the meter, and how to calibrate, use and store it. It names no products: HydroRigor has not tested any meter and has no affiliate links.

## Why hydroponics needs a pH meter

A nutrient solution's pH doesn't stay where you set it: plants, water and added nutrients all move it. The University of Kentucky gives pH 5.5–6.5 as the working range for hydroponic solutions, and Oklahoma State advises checking pH and EC daily, at the same time each day, so readings are comparable.

Your tap water plays a part. Oklahoma State says to test source water for pH, EC and alkalinity, and notes that alkalinity above 75 ppm pushes pH up. If your solution keeps climbing no matter how often you correct it, the water is the likely cause, and you will be reading pH often. That is the case for a meter you can trust and recalibrate.

Each crop has its own range inside or near that band. Look yours up in the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/); the [hydroponic nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains what happens to nutrient uptake when pH drifts.

## What to look for in a pH meter

A cap that keeps the probe wet; a dry probe needs a 1-hour soak. Check the listing for a stated accuracy and a replaceable probe. Optional: a combination pen that reads pH and EC.](/images/guides/ph-meter-features-checklist.svg)

*The features that matter, in order. From Purdue University (OFA Bulletin 917), Michigan State University Extension and UMass Extension; the last two rows are HydroRigor's buying checks.*

| Feature | Why it matters | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two-point calibration | Sets the meter at pH 7 and pH 4, either side of the range you grow in | UMass Extension; Michigan State |
| Automatic calibration | Purdue lists it as desirable; fewer steps to get wrong | Purdue University |
| Automatic temperature compensation | pH readings change with temperature | Purdue University; Michigan State |
| Storage cap that holds solution | A probe left unused will likely dry out | Michigan State |
| Stated accuracy and resolution | So you know what a reading is worth | HydroRigor check |
| Replaceable probe | So a failed probe doesn't mean a new meter | HydroRigor check |

**Calibrate to the range you measure.** Purdue's advice is to calibrate to the pH range you are testing. Hydroponic solutions sit between pH 5.5 and 6.5, so buffers at 4 and 7 bracket them. You don't need a pH 10 buffer for this job.

**Temperature compensation.** Michigan State notes that pH changes with temperature and tells growers to check the solution temperature before calibrating. A meter with automatic compensation handles the correction for you, which is why Purdue lists it as desirable.

**Accuracy.** None of the extension sources used here gives a required accuracy figure for home hydroponics, so this guide doesn't either. Read the accuracy and resolution the maker states, and prefer a meter that states them over one that doesn't.

## Pen, combination pen or bench meter?

| Type | Good for | Keep in mind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH pen | One or two reservoirs at home | Calibrate often; keep the cap wet |
| Combination pH and EC pen | One tool for both daily checks | Two calibrations: pH buffers and an EC standard |
| Bench meter | A fixed mixing station | No more accurate than a calibrated pen |

UMass Extension says pens and bench meters work on the same principles and should give the same readings when properly calibrated. For a home grower that settles it: buy the pen and spend the difference on buffers.

A combination pen is convenient because you check EC and pH together every day anyway. You then calibrate both halves. Michigan State uses a 1.41 mS/cm standard for EC, and points out that the meter and the packet may use different units: 1.41 mS/cm is about 1413 µS/cm.

On cost, UMass Extension's fact sheet, updated in 2016, put single pH or EC pens at $50–60 and a combination pen at about $80. Those are 2016 figures from one source, not current prices; HydroRigor does not track prices.

## What to buy with the meter

A meter without buffers can't be calibrated, so treat these as part of the purchase.

| Item | Look for | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH 7.01 and pH 4.01 buffers | In date; single-use sachets or small bottles | Two-point calibration (Michigan State) |
| Storage solution | The one your meter's maker specifies | A dry probe needs a soak of at least 1 hour (Michigan State) |
| Distilled water | For rinsing | Rinse before and between calibrations (Michigan State) |
| Small cups | One per buffer | Never dip the probe into the stock bottle (Michigan State) |
| EC standard, 1.41 mS/cm | Only for a combination pen | Michigan State |
| pH up and pH down | Small bottles | Oklahoma State describes pH adjusters for correcting the solution |

Sachets cost more per use than a bottle, but each one is fresh. That matters because Michigan State warns that standards expire and that dipping a probe into the big bottle makes the whole bottle inaccurate over time. For how adjusters fit into mixing, see [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) and the [best hydroponic nutrients for home growers](/best/hydroponic-nutrients-for-home-growers/).

## How to calibrate a pH meter

![Two-point calibration in six steps. 1: check the buffers are in date and check the temperature. 2: rinse the probe with distilled water. 3: calibrate in pH 7.01 buffer poured into a small cup. 4: rinse again with distilled water. 5: calibrate in pH 4.01 buffer in a fresh cup. 6: rinse, then measure your nutrient solution. Throw away used buffer and never dip the probe in the bottle.](/images/guides/ph-meter-two-point-calibration.svg)

*Two-point calibration with the buffers Michigan State uses (pH 7.01 and 4.01). Rinsing, pouring into a cup and checking expiry are from Michigan State; discarding used buffer is from UMass Extension.*

1. **Check the buffers.** Make sure they are in date. Check the temperature of the buffers before you start, because pH changes with temperature (Michigan State). Purdue stores meters and solutions at room temperature, which keeps this simple.
2. **Rinse the probe** with distilled water (Michigan State).
3. **Calibrate at pH 7.01.** Pour a little buffer into a small cup, put the probe in, and follow your meter's calibration instructions until it accepts the reading.
4. **Rinse again** with distilled water so the first buffer doesn't contaminate the second.
5. **Calibrate at pH 4.01** in a fresh cup.
6. **Rinse, then measure** your nutrient solution.
7. **Throw away the used buffer.** UMass Extension says to discard used calibration solutions. Never pour them back.

If your meter only supports one-point calibration, UMass Extension's minimum is a single calibration with pH 7 buffer. Two points are better because they check the meter across the range you grow in. Button presses differ between meters, so follow the maker's instructions for the calibration mode itself.

## How often to calibrate

There is no single interval that suits every meter. UMass Extension's method is the practical one: when the meter is new, calibrate at the beginning of every testing session. Watch how far the readings have moved since the last session. Once you know how much your meter drifts, you can decide how long it can go between calibrations. Readings drift over time even on expensive meters, UMass notes, so a higher price doesn't buy you out of the routine.

Calibrate again whenever:

- the probe has dried out (after its soak);
- the meter has been stored for a while;
- a reading surprises you, before you add any pH adjuster to the tank.

That last habit prevents the commonest mistake with pH: correcting a solution that was fine because the meter was wrong.

## Taking a reading

1. **Mix or top up first.** Oklahoma State's order is to bring EC into range first and adjust pH only after that.
2. **Stir the reservoir** so you read the solution, not a pocket of it.
3. **Rinse the probe**, then dip it and wait for the number to settle.
4. **Read at the same time each day** (Oklahoma State), so the numbers are comparable.
5. **Adjust in small steps** with pH up or pH down, stir, and read again.
6. **Rinse and cap the meter** with its storage solution.

Write the readings down. A log shows you whether pH tends to rise or fall in your system, and how fast, which tells you far more than any single number. Daily checks suit systems with a shared reservoir, such as [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/). For a no-pump tank that is filled once, see the [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/).

## Storage and care

- **Keep the probe wet.** Michigan State warns that a probe left unused will likely dry out. If it has, soak it in storage solution for at least an hour, then calibrate before you trust it.
- **Use the storage solution your meter's maker specifies.** The extension sources here don't cover storing a probe in distilled or tap water, so follow the manual on that point.
- **Store at room temperature in a low-humidity room.** Purdue says never to store meters or solutions in the greenhouse or outside. At home, that means a cupboard, not the grow tent.
- **Never dip the probe into the stock bottle** of buffer or standard. Pour some out, or use single-use sachets (Michigan State).
- **Check expiry dates** on buffers and standards (Michigan State).
- **Rinse with distilled water** before and between calibrations (Michigan State).

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Check first | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reading keeps moving and won't settle | Probe has dried out | Soak in storage solution for at least 1 hour, then calibrate | Michigan State |
| Meter won't accept calibration | Buffer is expired or contaminated | Open fresh buffer; never reuse or pour back | Michigan State; UMass Extension |
| Readings differ from last week with no change to the tank | Normal drift | Recalibrate; calibrate every session until you know the drift | UMass Extension |
| Two meters disagree | One or both are out of calibration | Calibrate both with the same fresh buffers | UMass Extension |
| Calibration seems off on a cold or hot day | pH changes with temperature | Let buffers and meter reach room temperature first | Michigan State; Purdue |
| pH climbs again a day after correcting | Alkaline source water (above 75 ppm alkalinity) | Test the source water; expect to correct more often | Oklahoma State |
| Meter reads fine but plants show problems | pH is only half the picture | Check EC as well, and adjust EC before pH | Oklahoma State |

If plants still look wrong with pH and EC in range, work through the deficiency and excess symptoms in the [hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a pH meter for hydroponics?

Yes, if you want to hold a range. Hydroponic solutions should stay at pH 5.5–6.5 (University of Kentucky), and Oklahoma State advises checking daily. A digital meter gives a number you can log and compare from day to day.

### How often should I calibrate a pH meter?

At the start of every testing session when the meter is new, until you learn how much it drifts between sessions (UMass Extension). After that, calibrate as often as your meter's drift requires, and always after the probe has dried out or a reading looks wrong.

### Which buffers do I need?

pH 7.01 and pH 4.01 for a two-point calibration (Michigan State). UMass Extension accepts pH 7 alone as a minimum. Purdue's rule is to calibrate to the range you test, and hydroponic solutions sit between those two buffers.

### Is a cheap pH pen accurate enough?

Purdue says portable, affordable units are available and very accurate, and UMass Extension says pens and bench meters give the same readings when properly calibrated. The catch is calibration: UMass notes that readings drift even on expensive meters. Check the accuracy the maker states before buying.

### Should I buy a combination pH and EC meter?

It is a convenience, not a requirement. You check both numbers every day, so one tool is handy, but you must calibrate both: pH with buffers and EC with a standard such as 1.41 mS/cm (Michigan State). Two separate pens work just as well.

### Can I store the probe in tap or distilled water?

The sources used here don't address it. Michigan State's advice for a dried-out probe is a soak in storage solution, so the safe choice is the storage solution your meter's maker specifies.

### What pH should my solution be?

pH 5.5–6.5 for most hydroponic crops (University of Kentucky). Crop ranges differ a little: Oklahoma State lists basil at 5.5–6.0 and lettuce at 6.0–7.0.
