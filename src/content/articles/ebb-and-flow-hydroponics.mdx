**Ebb and flow (also called flood and drain) hydroponics fills a tray of plants in pots or growing media with nutrient solution from a reservoir below, holds it for a few minutes, then lets it drain back.** A timer runs the pump. Flood to **about 1 inch (2.5 cm) below the top of the media**, hold the flood for **10–30 minutes** (sources differ; start near 15), and **start with one flood a day**, adding more if the media begins to dry and shrink before the next one. For leafy greens and herbs keep the solution near **pH 6.0** and **EC 1.2–1.8 mS/cm**.

The flood level and the "flood before it dries" rule come from Oregon State's ebb-and-flow publication (EM 9458), the flood times from UMass, Virginia Tech, the University of Georgia and Illinois Extension, and the solution targets from Oklahoma State. Ebb and flow is the system Oregon State calls ideal for seedlings, and it suits herbs, greens and peppers. Its weak point is the timer: no source gives one schedule, so you set it by watching the media.

HydroRigor has not built or tested these systems; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## How ebb and flow works

Oregon State lists four parts: flood trays, a nutrient reservoir, a pump and a timer. Illinois Extension describes the cycle: a submersible pump in the reservoir sends solution up into the tray, and once it reaches a set level an overflow pipe or fitting lets the extra drain back. When the pump switches off, the tray empties back into the reservoir, which sits below it.

The draining is what makes the system work. Oregon State notes that as the solution drains, it pulls air into the growing media, so roots get water and nutrients during the flood and oxygen between floods. That is why ebb and flow needs no air pump, unlike [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/), and why the medium matters: it has to hold enough water to carry the roots from one flood to the next and still let air back in.

Missouri sorts hydroponic systems into water-based (deep water culture, NFT, aeroponics) and media-based. Ebb and flow is media-based: the roots grow in clay pellets, rockwool or a substrate rather than hanging in water. Our [types of hydroponic systems guide](/systems/types-of-hydroponic-systems/) shows where it sits among all eight types.

## Flood timing: where the sources differ

Timing is the question every grower asks, and the sources answer it in three different ways: with a level, with a duration and with a sign to watch.

### How high to flood

Oregon State says to flood to **about an inch below the top of the media**. Keeping the top inch dry matters for two reasons it gives: shallow floods help stop salts building up near the base of the plants, and a drier surface means fewer pest problems. UMass, writing about greenhouse benches of potted plants, pumps solution to **¾–1 inch (2–2.5 cm) deep** in the bench and lets the pots soak it up from below. Both work the same way: the solution reaches the roots from underneath and never floods the crown of the plant.

### How long each flood lasts

| Source | Flood length | Setting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UMass Extension | About 10 min, or until the media absorbs the solution | Greenhouse benches of pots |
| Virginia Tech SPES-466 | 12 min, once a day | University trial of edible crops |
| University of Georgia (Pennisi and van Iersel) | Media saturated by wicking in about 15–20 min | Potted poinsettias on flood benches |
| Illinois Extension | 20–30 min flood cycle | Home hydroponic systems |

![A bar chart of flood length in minutes, scale 0 to 30. UMass Extension, greenhouse benches: about 10 minutes, three quarters to 1 inch deep. Virginia Tech trial: 12 minutes, once a day. University of Georgia study: media saturated in about 15 to 20 minutes. Illinois Extension, home systems: a 20 to 30 minute flood cycle. Start near 15 minutes and stop when the media glistens all the way up.](/images/guides/ebb-and-flow-flood-time-sources.svg)

*Flood length from four sources. The shorter times come from shallow pots on benches; Illinois's longer cycle is written for home systems, which often hold deeper media.*

The range is wider than it looks because each source floods something different. A shallow pot on a bench soaks up in about 10 minutes; Oregon State notes that **deeper media allow longer flood cycles**, which is why bigger plants such as tomatoes need more time. Our suggestion: start near **15 minutes**, which sits between the trial and the home figure, then check what Oregon State tells you to look for. Drain once the media is fully soaked and **glistening**. If the top of the media is still dry at the end of the flood, lengthen it; if it was glistening well before the pump stopped, shorten it.

### How often to flood

Here the sources give a range and a rule rather than a number:

- **Virginia Tech's trial flooded once a day** for 12 minutes.
- **UMass** says benches may be watered **once or twice a week to several times a day**, depending on the weather and the size of the crop.
- The **University of Georgia** study flooded potted plants and stored the solution "until the next fertigation (usually the next day)".
- **Oregon State** gives the rule: flood again **before the media begins to dry out and contract**. If the media has pulled away from the side of the pot, or the plant is wilting, you are too late.

So start with one flood a day and watch. Big plants, warm rooms, strong light and fast-draining media all shorten the gap; seedlings and cool rooms lengthen it. Oregon State adds that flooding less deeply or waiting longer between floods dries the surface and reduces pests, so don't flood more often than the plants need. None of our sources gives a separate schedule for each medium, so we don't print one; let the media tell you.

## Choosing the tray, media and reservoir

| Part | What to use | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flood tray | A level tray with ridges or channels in the floor | Drains evenly, leaves no puddles | Oregon State EM 9458; UMass |
| Media | Expanded clay pellets; rockwool; fabric pots; coir, bark or potting media | Clay pellets can be sterilized and reused; deeper media allow longer floods | Illinois; Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Pump | Submersible pump in the reservoir, on a timer | The timer controls every flood | Illinois; Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Overflow | A fitting set at the flood height | Returns extra solution so the tray can't overfill | Illinois |
| Reservoir | Opaque, below the tray, about ½ gal per sq ft of tray | Holds the flood volume and drains by gravity | UMass; Illinois Extension (Hydroponics at Home) |
| Filter | A screen on the return | Keeps solids out of the pump | UMass |

**Media.** Illinois calls expanded clay pellets "great for ebb and flow systems": expensive, but they can be sterilized between plantings. Oregon State also lists rockwool, fabric pots, coir, bark and potting media. Illinois gives rockwool an 80% water-holding capacity but no figure for clay pellets, so we can't compare them directly; if pellets dry out between floods, flood more often rather than guess. Whatever you use, keep the media depth even across the tray so every plant gets the same flood.

**Tray.** UMass installs benches "perfectly level" so the solution stands at the same depth everywhere, and Oregon State recommends a table with ridges or channels so it drains completely. A tray that holds water in one corner after draining is the most common cause of soggy roots in one spot.

**Reservoir size.** UMass sizes the holding tank at about **½ gallon per square foot (about 20 L per m²) of bench**. For a common 2 × 4 ft (0.6 × 1.2 m) tray that is about 4 gallons (15 L), our arithmetic. A tray full of open water 1 inch deep would take about 5 gallons (19 L), and the pump must stay under water at the end of the flood, so we suggest a reservoir of at least twice that, around 10 gallons (38 L) for that tray. A bigger tank also drifts more slowly in pH and EC. Keep it opaque and covered; Illinois Extension notes that light reaching the solution grows algae.

## The nutrient solution: pH, EC and temperature

| Crop | pH | EC (mS/cm) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lettuce | 6.0–7.0 (Kentucky 5.5–6.0) | 1.2–1.8 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Kentucky CCD-CP-63 |
| Basil | 5.5–6.0 | 1.0–1.6 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Spinach | 6.0–7.0 | 1.8–2.3 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Pepper | 5.5–6.0 | 0.8–1.8 | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Hydroponic crops overall | 5.0–6.0 | 1.5–3 | Oklahoma State HLA-6442 |

**pH: aim for about 6.0.** A mixed tray of herbs and greens needs one pH, and 6.0 is the one value that sits inside every crop range above, from basil's 5.5–6.0 to lettuce's 6.0–7.0. Oklahoma State's general fact sheet gives 5.0–6.0, and 6.0 is its upper edge.

**EC: 1.2–1.8 for greens and herbs.** That is Oklahoma State's lettuce range, and it overlaps basil (1.0–1.6) and pepper (0.8–1.8). Spinach runs higher. Set EC first, then pH, as Oklahoma State recommends, and check both regularly; Illinois says to test and record them. The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists other crops, and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) works out amounts.

**Salt buildup.** Because the solution enters from below and water leaves through the leaves and surface, salts collect near the top of the media. In the University of Georgia's flood-bench study, EC in the top layer of the pots was **up to ten times higher** than in the middle and bottom, while the roots grew mostly in the middle and bottom. Oregon State's two fixes are shallow floods and, now and then, a flood of plain distilled or reverse-osmosis water to rinse the media. Neither source gives a frequency for that rinse. The Georgia study did not find routine leaching necessary for its potted crop unless plants were over-fertilized.

**Temperature and replacing.** Oklahoma State gives a solution temperature of about 72–75 °F (22–24 °C) and UF/IFAS 65–80 °F (18–27 °C). Replace the whole solution about every two weeks (Oklahoma State) or every 2–3 weeks (Illinois), and top up with water between changes. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) covers both.

## Which crops grow well in ebb and flow

| Crop | Fit | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seedlings and transplants | Best | Oregon State calls ebb and flow ideal for seedling production | Oregon State EM 9458, EM 9453 |
| Herbs (basil, cilantro, mint) | Good | Listed for ebb and flow | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Leafy greens (lettuce, spinach, Swiss chard, arugula) | Good | Short crops; Oklahoma State says lettuce is usually grown in NFT or flood and drain | Oregon State EM 9458; Oklahoma State HLA-6442 |
| Radishes | Good | Listed for ebb and flow | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Peppers | Good | Listed; needs deeper media | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Tomatoes | Possible | Deeper media and longer floods needed | Oregon State EM 9458 |

Our sources give no ebb-and-flow yield figures to compare with other systems, so we don't rank it by yield. For lettuce, Kentucky gives harvest at 21–35 days after transplanting and a daily light integral of 12–17 mol/m²/day; Virginia Tech's trial grew greens and herbs at a DLI of at least 17. Our sources give no plant spacing specific to ebb and flow trays; space by the size of the grown plant. ## How to set up ebb and flow, step by step

1. **Place the tray and reservoir.** Put the flood tray on a stand above an opaque, covered reservoir so it drains by gravity (Illinois). Level the tray in both directions (UMass).
2. **Fit the pump and overflow.** Put a submersible pump in the reservoir with a line to the tray. Fit an overflow fitting set to the flood height, about 1 inch below the top of your media, and a screen over the return (Illinois; Oregon State; UMass).
3. **Size the reservoir.** At least ½ gallon per square foot of tray (UMass); we suggest about twice that so the pump stays covered at full flood.
4. **Add the media.** Fill pots or the tray evenly with rinsed expanded clay pellets, rockwool or another medium (Illinois; Oregon State).
5. **Test with plain water.** Run the pump and time how long the tray takes to fill to the overflow and drain completely. Look for leaks and for puddles left after draining.
6. **Mix the solution.** Set EC to 1.2–1.8 for greens, then pH to about 6.0 (Oklahoma State HLA-6722).
7. **Set the timer.** Start with one flood a day, held about 15 minutes once the tray is full (Virginia Tech; UMass; Illinois). Use a timer you trust; Oregon State says the system requires a reliable one.
8. **Plant.** Set seedlings or rooted cubes into the media so their roots sit below the flood line.
9. **Tune for a week.** Check the media before each flood. If it has started to dry and shrink, add a second flood; if it is still wet, keep one. Lengthen the flood if the media doesn't glisten to the top (Oregon State).
10. **Keep it clean.** Check pH and EC, top up with water, replace the solution every 2–3 weeks, and rinse the media with a plain-water flood now and then to limit salts (Oklahoma State; Illinois; Oregon State).

## Ebb and flow vs NFT, DWC and Kratky

| | Ebb and flow | NFT | Deep water culture | Kratky |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roots in | Media | Thin film | Aerated water | Still water, then air |
| Pump | Water pump on a timer | Water pump, always on | Air pump | None |
| Timer | Yes | No | No | No |
| Best for | Seedlings, herbs, greens, peppers | Rows of lettuce and herbs | Lettuce, greens, herbs | A few lettuce plants |
| Source | Oregon State EM 9458 | Oklahoma State HLA-6442 | Missouri G6986 | UF/IFAS HS1488 |

Choose ebb and flow when you want to grow seedlings, a mixed tray of herbs and greens, or plants in pots you can move. Choose [NFT](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) for long rows of lettuce, deep water culture for a forgiving raft of greens, and [Kratky](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) for a few plants with no pump at all.

## Troubleshooting ebb and flow

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Media dry and pulling away from the pot; plants wilt | Floods too far apart | Add a flood; check the timer | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Top of the media dry after a flood | Flood too short or too low | Lengthen the flood; raise the overflow to 1 in below the top | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Water stands in the tray after draining | Tray not level or no drainage channels | Level the tray; use a ridged tray | UMass; Oregon State EM 9458 |
| White crust on top of the media | Salts collecting at the surface | Flood shallower; rinse with a plain-water flood | Oregon State EM 9458; Pennisi and van Iersel 2000 |
| Fungus gnats or other pests | Surface staying wet | Flood less deeply or less often | Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Green growth on the tray or in the tank | Light on wet surfaces | Cover the tank; let the tray dry between floods; add an algae trap | Illinois Extension; UMass; Oregon State EM 9458 |
| Pump clogs | Media or debris in the return | Screen the return | UMass |
| pH or EC drifting fast | Small reservoir | Use a bigger tank; check more often | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is ebb and flow hydroponics?

A system in which a timed pump floods a tray of plants in media from a reservoir below, then lets the solution drain back. Oregon State lists flood trays, a reservoir, a pump and a timer; Illinois adds an overflow that sets the flood level.

### How long should an ebb and flow flood last?

Sources give 10–30 minutes: about 10 for greenhouse benches (UMass), 12 in Virginia Tech's trial, 15–20 for media to saturate (University of Georgia) and 20–30 for home systems (Illinois). Start near 15 minutes and stop when the media glistens.

### How many times a day should ebb and flow run?

Start with once a day, as Virginia Tech's trial did. UMass says greenhouse benches range from once or twice a week to several times a day; Oregon State's rule is to flood again before the media starts to dry and shrink.

### How high should the water go?

About 1 inch (2.5 cm) below the top of the media (Oregon State EM 9458). Shallow floods also limit salt buildup near the plant base.

### What is the best medium for ebb and flow?

Illinois Extension calls expanded clay pellets great for ebb and flow and notes they can be sterilized and reused. Rockwool, coir, bark, potting media and fabric pots also work (Oregon State). Our [hydroponic growing media guide](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/) compares them.

### Does ebb and flow need an air pump?

No. Oregon State notes that draining pulls air into the media, so roots get oxygen between floods.

### What grows best in ebb and flow?

Oregon State lists seedlings, herbs such as basil, cilantro and mint, leafy greens, radishes and peppers. Larger plants such as tomatoes need deeper media and longer floods.
