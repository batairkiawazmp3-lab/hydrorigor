**Yes, strawberries grow in NFT (nutrient film technique).** Solution is pumped to the high end of a sloped, light-tight channel, runs past the bare roots as a shallow film and drains back to the tank. Purdue's hydroponics specialist recommends a **slope of 1:100** (1:30 and 1:40 are also used) and a **flow of about 1 liter (¼ gallon) per minute per channel, with an upper limit of 2**. Oregon State gives a **2% minimum** slope. For the strawberries themselves, the University of Arizona keeps the solution at **pH 5.5–6.0 and EC 1.0 dS/m or below**, with **days at 20–24 °C (68–75 °F) and nights at 10–15 °C (50–59 °F)**. The crown must stay dry above the channel lid, and because the roots are not sitting in a reservoir, **plants can wilt quickly if the pump stops**.

This guide puts those numbers in one place, shows where the sources disagree, and walks through building and running a small strawberry NFT system at home. Every figure comes from a university, extension or journal source listed at the end. HydroRigor has not built or tested an NFT strawberry system for this guide.

## How NFT works for strawberries

Virginia Cooperative Extension describes NFT as a recirculating system: solution leaves a reservoir, enters the growing channels, flows past the roots and returns to be reused. Purdue's Petrus Langenhoven describes the same idea in one line: a shallow stream (the "film") recirculated past bare roots in a watertight, dark channel. The roots sit partly in that film and partly in the moist air above it.

University of Missouri Extension lists strawberries with lettuce and herbs among the crops NFT commonly supports, and Oregon State's EM 9457 lists "some strawberries" alongside leafy greens. Oregon State also explains why strawberries are not the easiest NFT crop: a channel has limited rooting space, so NFT suits short-lived or small-rooted crops best. A strawberry plant can fruit for six months to a year (Cornell), so its root mass keeps growing in a channel sized for lettuce. That is the central design problem.

## Channel design: slope, flow and length

### Slope

Purdue recommends a slope of 1:100 but notes that 1:30 and 1:40 are also used. A 2007 HortTechnology study (Sato and colleagues) ran its conventional NFT channel at a 1% slope. Oregon State's EM 9457 gives a minimum 2% slope for horizontal channels.

**Our recommendation: set the channel at 2% (a 1 in 50 fall: 2 cm per meter, or about ¼ inch per foot).** That meets Oregon State's minimum and sits inside the range Purdue reports in use. A steeper fall helps a strawberry channel drain past a growing root mat, which is the failure Oregon State's limited-rooting-space warning points to. Use a level on every channel; a channel that sags in the middle ponds solution around the roots.

### Flow rate

Purdue gives a flow of 1 L (0.26 gal) per minute per channel, with an upper limit of 2 L (0.53 gal) per minute. Sato and colleagues note that a flow between 1 and 2 L/min "is widely recommended for NFTs", citing earlier work by Cooper and Jones.

**Our recommendation: start at 1 L/min (about ¼ gal/min) per channel.** Measure it rather than trusting the pump rating: hold a measuring jug under the outlet of each channel for 30 seconds and double the reading. Adjust each channel's inlet so they match. Raise the flow toward 2 L/min (about ½ gal/min) if the last plants in the run look less vigorous than the first.

### Channel length

Purdue says channels should not exceed 10–15 m (33–49 ft). Sato and colleagues cite Jones recommending channels no longer than 9 m (30 ft). Home channels are almost always far shorter than either limit, so length rarely matters indoors. It is still worth knowing why the limit exists: in Sato's conventional 18 m (59 ft) lettuce channel, dissolved oxygen fell by up to 0.9 mg/L between the inlet and the outlet, and the solution warmed by 0.3–1.4 °C (0.5–2.5 °F) along the way. The plants at the far end get warmer, less oxygenated solution.

### Pump timing

Purdue and Virginia Tech describe NFT as a recirculating stream, and Oregon State warns that power and pump backups are crucial because NFT plants can wilt quickly. None of our sources gives an on/off schedule for strawberries in NFT channels. The only strawberry pump schedule we found is for vertical towers, where Oklahoma State runs the pump 7–10 times a day for one hour. **We recommend running an NFT channel continuously** and treating a pump stop as an emergency, because a short channel holds very little solution.

## Nutrient solution: pH, EC and temperature

### EC: start low

The University of Arizona recommends keeping strawberry solution at EC 1.0 dS/m or below and notes that typical tomato solutions are around 2.4 dS/m. Cornell reports that 1.2 mS/cm works in most cases, and the UA CEAC short course warns that a higher EC risks watery-flavored fruit. Oklahoma State's general table (HLA-6722) lists strawberry at EC 1.8–2.2.

**Our recommendation: run 1.0–1.2 mS/cm.** The Arizona and Cornell figures come from strawberry-specific controlled-environment programs; Oklahoma State's is one row in a table covering many crops. If plants show pale new growth at 1.0, move toward 1.2 before going higher. To reach a target, follow [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) and scale doses with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/).

### pH

Keep the solution at pH 5.5–6.0 (University of Arizona); Oklahoma State lists 6 for strawberry, inside that range. The University of Arizona notes that iron deficiency becomes likely if root-zone pH climbs above about 7.0. Oklahoma State advises checking pH and EC daily at the same time and adjusting pH only after EC is in range. The [pH and EC target reference](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists strawberry beside other crops by source.

### Solution temperature and oxygen

NFT channels are shallow, so the film warms with the room. In Sato's study, the catchment tank averaged 28.3 °C (83 °F) and reached 38.9 °C (102 °F), which the authors called too warm for lettuce roots (they cite 13–18 °C, or 55–64 °F, as optimal for lettuce). None of our sources gives a strawberry solution temperature. The closest figure is the University of Arizona's crown temperature of about 18 °C (64 °F), which raised winter yields by about 10% in their work. Keep the tank out of direct light and away from lamps and heaters.

On oxygen, Sato and colleagues cite older work showing that dissolved oxygen below 8 mg/L reduces calcium, potassium and phosphorus uptake. They found oxygen fell along a long channel. In a short home channel the drop is smaller, but warm solution holds less oxygen, which is one more reason to keep the tank cool.

## Light, air and humidity

Fruit needs much more light than lettuce. The UA CEAC short course gives a minimum DLI of 12 and an optimum near 20 mol/m²/day, with good indoor results at 200–250 µmol/m²/s. Purdue's HO-283-W puts tomatoes and strawberries at more than 20 mol/m²/day. Our reading: 12 is the floor, and the plants will use 20 or more if you can supply it.

| LED intensity at the canopy (µmol/m²/s) | 14 h on | 16 h on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 200 | 10.1 | 11.5 |
| 250 | 12.6 | 14.4 |
| 350 | 17.6 | 20.2 |

*DLI in mol/m²/day = µmol/m²/s × hours × 0.0036.*

Keep days at 20–24 °C (68–75 °F) and nights at 10–15 °C (50–59 °F) (University of Arizona); Cornell's indoor program used 72 °F by day and 58 °F at night. Humidity of 50–60% by day and about 75% at night (UA CEAC short course), with gentle air movement, keeps leaves and fruit drier. Our guide to [indoor hydroponic systems for home](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) covers lighting a home setup.

## Step-by-step: set up a small NFT strawberry run

1. **Choose the plants.** University of Minnesota Extension lists day-neutral varieties such as 'Albion', 'Monterey', 'San Andreas' and 'Seascape', which flower whenever temperatures are moderate. Cornell's indoor work used 'Albion' and 'Cabrillo'. Buy named, disease-free plants.
2. **Use light-tight channels.** Purdue specifies a watertight, dark channel. Light reaching the film encourages algae.
3. **Set the slope.** Fix each channel at 2% and check it with a level along its full length.
4. **Plumb the loop.** Pump from the tank to the high end of each channel, with a valve on each inlet, and give the low end a wide, free return to the tank.
5. **Measure the flow.** Time the outlet of each channel with a jug and set it to about 1 L/min (¼ gal/min).
6. **Fill and adjust.** Mix to EC 1.0–1.2 and adjust pH to 5.5–6.0. Run the system for a day before planting and check for leaks.
7. **Plant with the crown up.** Rinse the roots, set each plant in a net cup or collar so the crown sits above the lid, and let the roots hang into the channel. In Cornell's DWC runner work, only the bottom of the crown touched the water.
8. **Space for mature plants.** None of our sources gives an NFT spacing for strawberries; Oklahoma State uses 12 inches between strawberry plants on a vertical tower, a reasonable starting point.
9. **Let roots establish before fruit.** University of Minnesota Extension removes flowers for four to six weeks after planting, until plants have five or six expanded leaves (a field recommendation; indoors, use plant size as your guide).
10. **Pollinate by hand.** University of Minnesota Extension notes most varieties are self-fruitful but rely on insects and wind to move pollen. Indoors, brush open flowers every day or two.

Cornell limits each plant to three crowns and four flower clusters and harvests at least three times a week. Remove runners unless you want new plants. To make your own, Cornell rooted runner tips in aerated water and got roots 10–20 cm (4–8 in) long in about two weeks.

## Daily and weekly checks

- **Every day:** confirm the pump is running and each channel outlet is flowing, check the tank level, look for wilting, and check pH and EC at the same time of day (Oklahoma State HLA-6722).
- **Every week:** look at the roots in each channel for crowding or brown, slimy tips, and remeasure each channel's flow.
- **As plants grow:** University of Illinois Extension observed that water delivery may need adjusting as plants grow, set fruit and receive more light. Water use rises with a larger canopy; that alone does not mean the EC should go up.

Oregon State calls power and pump backups crucial for NFT. A battery-backed air or water pump, or simply a plan to hand-water the channels, protects a fruiting crop worth months of growing.

## Troubleshooting NFT strawberries

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole run wilts suddenly | Pump or power failure | Restore flow at once; add a backup | Oregon State EM 9457; Missouri G6986 |
| Plants at the far end weaker | Flow too low; warmer, lower-oxygen solution at the outlet | Raise flow toward 2 L/min; shorten or split the run | Purdue; Sato et al. 2007 |
| Solution pooling around roots | Too little slope or a sag in the channel | Re-level to 2%; support the channel along its length | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Roots matting and backing up the flow | Limited rooting space for a long-lived crop | Widen the outlet; replant or thin crowded plants | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Crown rotting | Crown set too low or splashed | Reset so the crown sits above the lid | Cornell |
| Yellow-green young leaves | pH too high, iron less available | Bring pH back to 5.5–6.0 | University of Arizona CEAC |
| Watery-tasting fruit | EC too high | Lower EC toward 1.0–1.2 | UA CEAC short course; Cornell |
| Sour fruit | Warm nights | Aim for nights at 10–15 °C (50–59 °F) | University of Arizona CEAC; UA CEAC short course |
| Small or misshapen berries | Incomplete pollination | Hand-pollinate open flowers every day or two | University of Minnesota Extension |
| Leggy plants, few flowers | Too little light | Raise DLI toward 20 mol/m²/day | UA CEAC short course; Purdue HO-283-W |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you grow strawberries in an NFT system?

Yes. University of Missouri Extension lists strawberries among the crops NFT commonly supports, and Oregon State lists "some strawberries". They are harder than lettuce because the plants live longer and their roots keep filling a channel with limited rooting space.

### What slope should an NFT channel have for strawberries?

Purdue recommends 1:100 but notes 1:30 and 1:40 are used; Oregon State gives a 2% minimum. We recommend 2% (1 in 50) as a starting point.

### What flow rate do strawberries need in NFT?

About 1 L/min (¼ gal/min) per channel, with an upper limit of 2 L/min, about ½ gal/min (Purdue). A 2007 HortTechnology paper calls 1–2 L/min the widely recommended range. No source gives a strawberry-specific NFT flow.

### What pH and EC do NFT strawberries need?

pH 5.5–6.0 and EC 1.0 dS/m or below (University of Arizona). Cornell reports 1.2 mS/cm works in most cases. Oklahoma State's general table lists 1.8–2.2, but we follow the strawberry-specific figures.

### Should the NFT pump run all the time?

Our sources describe NFT as a continuous recirculating stream and warn that plants can wilt quickly if the pump stops (Oregon State). None gives an on/off schedule for strawberries in NFT, so we recommend running it continuously. Towers are different: Oklahoma State runs tower strawberries 7–10 times a day for an hour.

### How long until NFT strawberries fruit?

Our sources do not give a single figure. University of Minnesota Extension removes the first flowers for four to six weeks after planting, and Cornell reports day-neutral plants then fruit continuously for six months to a year.

### Is NFT better than a tower for strawberries?

Both are pumped systems. A [hydroponic tower](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) fits more plants per square foot but gives the lower plants less light; NFT channels are easier to light evenly from above. For a simpler pump-free option for leafy greens, see [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/), and for lettuce in channels, our [hydroponic lettuce systems](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-systems/) guide.
