The best lettuce seed for hydroponics is a **butterhead or looseleaf variety that is described as slow to bolt and resistant to tipburn**, bought fresh from a supplier that prints the variety name and a germination figure on the packet. Sow one seed per pre-wetted plug, keep it near **68 °F (20 °C)**, and you should see seedlings in two to three days. The most common reason lettuce seed "fails" indoors is not bad seed but a warm room: above about **77 °F (25 °C)** lettuce seed can go dormant and refuse to sprout.

## Which lettuce type should you buy?

Start with the lettuce type, then pick a variety within it. UF/IFAS groups hydroponic lettuce into butterhead (Boston or Bibb), looseleaf, romaine and blends, and says iceberg (crisphead) is not yet considered appropriate for typical hydroponic production. The University of Kentucky adds that romaine is more challenging to grow in controlled environments than leaf and butterhead types.

| Type | What it gives you | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Butterhead (Boston, Bibb) | Soft, loose heads; the classic greenhouse lettuce | Whole-head harvests in DWC or NFT |
| Looseleaf (including oakleaf and lollo) | Open plants you can pick a few leaves at a time; many red and green colours | Beginners, Kratky jars, cut-and-come-again |
| Romaine | Upright, crunchy heads; mini "Little Gem" types stay small | Growers who can keep the room cool and steady |
| Blends and baby-leaf mixes | Several textures and colours from one sowing | Dense trays harvested young |

**Recommendation:** for a first hydroponic crop buy a butterhead or a looseleaf. They are the types extension sources describe as easiest indoors, and looseleaf lets you harvest leaves as you go if growth is uneven. Leave romaine for a second round, and skip iceberg.

A "hydroponic" label on a packet tells you little; any lettuce can be grown hydroponically. A better guide is what university trials report about bolting, tipburn and low-light colour. ### What a tipburn trial adds

Tipburn (brown, dry edges on the youngest leaves) is the main quality problem in fast indoor lettuce. In a 2023 Ohio State trial reported in an e-GRO alert, Chieri Kubota and John Ertle grew 20 varieties in NFT under deliberately tipburn-inducing conditions: 23/19 °C day and night, a daily light integral of 17 mol/m²/day over 16 hours, and very little air movement.

- **'Rabello'** (green romaine) showed no tipburn and was the most tolerant variety in the trial.
- **'Rouxai'** (red oakleaf) showed no visible tipburn at 28 days after transplanting, though a few hidden inner leaves were affected.
- **'Dragoon'** (green romaine) was the most sensitive.
- Most other varieties, including the industry-standard butterhead 'Rex', developed tipburn between 16 and 24 days after transplanting.

The authors found tipburn sensitivity was not linked to type, colour or yield, and stress these are preliminary results from one environment. The lesson: **if you run strong lights and a warm room, pick a variety reported as tipburn-tolerant, or plan to harvest earlier.** The full tipburn fix list is in the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/).

**Recommendation:** buy two varieties for your first sowing: a slow-bolting looseleaf such as 'New Red Fire' or 'Tropicana', and one reported as tipburn tolerant, such as 'Charles' (UF/IFAS) or 'Rouxai' (Ohio State trial). Grow them side by side and keep notes. 'Rex' is the commercial standard, but it developed tipburn in the Ohio State trial's warm, bright conditions.

## Pelleted, primed or raw seed?

Lettuce seed is small and light: Nebraska Extension puts it at about 25,000 seeds per ounce, or roughly 900 per gram. That is why commercial growers often buy **pelleted** seed, which is coated so each seed is larger, rounder and easier to place one at a time.

| Seed form | What it is | When to choose it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw (bare) seed | Plain seed, cheapest per packet | Small batches; fine if you can handle tiny seeds and keep plugs humid |
| Pelleted seed | Seed coated to make it easier to place one per plug | Rockwool sheets and plug trays where you want exactly one plant per cell |
| Primed seed | Seed treated by the supplier so it germinates faster and more evenly | Warm rooms, where thermodormancy is a risk |

The Cornell CEA lettuce handbook says humidity covers in its germination area are required when seeding with bare (not pelleted) seed, so if you sow raw seed, keep a dome or cover on until emergence. And UF/IFAS describes seed priming as an effective treatment to prevent thermodormancy, so primed seed is worth looking for if you cannot keep the germination area below 77 °F. Neither source claims pellets germinate better at home; treat them as a handling convenience.

## How to germinate lettuce seed for hydroponics

![Timeline from sowing lettuce seed to transplant: day 0 sow one seed per plug at about 20 °C, days 2–3 seedlings emerge, day 3 count germination, days 14–21 transplant at 3–4 true leaves; germination is best near 20 °C and above 25 °C seed can go dormant.](/images/guides/lettuce-seed-germination-timeline.svg)

These steps follow the University of Kentucky's propagation section, with the Cornell handbook's commercial schedule as a cross-check.

1. **Choose plugs.** Use bonded 1-inch plugs such as rockwool cubes if the seedlings are going into NFT or deep water culture; Kentucky strongly recommends them for those systems. Loose substrates work in high-density seedling trays.
2. **Pre-charge the plugs.** Soak rockwool in a weak nutrient solution at **EC 1.0 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0** before seeding (University of Kentucky). The [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) and the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) help you set the solution.
3. **Sow one seed per plug.** Both Kentucky and Cornell use one seed per plug or cell. Drop it into the hole in the plug rather than burying it deep.
4. **Keep it at 68 °F and humid.** Kentucky gives **68 °F (20 °C)** and relative humidity above 88% as the best germination conditions. Use a clear dome to hold humidity, and a heat mat only if the room is colder than that.
5. **Give gentle light.** Kentucky suggests minimal light of about **50 µmol/m²/s** to start germination. Cornell's commercial schedule also starts at 50 µmol/m²/s for the first 24 hours, noting it prevented seedlings from stretching.
6. **Remove the dome at emergence.** Seedlings usually appear about two days after seeding; take the dome off as soon as they emerge (Kentucky).
7. **Check germination on day 3.** Cornell counts germination percentage on day 3 to monitor seed quality and growing conditions, and removes double seedlings at the same stage. If you sowed two seeds in a plug and both came up, snip the weaker one at the base rather than pulling it.
8. **Raise the light.** As true leaves form, increase light gradually towards **200–300 µmol/m²/s** (Kentucky). Cornell's schedule uses 250 µmol/m²/s after the first day.
9. **Transplant at 3–4 true leaves.** Move seedlings into the system when they have three to four true leaves and roots are showing through the plug, usually **14–21 days after seeding** (Kentucky).

From there, the [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) covers EC, pH, light and harvest. If you have not chosen a system yet, compare them in [hydroponic lettuce systems compared](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-systems/) or start with a [deep water culture build](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

## Why warm rooms stop lettuce seed

Lettuce is one of a handful of crops whose seed can shut down in heat. UF/IFAS explains that for species such as lettuce, celery and pansy, germination at high temperatures, above **25 °C (77 °F)**, can induce thermodormancy, and that thermodormant seed may not germinate even after the temperature comes back down to normal.

Indoors, lights, pumps, closed tents and heat mats all add heat. Nebraska Extension's germination table lists 75 °F (24 °C) as the optimum for lettuce, close to Kentucky's 68 °F, and neither source suggests running warmer.

**What to do:**

- Germinate in the coolest spot you have, away from the light ballast or driver.
- Put a thermometer in the tray, not just in the room.
- Switch heat mats off unless the room is below about 68 °F.
- In a warm season or a warm house, buy primed seed (UF/IFAS describes priming as an effective treatment) or start seed in a cooler room and move seedlings under the lights once they have emerged.

## How long does lettuce seed keep?

Extension sources do not agree on lettuce seed life, and the difference is mostly about storage conditions:

| Source | Lettuce seed life | Storage advice given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| South Dakota State University Extension | Listed under 2 years | Relative humidity below 60%; temperatures above 90 °F greatly reduce viability; refrigeration can add several years |
| University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension | About 5 years under cool, dry conditions | Keep seed dry and at low temperatures |
| University of Illinois Extension | About 6 years | Low humidity and below 45 °F |

**Recommendation:** plan on seed bought in the last year or two for anything important, and store opened packets in a sealed jar in the refrigerator. Older seed is not worthless: if it has been kept cool and dry it may still germinate well, but run a quick germination test before you rely on it.

### A ten-seed germination test

University of Illinois Extension describes a simple home test:

1. Place **10 seeds** on a damp paper towel, roll or fold it, and put it in a bag or container to keep it moist.
2. Leave it somewhere warm (sunlight is not needed) for the number of days on the seed packet. For lettuce, keep "warm" within the 68–75 °F range above, not hotter.
3. Count the seeds that sprouted.

| Seeds sprouted out of 10 | What Illinois Extension advises |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | Sow as normal |
| 7–9 | Sow more seeds per plug or more plugs than you need |
| 6 or fewer | Buy new seed |

For a sharper estimate, Nebraska Extension suggests testing 25 or 50 seeds at 70–75 °F. Its table gives 80% as the minimum germination standard for lettuce, which is a useful benchmark to compare against.

## What to look for on a seed packet

When you buy lettuce seed, look for:

- **The variety name**, not just "salad mix", so you can compare and reorder.
- **A germination percentage and test date.**
- **Indoor traits:** slow bolting, heat or tipburn tolerance, low-light colour.
- **Disease-resistance codes**, which cover only the named diseases.
- **Clean seed.** UF/IFAS advises small-system growers to plant certified seed that is free of pathogens and pests.

Organic seed is a fine choice, but organic wording on the packet does not by itself make your hydroponic harvest certified organic; certification depends on how the crop is produced. Our [organic hydroponic nutrients guide](/nutrients-water/organic-hydroponic-nutrients/) explains more.

## Troubleshooting lettuce seed

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nothing sprouts after 4–5 days | Plugs too warm (above 77 °F) causing thermodormancy, or old seed | Move the tray somewhere cooler, check plug temperature, run a ten-seed test on the packet |
| Patchy germination | Plugs dried out, raw seed without a humidity cover, or seed sown too deep | Keep a dome on until emergence; keep plugs evenly moist; sow on the surface of the plug hole |
| Tall, thin, floppy seedlings | Too little light, or the dome left on too long | Remove the dome at emergence; raise light from about 50 towards 200–300 µmol/m²/s |
| Seedlings yellow or stall in rockwool | Plugs not pre-charged, or pH off | Pre-soak at EC 1.0 and pH 5.5–6.0 before sowing |
| Two seedlings in one plug | Two seeds dropped, common with raw seed | Snip the weaker one at the base by day 3 |
| Plants bolt early or tipburn after transplant | Variety not suited to warm rooms or strong light | Choose slow-bolting, heat- or tipburn-tolerant varieties next time; see the [lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best lettuce seed for hydroponics?

A butterhead or looseleaf variety described as slow to bolt and tipburn resistant. UF/IFAS names 'Rex' as an industry standard, 'Charles' as slow to bolt and strong against tipburn, and 'New Red Fire' and 'Tropicana' as heat tolerant. In an Ohio State tipburn trial, 'Rabello' and 'Rouxai' showed no visible tipburn at 28 days.

### How long does lettuce seed take to germinate?

Two to three days at the right temperature (Nebraska Extension); the University of Kentucky gives about three days at 68 °F.

### Why won't my lettuce seeds germinate?

The usual culprit indoors is heat. Above 77 °F lettuce seed can become thermodormant (UF/IFAS). Old or badly stored seed is the second cause, which a ten-seed test will show.

### Should I buy pelleted lettuce seed for hydroponics?

Only if you want easier handling: pellets make it simple to place one seed per plug. Raw seed works too if you keep a humidity dome on until emergence, which Cornell's handbook requires for bare seed.

### Which lettuce grows best in a warm room?

Heat-tolerant, slow-bolting varieties from the UF/IFAS list, such as 'Cherokee', 'New Red Fire', 'Tropicana' or 'Salanova'. Germinate the seed below 77 °F, or buy primed seed.
