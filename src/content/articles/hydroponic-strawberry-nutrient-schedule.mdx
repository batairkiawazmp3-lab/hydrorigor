The best-supported nutrient schedule for hydroponic strawberries is almost no schedule at all: **one strawberry solution at EC 1.0–1.2 mS/cm and pH 5.5–6.0, held steady from transplant to the last harvest.** The University of Arizona and Ohio State set EC at **1.0 or less**, Cornell uses **1.2**, and a Korean trial found **0.8–1.2** best in the cool season. What changes by stage is how you water and what you remove, not the recipe.

The common advice to "switch to a bloom formula" with more potassium once fruit sets did not hold up in the newest trial we found. When Arizona State raised potassium from **117 to 348 mg/L** (EC 1.0 to 1.9) after the first three weeks, 'San Andreas' gave **45% less fruit by weight** and berries got smaller in both varieties tested (Ries, Meng and Park 2025).

## Quick answer: strawberry feeding numbers

| Measure | Target | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EC, every stage | 1.0 or less; 1.2 used by Cornell | University of Arizona; Ohio State; Cornell (Greenhouse Grower 2021) |
| Solution pH | 5.5–6.0 | University of Arizona; Ohio State |
| Root-zone pH warning | Above 7.0: yellow-green young leaves (iron) | Ohio State; University of Arizona |
| Flush or replace | When root-zone EC passes 1.2 | Ohio State |
| Base recipe | Yamazaki strawberry solution: 77 N, 24 P, 117 K, 40 Ca, 12 Mg, 16 S mg/L at EC 1.0 | Ries, Meng and Park 2025 |
| Potassium for fruit | No benefit above 117 mg/L | Ries, Meng and Park 2025 |
| Nitrogen form | Mostly nitrate; never all ammonium | USDA-ARS (Yafuso and Boldt 2024); Ohio State |
| Runner plugs | 50–100 mg/L N | Yafuso and Boldt 2024 |
| Substrate watering | About 33 mL (1.1 fl oz) per event, 6–12 events a day | Ohio State |
| Drainage | 20–30% of what you apply | Ohio State |

On a meter that reads in ppm, EC 1.0 is about 500 ppm on a 0.5 scale or 700 ppm on a 0.7 scale (our conversion; check which scale your meter uses). The [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains both. To compare strawberries with other crops, use the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).

## The schedule by growth stage

EC range: University of Arizona, Ohio State and Cornell. Drainage and flush point: Ohio State. First ripe fruit about 7 weeks after potting dormant crowns: Paparozzi et al. 2018.*

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Runner plugs | Rooting daughter plants | Not given; 50–100 mg/L N | Weak feed in plug trays | Yafuso and Boldt 2024 |

### Runner plugs and bare-root plants

USDA researchers rooted daughter plants in peat cubes and fed them a 15-2.5-12.5 fertilizer at **50–100 mg/L nitrogen**, and kept mother plants at 150 mg/L N with EC under 2.0 (Yafuso and Boldt 2024). Ohio State notes that growers raise ammonium for runner propagation, because ammonium pushes plants toward leaves and runners. That is the one stage where a different feed has a stated reason. If you buy bare-root plants or plugs, skip this stage.

### Establishment, weeks 0–3

Arizona State started every plant on the **standard Yamazaki strawberry solution** for the first three weeks: 77 mg/L nitrogen, 24 phosphorus, 117 potassium, 40 calcium, 12 magnesium and 16 sulfur, at **EC 1.0**, pH 5.8, made from a two-part strawberry fertilizer (Ries, Meng and Park 2025). Runners and flowers were removed daily for the first six weeks, so the plants put their energy into crowns and roots first.

USDA used a modified Yamazaki solution at 100 mg/L nitrogen and **EC 1.0** for the first two weeks in deep water culture, with 80% of the nitrogen as nitrate (Yafuso and Boldt 2024).

### Growth and first flowers, weeks 3–7

Keep the same solution. The University of Arizona holds strawberry EC at **1.0 dS/m or less** and Ohio State does the same; Cornell reports **1.2** works in most cases. Remove runners as they appear: Cornell limits each plant to three crowns and four flower stalks.

### Fruiting, from about week 7

This is where most feeding charts tell you to raise EC or switch to a bloom formula. The trials we read do not support it (next section). In fruit, plants use more water, so the change is in **watering**, not strength. In substrate, Ohio State applies about **33 mL (1.1 fl oz) per plant at a time, 6–12 times a day**, which works out to roughly 200–400 mL (7–14 fl oz) per plant per day in winter and spring, and aims for **20–30% drainage**. Growers often flush when root-zone EC passes **1.2**.

Plants in Nebraska gave their first ripe fruit about **7 weeks** after dormant crowns were potted, with 3–4 weeks from flower to ripe berry (Paparozzi et al. 2018). Indoors under LEDs, where flowers were removed early, harvest began **66 days** after transplant (Ries and Park 2024). Expect this stage from about week 7 to week 10.

## Should you boost potassium for fruiting?

![Bar chart of what extra potassium did in an Arizona State trial: raising potassium from 117 to 348 milligrams per liter, and EC from 1.0 to 1.9, cut San Andreas fruit mass by 45 percent and fruit number by 34 percent; Monterey yield did not change; berries were smaller and more acidic in both varieties; leaf calcium fell 37 and 29 percent.](/images/guides/hydroponic-strawberry-potassium-boost.svg)

*Arizona State grew 'Monterey' and 'San Andreas' in deep water culture under LEDs (350 µmol/m²/s, 18 h, 23 °C or 73 °F) with nitrogen held at 77 mg/L. Ries, Meng and Park, HortScience 60(2), 2025.*

The case for more potassium is real on paper. Strawberry fruit "accumulate more K than all other plant organs combined," the California fertilization guidelines say, and field crops take up about 240 lb of potassium per acre in a season, much of it leaving in the fruit (CDFA and UC Davis).

**The trial evidence points the other way for hydroponics.** Arizona State gave plants three weeks on the standard Yamazaki solution, then four potassium levels (117, 194, 271 and 348 mg/L, as potassium sulfate), which raised EC from 1.0 to 1.3, 1.6 and 1.9:

| Result at 348 vs 117 mg/L K | 'San Andreas' | 'Monterey' |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total fruit mass per plant | −45% | No change |
| Fruit number per plant | −34% | No change |
| Single berry size | Smaller | Smaller |
| Acidity | Higher | Higher |
| Leaf potassium | +22% | +26% |
| Leaf calcium | −29% | −37% |
| Time to flower and first fruit | No change | No change |

The authors found no benefit above 117 mg/L and suggest the best level may be at or below it. Extra potassium also pushed calcium and magnesium out of the leaves, which matters for tipburn (see troubleshooting).

**A trial that found the opposite.** A Moroccan pot trial with 'Fortuna', 'San Andreas' and 'Sabrina' changed the potassium-to-nitrogen balance between the growth and production phases, with the same total N, P, K, Ca and Mg in every treatment. One balance (written 2.6/1.0 in the abstract) gave **30% more yield**, 14% more soluble solids and 19% longer shelf life (Nakro et al. 2023). We read only the abstract, which gives no concentrations in mg/L or EC values, so we cannot say how its feed compares with the Yamazaki solution.

**Our reading:** a K boost is not needed when the base solution is a complete strawberry formula, and in the one hydroponic trial that measured it, raising K by raising EC cost fruit. If you want to test the Moroccan approach, change the K:N ratio without raising total strength, and keep EC near 1.0–1.2.

## How strong should the solution be? The EC conflict

![Range chart of strawberry EC targets by source in millisiemens per centimeter: University of Arizona and Ohio State 1.0 or less; a Korean winter trial 0.8 to 1.2; a University of Georgia trial 0.75 to 1.25; Cornell 1.2; the Arizona State potassium trial 1.0 to 1.9, where higher EC cut yield in one variety; and Oklahoma State 1.8 to 2.2 from a general crop table.](/images/guides/hydroponic-strawberry-ec-sources.svg)

*Strawberry EC by source. University of Arizona; Ohio State; Jun et al. 2013 ('Maehyang', cool season); Hutchinson et al. 2025; Cornell (Greenhouse Grower 2021); Ries, Meng and Park 2025; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 Table 2.*

| Source | EC (mS/cm) | What it rests on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| University of Arizona | 1.0 or less | Hydroponic strawberry research program |
| Ohio State | 1.0 or less; both Japanese strawberry solutions about 1.0 at full strength | Arizona and Ohio State studies |
| Jun et al. 2013, Korea | 0.8–1.2 best (0.6, 0.8, 1.2 and 1.8 tested) | Coir trial, cool season |
| University of Georgia | 0.75–1.25 | Four-system yield trial |
| Cornell | 1.2 | Greenhouse production |
| Delaware SARE | 1.2 | Lit indoor gutters |
| Arizona State (Park 2023) | About 1.7–2.4 | Light trial in rafts; EC was not the variable |
| Oklahoma State | 1.8–2.2 | General table covering many crops |

Most sources sit at 1.0–1.2. In the Korean trial, berries in the first cluster were lightest at EC 1.8 and heaviest at 0.8 and 1.2, and soluble solids (sweetness) were highest at 0.6 (Jun et al. 2013). Oklahoma State's 1.8–2.2 is the outlier; it comes from a table covering dozens of crops. We recommend **starting at 1.0** and going no higher than about 1.2 unless your own harvest weights say otherwise.

## pH, nitrogen form and buffering

**Target pH 5.5–6.0.** Ohio State notes that the feed sometimes has to go in slightly below 5.5 to keep the root zone in range, and that drainage pH above 7.0 turns young leaves yellow-green from iron shortage.

**In deep water culture, pH falls fast.** USDA grew strawberries in 3.75 L (about 1 gal) reservoirs and measured:

| Nitrogen as nitrate : ammonium | Average pH | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 : 0 (with 0.8 mM potassium bicarbonate) | 5.8 | Held steady | Yafuso and Boldt 2024 |
| 80 : 20 to 20 : 80 | About 4.2 | Dropped from 5.7 within 2–3 days | Yafuso and Boldt 2024 |
| 0 : 100 | Fell | All plants dead by 90 days | Yafuso and Boldt 2024 |

Runner count and plant mass did not differ between the surviving ratios, so the problem with ammonium in rafts is pH control, not growth. In a later test, a 5 mM MES buffer held pH at 5.6, steadier than potassium bicarbonate, though MES is a laboratory chemical that costs more. **Our pick for home DWC:** a mostly nitrate strawberry formula, a pH check every 2–3 days, and a full change of solution weekly, as the USDA team did. Our [pH meter guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) covers calibration.

## Leaf tests by stage

If you send leaves to a lab, the California guidelines give these ranges for high-yielding 'Albion' fields (% of dry matter). They come from soil-grown fields, so treat them as a guide, not a hydroponic standard.

| Nutrient | Before harvest | Main harvest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrogen | 3.1–3.8% | 2.4–3.0% |
| Phosphorus | 0.5–0.9% | 0.3–0.4% |
| Potassium | 1.8–2.2% | 1.3–1.8% |
| Petiole nitrate-N | Above 1,000 ppm | Above 400 ppm |

Leaf nitrogen and potassium fall naturally once fruiting starts, so a lower reading in harvest is expected, not a reason to strengthen the feed.

## Step by step: mixing and running the schedule

1. **Choose a complete strawberry formula** with calcium, ideally a two-part Yamazaki-type mix like the one the Arizona State trials used. Delaware used an 8-10-26 base with a 15-0-0 fertilizer at EC 1.2. Mix the parts separately, following [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/).
2. **Test your water.** Note its starting EC so you know how much of the reading is fertilizer.
3. **Mix to EC 1.0**, stir and let the reading settle. Use the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) to scale label rates.
4. **Set pH to 5.5–6.0**, adding acid slowly and waiting a few minutes between doses.
5. **Weeks 0–3:** keep EC at 1.0; pick off flowers and runners.
6. **Weeks 3–7:** hold EC 1.0–1.2; keep removing flowers until week 6; thin to three crowns; keep pulling runners.
7. **From first fruit:** water more often rather than feeding stronger; in substrate, aim for 20–30% drainage.
8. **Measure the drain or reservoir** two or three times a week. If root-zone EC passes 1.2, water with fresh solution or plain water to flush.
9. **In DWC, change the solution weekly** and check pH every 2–3 days.
10. **Weigh each harvest.** It is the only way to know whether a feeding change helped. Our [strawberry yield guide](/crops/hydroponic-strawberry-yield/) gives trial yields to compare against.

For the system side, see the [Dutch bucket guide](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/), the [drip system guide](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/) or [NFT strawberries](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/); light, temperature and varieties are in the main [hydroponic strawberries guide](/crops/hydroponic-strawberries/).

## Troubleshooting feeding problems

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow-green young leaves | Root-zone pH above 7.0 (iron) | Feed slightly more acidic; aim for 5.5–6.0 | Ohio State; University of Arizona |
| Brown, dry new leaf tips or calyx | Tipburn: calcium not reaching growing tips, usually from dry air | Raise night humidity; extra calcium in the feed often does not help | University of Arizona (Tip Burn) |
| Small, sour berries after a feed change | EC or potassium raised for fruiting | Return to EC 1.0–1.2 and the standard recipe | Ries, Meng and Park 2025; Jun et al. 2013 |
| Drain EC climbing above 1.2 | Salts building up in the medium | Flush with fresh solution or water; increase drainage | Ohio State |
| pH crashing to about 4 in DWC | Ammonium in the feed | Use a mostly nitrate formula; buffer; change weekly | Yafuso and Boldt 2024 |
| Lots of leaves and runners, few flowers | Too much ammonium; or a short day | Cut ammonium; give 16-hour days | Ohio State; Park et al. 2023 |
| Older leaves brown at the edges, pale soft fruit | Potassium deficiency | Check the formula contains potassium at full rate | CDFA and UC Davis |
| Wet medium, drainage on the first waterings | Watering too much early in the day | Water less at dawn; reach drainage later in the day | Ohio State |

On tipburn, the University of Arizona found the root zone usually already has enough calcium. Covering plants at night to keep the air humid cut tip burn from **47.2% to 9.0%** and calyx burn from **48.6% to 16.7%** in a 2010 greenhouse trial.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do hydroponic strawberries need a bloom nutrient?

Not on the evidence we found. The trials that produced good yields ran one strawberry solution throughout, and raising potassium for fruiting reduced yield in one of two varieties at Arizona State. A complete strawberry formula at EC 1.0–1.2 covers flowering and fruiting.

### What EC should strawberries be at when fruiting?

1.0–1.2 mS/cm, the same as earlier. The University of Arizona sets 1.0 or less, Cornell uses 1.2 and the Korean trial found 0.8–1.2 best. Oklahoma State lists 1.8–2.2, but that comes from a general table covering many crops.

### What ppm is that?

About 500–600 ppm on a 0.5-scale meter or 700–840 ppm on a 0.7-scale meter for EC 1.0–1.2 (our conversion).

### Can I use a general hydroponic or lettuce nutrient?

Strawberries can grow on it, but check it supplies calcium and magnesium and run it at EC 1.0–1.2 rather than the label's full strength for fruiting crops. See [best hydroponic nutrients](/best/hydroponic-nutrients-for-home-growers/) for how to read a label.

### How often should I change the solution?

The USDA team changed DWC solution weekly. In drip-fed substrate, the 20–30% drainage does much of the refreshing; flush when drain EC passes 1.2.
