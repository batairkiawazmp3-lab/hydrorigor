Hydroponic kale grows well at **pH 5.8** and an EC of **1.5–2.1 mS/cm**, with about **17 mol/m²/day** of light and cool air: an Ohio State trial ran **69 °F (20.7 °C) by day and 56 °F (13.6 °C) at night**. A plain air stone gives the roots enough oxygen. Cut baby leaves about **13 days after sowing**, or grow full plants and start harvesting **4 weeks after transplant**, then keep cutting outer leaves every two to three weeks.

The research agrees on pH and oxygen but not on how strong to feed. This guide shows where the studies differ and which number to start with.

## Which system to use

Kale is a leafy green, so it fits the same systems as lettuce. Cornell's Neil Mattson writes that his head-lettuce method in [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) "can be similarly used" for kale. The Ohio State trial grew kale in an [NFT system](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/), and the Florida trial used coir bags fed by drip lines.

For a home grower:

- **Deep water culture (DWC):** the simplest choice for full plants. The big water volume keeps pH and EC steady, and an air stone covers the oxygen (see below).
- **Kratky:** works for a single batch of baby leaf or young plants. The [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) explains the air gap. For repeat cuts over months, a system you can top up and aerate is easier.
- **NFT channels:** good for many plants. The Ohio trial fit 18 plants in each 4 m (13 ft) channel, roughly 22 cm (9 in) apart (HydroRigor's arithmetic from the stated numbers).

If you are choosing between systems, the [lettuce systems comparison](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce-systems/) applies to kale too.

## EC: where the studies disagree

This is the one number the research does not settle.

| Study | EC tested | What happened to kale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ohio State, NFT (2024) | 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.1 | Yield rose with EC: 38.9% more fresh weight at 2.1 than at 1.2 |
| Iowa State, deep flow (2021) | 0.5 to 5.0 | "Growth of kale was unaffected by EC"; grow at 1.0–5.0 to avoid sulfur deficiency |
| UF/IFAS, coir bags (2015) | 2.0 set point | High yields over six harvests |
| Wortman, ebb and flow (2015) | Aquaponic-style, below 1.0 at pH 7.2 | Marketable kale yield fell 76% |

What this means: kale tolerates a wide EC range, but very weak, high-pH solutions clearly hurt it. Where yield changed, more feed helped. The Ohio authors add that kale may need more nitrogen than a lettuce-based EC 1.8 setting gives.

**Start at EC 1.5–2.0.** If plants are pale or slow, move up to about 2.1, the strongest level that still raised yield in Ohio. Above that, no study here found a benefit. To match a nutrient label to your reservoir, use the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/). For other crops, the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) covers them.

## pH: keep it near 6

Both the Ohio and Florida trials held the solution at **pH 5.8**. Cornell's lettuce method, which Mattson says also works for kale, uses 5.5–6.0.

An Arizona State poster tested how much pH drift kale tolerates. Red Russian kale grew in deep water culture for 31 days, either uncontrolled (pH averaged 7.3) or held near 6 with different allowed swings. Shoot weight and leaf area were higher whenever pH was held near 6, even with swings of up to ±1.5, than in the uncontrolled tank. The authors also saw lower EC when pH stayed steady, which they read as the plants taking up more nutrients.

In short: aim for 5.8–6.0 and check it every few days. Small wobbles are fine. A tank that creeps up toward 7 and stays there is not.

## Light and temperature

**Light.** Cornell grew baby-leaf kale at a daily light integral (DLI) of 17 mol/m²/day. In a growth-chamber study, kale grown for 28 days gained 47% more dry weight as DLI rose from 8 to 14, so more light kept helping. Iowa State found growth rising with DLI up to 22 for these greens, with no ceiling except for arugula.

Indoors, you can work out your DLI from a light's rated PPFD. A light at 220 µmol/m²/s running 24 hours, as in the Arizona trial, gives about 19 mol/m²/day (HydroRigor's arithmetic). Most home growers run fewer hours: the same light for 16 hours gives about 12.7.

**Temperature.** Kale is a cool-season crop. Texas A&M lists a field optimum of 60–65 °F (16–18 °C) as a monthly average, and says it does not tolerate monthly means much above 70 °F (21 °C). The Ohio trial grew well at 69 °F (20.7 °C) days and 56 °F (13.6 °C) nights. Cornell's baby-leaf method ran warmer, at 75/66 °F (24/19 °C), though Mattson notes they had not tested the best light and temperature for these crops.

A cool room or a winter windowsill suits kale better than a hot grow tent.

## Oxygen: an air stone is enough

University of Georgia researchers tested whether extra oxygen helps kale in deep water culture. They raised dissolved oxygen to 10, 15 and 20 mg/L with an oxygen generator and compared it with a normal air pump and air stones, which held 6.5–8 mg/L. Arugula grew much more with extra oxygen. Kale (KX-1 and Red Russian) did not change. The authors conclude that standard air-pump aeration meets kale's needs. The oxygen generator also used about 2.4 times as much energy.

So a correctly sized air pump is all you need. The [DWC air pump guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/) covers sizing.

## Step-by-step: grow kale in a DWC bucket or tote

1. **Pick a variety.** In the Florida trial, Winterbor, Red Russian and Nero di Toscano gave the highest total yields and were still producing in July. Red Russian is the most studied in hydroponics.
2. **Start seeds** in rockwool or coir plugs. For baby leaf, Cornell sowed one seed per cell in 336-cell trays. For full plants, one seed per plug is enough.
3. **Germinate.** Seeds sprouted in about 3 days in Cornell's trays. Keep plugs moist, not flooded.
4. **Mix the solution** at EC 1.5–2.0 and pH 5.8, using a complete hydroponic nutrient. The [vegetables nutrient guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-vegetables/) explains mixing order.
5. **Transplant at about 3 weeks**, as in the Ohio trial, into net pots in the lid. Space plants about 6 in (15 cm) apart for leaf picking, or wider for big plants.
6. **Run the air stone** around the clock.
7. **Check pH and EC every few days.** Top up with plain water, and correct pH back toward 5.8–6.0.
8. **Start harvesting at about 4 weeks after transplant** (next section).

## Harvest: cut so it keeps growing

![Kale timeline: sow on day 0; baby leaf about day 13; transplant at week 3; first full harvest about week 7; cut again every 13 to 22 days. Cut outer leaves only, no more than one third of the plant, and leave the center and at least 5 leaves](/images/guides/hydroponic-kale-harvest-timeline.svg)

*Days from sowing, combining Cornell's baby-leaf method, the Ohio State schedule and the Florida harvest dates.*

**Baby leaf.** Cornell's Red Russian kale reached 14 oz of saleable leaves per square foot (about 4.3 kg/m², HydroRigor's conversion) after 3 days of germination and 10 days floating. Cut the whole tray at once.

**Full plants.** The Florida trial took six harvests from the same plants, starting 59 days after setting them out under shade and then every 13–22 days (dates from the report, HydroRigor's arithmetic). Indoors at steadier temperatures, the Ohio plants were ready 4 weeks after transplant.

To keep plants producing, extension guides agree on the method:

- Cut outer leaves near the base, about a fistful per harvest and **no more than one-third of the plant** at once (University of Georgia).
- Take the **lower leaves** and leave **at least 5 larger leaves** on the plant (NC State).
- Pick leaves while they are tender, **no more than 8 in (20 cm) long** (Oregon State).
- Never cut the small center leaves. That is where new growth comes from.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pale, slow plants | Feed too weak for kale | Raise EC toward 2.0–2.1 |
| Pale leaves, low yield | Weak solution with pH near 7 (leaves up to 37% less green in Wortman's trial) | Bring pH back to 5.8–6.0 and EC up to 1.5–2.0 |
| Slow growth under a dim light | Low DLI (kale dry weight rose 47% from DLI 8 to 14) | Raise DLI toward 17 by adding hours or a stronger light |
| Slow growth in a hot room | Kale prefers cool air | Aim for days near 69 °F (21 °C) and cooler nights |
| Plant stops regrowing after a harvest | Center leaves cut, or too much taken at once | Leave the center and 5+ leaves; take one-third at most |
| Wilting, weak roots in DWC | Too little oxygen (Cornell reports crop failure below 3 ppm) | Check the air pump and stone are running |

## FAQ

**What pH does hydroponic kale need?**
Aim for 5.8–6.0. Both university trials used 5.8, and an Arizona State test found kale grew better held near 6 than drifting up to 7.3.

**What EC is best for kale?**
Start at 1.5–2.0 mS/cm. Ohio State got the most yield at 2.1, the highest it tested, while Iowa State saw no difference from 0.5 to 5.0. Within Iowa State's tested range a stronger solution did no harm, while a very weak one at high pH cut yield by 76% in another study.

**How long does hydroponic kale take?**
About 13 days from seed for baby leaf, or roughly 7 weeks from seed to the first full harvest (3 weeks as a seedling, then 4 weeks in the system). After that, you can cut every 2–3 weeks.

**Does kale need extra oxygen like lettuce?**
No more than a normal air stone gives. The University of Georgia found extra oxygen did not change kale growth.

**Can I grow kale in Kratky?**
Yes, for baby leaf or one round of young plants. For months of repeat cuts, a system you can aerate and top up is easier. Start with [how to grow hydroponic lettuce](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/), since kale follows the same steps, or try [microgreens](/crops/hydroponic-microgreens/) for the fastest harvest.

**Which kale varieties grow best hydroponically?**
In the Florida trial, Winterbor, Red Russian and Nero di Toscano had the highest yields. Red Russian appears in almost every hydroponic kale study.
