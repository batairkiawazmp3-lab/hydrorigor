The best indoor hydroponic system for a home is the one that matches **what you want to grow, how much light you can give it and whether you can keep a pump running**. For lettuce, greens and herbs, a passive **Kratky** bucket (no pump, about **1–1.5 gallons of water per lettuce plant**, UF/IFAS) or a small **deep water culture (DWC)** tub (about **3–4 lettuce plants per square foot**, University of Kentucky) is the simplest start. For tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers, choose **buckets of perlite fed by drip** (Oregon State; Virginia Tech). Whatever you buy, budget for light: lettuce needs a **daily light integral (DLI) of 12–17 mol/m²/day** (Kentucky), and fruiting crops need **more than 20** (Purdue).

Oregon State Extension sums it up: "the best system depends on your crops and space". Product listings for indoor systems mostly sell features, such as apps, plant counts and colour-changing lights. This guide compares the six system types you can buy or build, then lists what to check on a product page before you pay. HydroRigor has not tested any of these products; every number comes from the university and extension sources listed at the end.

## The six indoor system types

Most indoor systems sold for homes are versions of six root-zone designs. Knowing which one a product uses tells you more than its feature list, because it decides what has to keep working for your plants to survive.

![Table comparing six indoor hydroponic system types. Kratky bucket: no power; lettuce and leafy greens; 1–1.5 gallons of water per lettuce plant. DWC tub: air pump running 24 hours; lettuce, spinach, kale and herbs; 3–4 lettuce per square foot, 12 inches deep. NFT channels: pump with constant flow; lettuce, herbs and leafy greens; 2% slope, roots dry fast if the pump stops. Vertical tower: pump; lettuce, greens, herbs and strawberries; 28 plants, light falls toward the base. Ebb and flow: pump and a reliable timer; seedlings, herbs, peppers and greens; flood to about 1 inch below the top of the media. Dutch buckets with drip: pump and timer; tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers; perlite media, best tomato and pepper yields.](/images/guides/indoor-hydroponic-system-types-compared.svg)

*The six designs behind most home systems. Figures from UF/IFAS HS1488, Oregon State EM 9455–9458, University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63, Oklahoma State HLA-6724 and Virginia Tech SPES-466.*

### 1. Kratky (passive water culture)

All the nutrient solution goes in before planting and nothing is added. As the plant drinks, the level falls and an air gap opens for the upper roots. UF/IFAS describes a 5-gallon bucket with three 2-inch net cups, about 1 inch of air space at the start, and no pumps or electricity. Each lettuce plant uses 1–1.5 gallons from seed to harvest; transplants go in after 1–3 weeks and are ready about 3–4 weeks later. UF/IFAS adds that fruiting crops do better in other methods.

**Choose it if** you want lettuce or greens with nothing to plug in. Oklahoma State calls the related wick system the simplest of all.

### 2. Deep water culture (DWC)

Roots hang in aerated solution under a floating raft or a lid. An air pump runs all the time. The University of Kentucky describes raft tanks at least 12 inches deep with 3–4 lettuce plants per square foot. Oregon State recommends DWC for lettuce, spinach, kale and herbs and notes that the large water volume buffers temperature, but the tank must sit on the ground with its bottom level to within one inch. University of Missouri Extension points out that if the pump stops, roots are still in water. Full details are in the [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/).

### 3. Nutrient film technique (NFT)

A pump sends a thin stream of solution along sloped channels. Oregon State asks for a slope of at least 2% and says limited rooting space suits short-lived or small-rooted crops such as lettuce, herbs, spinach, arugula, kale, chard and mustard. The catch: plants can wilt quickly when the pump or power fails, so Oregon State calls backups crucial. Oklahoma State notes that NFT runs on constant flow, so it needs no timer.

### 4. Vertical towers

A pump lifts solution to the top of a column and it trickles down past roots in pockets. Oklahoma State gives 28 plants per tower, with two towers fitting a 5 ft × 5 ft space and lights above them indoors. The trade-off is light: in a peer-reviewed lettuce study, light and lettuce weight both fell from the top of a vertical system to its base, even though the vertical system produced more per unit of floor area. See the [hydroponic tower garden guide](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/).

### 5. Ebb and flow (flood and drain)

A tray of media floods from a reservoir, then drains. Oregon State says to flood to about an inch below the top of the media, drain when it is soaked, and flood again before the media dries; that needs a reliable timer. It suits seedlings, peppers, herbs, radishes and leafy greens.

### 6. Dutch buckets with drip

Each plant sits in its own bucket of perlite, fed by drip emitters. Oregon State lists tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, zucchini, eggplant, beans and small melons, and notes the larger rooting volume buffers better than NFT. Virginia Tech's trial ran drip stakes at 0.5 gallons per hour, and its best tomato and pepper yields came from media systems.

### What about countertop smart gardens?

All-in-one countertop units add a built-in light, a timer and often an app. They are still one of the designs above underneath, so check which one before comparing features. The [smart indoor hydroponic systems guide](/systems/smart-indoor-hydroponic-systems/) covers what automation can and cannot do.

## Which system fits which crop?

| Crop | First choice indoors | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lettuce | Kratky or DWC | No pump (Kratky) or roots stay wet if the pump stops (DWC); short crop | UF/IFAS HS1488; University of Kentucky |
| Spinach, kale, chard | DWC or NFT | Listed for both; Virginia Tech's spinach developed Pythium root rot in NFT and DWC, so keep solution cool and aerated | Oregon State EM 9455 and 9457; Virginia Tech |
| Basil and other herbs | NFT or DWC | Greens and herbs gave their highest yields in these two | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |

**Recommendation:** if this is your first system, start with lettuce in a Kratky bucket or a small DWC tub. Both are simple to build and teach pH, EC and light before you spend money. Move to drip buckets only when you want fruiting crops. [The best hydroponic system for home growers](/best/hydroponic-system-for-home-growers/) goes deeper on that choice.

## Light decides more than the system does

Indoors, the system looks after the roots, but light sets how much you harvest. DLI is the total light a plant receives in a day. University of Georgia Extension lists light as one of the essentials of a working home setup, alongside air, a balanced nutrient solution and water.

![Bar chart of daily light integral needs in mol per square metre per day. Seedlings and cuttings 8–10 and potted plants 10–15 (Purdue). Lettuce 12–17 (University of Kentucky). Leafy greens 15–20 (Purdue). Greens and herbs yielded more at 17 or more (Virginia Tech). Tomatoes and strawberries more than 20 (Purdue). A shaded band shows that a Purdue greenhouse in Indiana received under 10 from November through February.](/images/guides/indoor-crop-light-needs-dli.svg)

*Light targets by crop. Figures from Purdue HO-283-W, University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 and Virginia Tech SPES-466.*

A sunny window is rarely enough in winter. Purdue measured a greenhouse in West Lafayette, Indiana, and found winter sunlight of about 5 mol/m²/day, with levels below 10 from November through February. A greenhouse lets in light from above and all sides, so a window that lights plants from one side should not be expected to do better. In Purdue's trial, 15 lettuce varieties grown for 28 days averaged 0.90 lb/m² at a DLI of 5.6 and 6.7 lb/m² at 19.8.

University of Illinois Extension suggests 6–8 hours of light a day for a home tank. That is a much looser guide than a DLI, because hours alone ignore how strong the light is. If a grow light lists its intensity at plant level (PPFD, in µmol/m²/s), you can work out the DLI yourself: **PPFD × hours on × 0.0036**. The 0.0036 simply converts seconds to hours (3,600) and micromoles to moles (÷ 1,000,000). For example, 200 µmol/m²/s for 16 hours gives 11.5 mol/m²/day, just short of Kentucky's lettuce range.

**Recommendation:** plan on a grow light for any indoor system, and choose one that publishes PPFD at a stated distance, so you can check it reaches 12–17 mol/m²/day for lettuce over a sensible day length.

## What to check before you buy

Product listings rarely say how a system fails. These checks come from the sources above:

| Check | What to look for | Why it matters | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Root-zone type | Kratky, DWC, NFT, tower, ebb and flow or drip | Decides crop fit and what happens if power fails | Oregon State EM 9453 |
| Reservoir size against plant count | For lettuce, roughly 1–1.5 gal per plant over the crop (Kratky) | Small tanks drift faster; Tennessee changes small recirculating tanks every 1–2 weeks | UF/IFAS HS1488; University of Tennessee |
| Light output | PPFD at canopy height and coverage area | Lets you calculate DLI | University of Kentucky; Purdue |
| Opaque reservoir and lid | No light reaching the solution | Light feeds algae | University of Illinois; Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Pump backup | Battery or a design that tolerates outages | NFT plants can wilt quickly | Oregon State EM 9457 |
| Drain and access | A drain valve; room to reach roots and tubing | Cleaning between crops | Oregon State EM 9455; Oklahoma State HLA-6724 |
| Height clearance | Space for mature plants plus the light | Vining crops such as tomatoes and cucumbers go in buckets and grow tall | Oregon State EM 9456 |
| Floor and weight | Level floor for large tanks | Oregon State says DWC tanks belong on the ground | Oregon State EM 9455 |

Plant count is the most misleading number on a listing. Oklahoma State's tower table spaces head lettuce at 12 inches and thin-leaf lettuce at 6–8 inches, so a system with more holes than its light and reservoir can support will not grow more food.

## Step-by-step: setting up a home system

1. **Pick the crop first.** Leafy greens and herbs for water culture; tomatoes and peppers for drip buckets.
2. **Pick the spot.** Level floor, near an outlet but away from any spill path, with room above for the light.
3. **Plan the light.** Hang or set the light so you can raise it as plants grow; aim for the DLI in the table.
4. **Fill and mix.** Use a nutrient made for hydroponics; Illinois advises against fertilizers made for soil. Aim for EC 1.2–1.8 for lettuce (UF/IFAS) or 2.0–3.0 for fruiting crops (Tennessee). The [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) helps with doses.
5. **Set the pH.** Oklahoma State advises getting EC right first, then adjusting pH to 5.5–6.2 for greens.
6. **Run it empty for a day.** Check pumps, timers and leaks before plants go in.
7. **Transplant.** Lettuce goes in 14–21 days after seeding, at 3–4 true leaves (Kentucky).
8. **Keep a routine.** Check pH and EC at the same time each day if you can (Oklahoma State); top up every few days and replace solution every 3–4 weeks (Kentucky), sooner in small tanks (Tennessee). Use the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) for crop numbers.

## Troubleshooting indoor systems

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leggy, pale or slow plants | Too little light | Add or lower a grow light; calculate DLI and aim for 12–17 for lettuce | University of Kentucky; Purdue |
| Lower tower plants smaller than upper ones | Light falls toward the base | Add side lighting or rotate crops; put light-hungry plants at the top | Touliatos et al. 2016 |
| Plants wilting after a power cut | NFT or tower roots dried out | Add a pump backup, or switch to DWC or Kratky | Oregon State EM 9457; University of Missouri |
| Brown, slimy roots | Warm, poorly aerated solution; Pythium | Keep solution 65–80 °F and the air pump running | UF/IFAS HS1422; Virginia Tech |
| Green film in the tank | Light reaching the solution | Cover clear sides; close gaps in the lid or raft | University of Illinois; Oregon State EM 9455 |
| pH or EC swings every few days | Small reservoir for the plant load | Top up more often; change small tanks every 1–2 weeks | University of Tennessee W 844-C |
| Lettuce bolting or tipburn | Room too warm | Keep days at or below 75 °F and nights at or below 65 °F | University of Kentucky |
| Salt crust on ebb-and-flow media | Salt build-up | Use shallow floods and an occasional plain-water flood | Oregon State EM 9458 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best indoor hydroponic system for beginners?

For lettuce and greens, a Kratky bucket: UF/IFAS describes it as needing no pumps or electricity. A small DWC tub is the next step. See [hydroponic systems for beginners](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/).

### Do indoor hydroponic systems need a grow light?

Almost always. Lettuce needs a DLI of 12–17 mol/m²/day (Kentucky), and a Purdue greenhouse in Indiana received under 10 from November through February.

### How many plants can an indoor system hold?

It depends on the design and crop. Kentucky gives 3–4 lettuce plants per square foot in a DWC raft; Oklahoma State gives 28 plants per tower. More holes do not help if light or reservoir volume runs short.

### Can I grow tomatoes in an indoor hydroponic system?

Yes, but choose buckets of perlite on drip, which Oregon State recommends for long-lived and vining crops. Tomatoes need a DLI above 20 (Purdue) and EC around 2.0–3.0 (Tennessee). See [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).

### Are vertical towers better than flat systems?

A peer-reviewed study found towers produce more per unit of floor area, but plants near the base got less light and grew smaller. They suit small floors if you can light the whole column.

### What pH should an indoor system run at?

For leafy greens, 5.5–6.2: Kentucky gives 5.5–6.0 and Illinois about 5.8–6.2.

### Should I build or buy?

Building costs less and teaches the system; buying saves time and adds a light and housing. The [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) has three builds, and [indoor hydroponic system kits](/best/indoor-hydroponic-system-kits/) covers what kits include.
