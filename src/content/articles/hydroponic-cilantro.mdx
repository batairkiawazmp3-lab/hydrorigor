Hydroponic cilantro grows well at a solution **pH of 5.5–6.0** (Iowa State's Christopher Currey) and an **EC of 1.2–2.0 mS/cm** (Virginia Tech), in air at **64–81 °F (18–27 °C)** (Ontario's agriculture ministry, OMAFRA). Space plants about **8 in (20 cm) apart** and give them a daily light integral (DLI) near **17–18 mol/m²/day**: in an Iowa State NFT trial, that much light more than doubled cilantro yield. Cut the crop **40–60 days from seed** (OMAFRA); the Iowa State plants were harvested at about 7 weeks.

The catch is bolting. Cilantro is a long-day plant: Michigan State researchers recommend **12 hours of light or less** to delay flowering, while most herb lighting advice runs 16 hours. This guide shows that conflict, what each choice costs, and how to set up a small system so you cut leaves before the plants flower.

## Why cilantro bolts, and what controls it

Bolting is the plant switching from leaves to a flower stalk. Leaves get smaller and more finely cut, and the harvest window closes. Three things push cilantro toward it.

**Long days.** In a Tokyo University of Agriculture trial, coriander formed flower buds **49 days** after sowing under 16-hour days and **73 days** after sowing under 8-hour days, and longer days also shortened the time from bud to open flower (Tomitaka et al. 2001, abstract). The plant still flowered under short days, only later, so the authors called it a quantitative long-day plant. Michigan State's herb trials reached the same conclusion: coriander 'Santo' showed buds sooner under days of 13 hours or more, and after 20 weeks nearly all plants had flowered whatever the photoperiod (Lopez and Garcia, 2021).

**Heat.** UC ANR's California production guide says hot weather makes cilantro bolt quickly and cuts foliage; summer crops there mature in 40–45 days. Oregon State notes that cilantro sown in late June or early July bolts in the long, hot days of summer, and Utah State advises keeping soil below 75 °F (24 °C) when growing indoors.

**Light color.** In a 2025 growth-room study at 64 °F (18 °C) and 16-hour days, coriander under blue-only LEDs flowered **36 days** after treatment started, against 62–69 days under white, red or green light; a 50:50 white and blue mix also flowered sooner than white alone (Kim et al. 2025). The plants grew at only 100–120 µmol/m²/s, and the authors note coriander "did not thrive" at 100, so treat the result as a direction rather than a target.

## Light: the conflict between yield and bolting

Light raises cilantro yield more than any other factor in the research. In the Iowa State NFT trial, raising the DLI from 7 to 18 mol/m²/day increased cilantro fresh mass by **154%**, while EC had no effect at all (Currey, Walters and Flax, 2019). That trial used a **16-hour** photoperiod, the same length Iowa State Extension's indoor herb advice tops out at (12–16 hours, lamp 6–12 in or 15–30 cm from the leaves).

Michigan State's advice points the other way: grow coriander under short days of **12 hours or less** to prevent or delay flowering. Their trial plants grew at a DLI of 10 and 68 °F (20 °C), with the day length stretched by dim LEDs, so the flowering signal came from the length of the day, not the amount of light.

Both can be right, because they suit different harvests:

| Plan | Hours | Light needed for DLI 17–18 | Best for | What it rests on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fast single cut | 16 | About 310 µmol/m²/s | Cutting the whole crop at about 7 weeks | Iowa State trial: DLI 18 at 16 h, harvest 4 weeks after transplant |
| Longer picking | 12 | About 395 µmol/m²/s | Picking leaves over several weeks | Michigan State: 12 h or less delays flowering |

*Light needed: DLI × 1,000,000 ÷ (hours × 3,600). DLI 18 at 16 h is 312; DLI 17 at 12 h is 394.*

**What we recommend:** if your light is strong enough, run it **12 hours a day at about 400 µmol/m²/s** and keep picking. If it cannot reach that (many countertop units cannot), run 16 hours, sow in small batches and cut each batch at about 7 weeks from seed, before the day-49 bud stage in the Tokyo trial turns into a flower stalk. Either way, use a white, full-spectrum LED rather than a blue-heavy one.

## Feeding: pH and EC

**EC.** Cilantro is not fussy. In the Iowa State NFT trial, cilantro 'Santo' grew the same at every EC from 0.5 to 4.0 mS/cm, at both low and high light. Currey later wrote that 4.0 is "simply a waste of fertilizer" and that 0.5 may let nutrient imbalances develop sooner. Virginia Tech's NFT range for leafy greens and herbs, 1.2–2.0, sits comfortably inside that.

**A conflict worth noting.** UC ANR describes field cilantro as salt sensitive, with yield falling 12–14% for every 1 dS/m of soil salinity above 1.0. That is a soil salinity measurement, not a nutrient solution reading, so it does not overturn the hydroponic trial, but it is a reason not to push EC to the top of the range.

**pH.** Currey keeps herb solutions at 5.5–6.0 and says growth suffers much above 7.0–7.5; the cilantro trial held 6.0. OMAFRA's pH of 6.5–7.5 is for field soil and does not apply to a nutrient solution.

| Setting | Our pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EC, seedlings | 1.2 mS/cm | Bottom of Virginia Tech's range; well above Currey's 0.5 floor |
| EC, established plants | 1.2–1.6 mS/cm | Inside every tested range; lower half because field cilantro is salt sensitive (UC ANR) |
| pH | 5.8–6.0 | Inside Currey's 5.5–6.0 and at the trial's 6.0 |
| Checks | Two to three times a week | Virginia Tech SPES-463 |

## Temperature

OMAFRA gives an optimal range of **18–27 °C (64–81 °F)** and UC ANR a field range of **50–85 °F (10–30 °C)**; both note the plant tolerates light frost. The Iowa State trial held air and solution at **22 °C (72 °F)** with **8.3 mg/L** of dissolved oxygen, and Virginia Tech gives 7–10 ppm dissolved oxygen for deep water culture.

**What we recommend:** keep the grow space at about 64–72 °F (18–22 °C). That is inside OMAFRA's range, matches the trial's 72 °F at the top, and stays well under the heat that makes cilantro bolt. Utah State's 75 °F (24 °C) is a sensible ceiling. In a warm room, a cooler corner or basement shelf is worth more than any nutrient change.

## Seed to harvest: the timeline

![Timeline of hydroponic cilantro from seed: soil germination at 55 to 68 degrees F, transplant at 3 weeks, harvest window 40 to 60 days, Iowa State harvest at 7 weeks, flower buds from day 49 under 16 hour days.](/images/guides/hydroponic-cilantro-timeline.svg)

*Seed-to-harvest milestones. Germination temperature: Oregon State. Transplant and harvest timing: Currey et al. (2019). Harvest window: OMAFRA. Bud formation: Tomitaka et al. (2001).*

- **Germination.** Oregon State says cilantro seed germinates at soil temperatures of 55–68 °F (13–20 °C). Utah State gives about 3 weeks in the garden; it is a slow starter, so do not give up on a plug tray after a week.
- **Transplant.** The Iowa State trial sowed two seeds per cell, thinned to one and moved plants into NFT troughs **3 weeks after sowing**.
- **Harvest.** The trial harvested **4 weeks after transplant**, about 7 weeks from seed. OMAFRA gives **40–60 days** for cilantro (and 100–120 days for coriander seed); Utah State harvests leaves when plants are 4–6 in (10–15 cm) tall.
- **Flowering.** Coriander typically flowers 6–8 weeks from seed (Kim et al. 2025, introduction), which is why the harvest window is short.

**Cilantro microgreens** are a different crop: Utah State's microgreen table gives 28 g of cilantro seed per 10 × 20 in tray, 7–14 days under cover and **21–28 days to harvest**, slower than most microgreens. See [hydroponic microgreens](/crops/hydroponic-microgreens/) for that method.

## Which system suits cilantro

| System | Fit | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFT channels | Very good | The Iowa State cilantro trial used NFT on 8 in (20 cm) centers; greens and herbs did best in NFT and DWC (Virginia Tech SPES-466) |
| Deep water culture | Very good | Best with NFT for greens and herbs in Virginia Tech's trials; aerate to 7–10 ppm oxygen |
| Ebb and flow | Good | Oregon State EM 9458 lists cilantro among common ebb and flow crops |
| Countertop unit | Fair | Works, but light is often too weak for DLI 17 and may run long hours; check its timer settings |
| Kratky jar | Unknown | No source we read tested cilantro this way |

**Our pick:** a small [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tub or an [NFT system](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/), the two system types with research behind them for herbs. If you grow several herbs together, our [best herbs for hydroponics](/crops/best-herbs-for-hydroponics/) guide covers a shared solution; cilantro's wide EC tolerance makes it an easy neighbor for [basil](/crops/hydroponic-basil/) and parsley, though basil prefers a warmer room.

## Step by step: set up a cilantro system

1. **Choose a slow-bolting variety.** UC ANR names 'Long Standing', 'Leisure', 'Santos' and 'Terra'; 'Santo' was used in the Iowa State and Michigan State trials.
2. **Sow in plugs.** Put two seeds in each rockwool or foam cube about ¼–½ in (0.6–1.3 cm) deep (Utah State), keep them at 55–68 °F (13–20 °C) and moist, and thin to one seedling.
3. **Mix the solution.** Fill the reservoir, bring EC to about 1.2 mS/cm and pH to 5.8–6.0.
4. **Transplant at about 3 weeks.** Set plants 8 in (20 cm) apart in NFT channels or a DWC lid. In DWC, run an air pump so the solution stays oxygenated.
5. **Set the light.** Either 12 hours at about 400 µmol/m²/s, or 16 hours at about 310 if you plan one cut; lamp 6–12 in (15–30 cm) above the leaves if the maker gives no intensity figure (Iowa State).
6. **Hold the room cool.** Aim for 64–72 °F (18–22 °C) and keep it under 75 °F (24 °C).
7. **Check pH and EC two to three times a week.** Raise EC toward 1.6 as plants grow; top up with mixed solution, not plain water.
8. **Harvest.** For a single cut, take the whole plant at the base or 1.5–2 in (4–5 cm) above the crown (UC ANR) at about 6–7 weeks. To pick over time, Iowa State's advice for annual herbs is to leave about 4–6 in (10–15 cm) of stem, so let plants grow taller before the first cut.
9. **Sow the next batch.** Oregon State and Utah State both advise small sowings every few weeks. Neither gives an interval; with a 40–60-day crop, we sow a new tray about every 3 weeks so one is always ready.
10. **Store cold.** OMAFRA gives 10–14 days at 32 °F (0 °C) and high humidity; UC ANR gives at least 14 days at 33–35 °F (0.6–1.7 °C).

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tall center stalk, feathery leaves | Bolting from long days or heat | Cut the crop now; next batch, use 12 h light and keep below 75 °F (24 °C) | Michigan State; Utah State; UC ANR |
| Plants flower very early | Blue-heavy light, long days | Switch to a white full-spectrum LED | Kim et al. 2025 |
| Few seedlings after 10 days | Slow germination or warm media | Wait up to 3 weeks; keep media at 55–68 °F (13–20 °C) | Utah State; Oregon State |
| Thin, pale, slow plants | Too little light | Raise intensity toward DLI 17–18 | Currey et al. 2019; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Slow growth with good light | pH drifted high | Bring pH back to 5.5–6.0 | Currey 2024 |
| Slow growth at high EC | EC high for a salt-sensitive crop | Dilute to EC 1.2–1.6 | UC ANR; Virginia Tech SPES-463 |
| Brown, slimy roots | Root rot (Pythium) | Aerate (the trial held 8.3 mg/L oxygen), keep pH at 5.5–6.0, clean between crops | Currey et al. 2019; Currey 2024 |

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cilantro easy to grow hydroponically?

Its feeding is easy: Iowa State found the same yield at any EC from 0.5 to 4.0. Its timing is the hard part, because long days and heat make it bolt within weeks. Iowa State Extension still lists cilantro among the best herbs for growing indoors.

### How do I stop hydroponic cilantro from bolting?

Keep days at 12 hours or less (Michigan State), keep the room below about 75 °F (24 °C) (Utah State), use white rather than blue-heavy light (Kim et al. 2025) and choose a slow-bolting variety such as 'Long Standing' or 'Santos' (UC ANR). You can delay it, not prevent it: nearly all Michigan State plants flowered by 20 weeks.

### What pH and EC does hydroponic cilantro need?

pH 5.5–6.0 (Currey) and EC 1.2–2.0 mS/cm (Virginia Tech). We run cilantro at 1.2–1.6 because field cilantro is salt sensitive (UC ANR).

### How long does hydroponic cilantro take to grow?

About 40–60 days from seed to harvest (OMAFRA). In the Iowa State NFT trial, plants were transplanted at 3 weeks and harvested 4 weeks later.

### Does hydroponic cilantro regrow after cutting?

None of the sources we read measured regrowth. Iowa State advises leaving 4–6 in (10–15 cm) of stem on annual herbs for later growth and replacing old plants from seed after several harvests, so plan on new sowings rather than one long-lived plant.
