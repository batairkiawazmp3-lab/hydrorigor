Hydroponic spinach grows best at **pH about 6.0** and **EC 1.8–2.3 mS/cm** (Oklahoma State University Extension), in air at **68–77 °F (20–25 °C)** (Cornell University). Its weak point is the root zone: Cornell found Pythium root rot was the main threat and kept the nutrient solution cool, at **64–68 °F (18–20 °C)**, with dissolved oxygen **above 7 mg/L**. Give it a daily light integral (DLI) of about **17 mol/m²/day** (Cornell; Virginia Tech). Baby leaf sown densely is first cut **12–16 days after seeding** (Cornell); transplanted plants spaced **6 in (15 cm)** apart are ready **3–4 weeks after transplant**, about 5–7 weeks from seed (University of Georgia).

The second problem is bolting. Long days and heat make spinach send up a flower stalk (Clemson), so pick a slow-bolting variety and keep the room cool. Below are the numbers, where the sources disagree, and a setup that works at home.

## Why spinach is harder than lettuce

Spinach looks like an easy leafy green, and its numbers are close to lettuce's. Two things make it harder in water.

**Root rot.** Cornell's spinach program, run for the New York State Energy Research and Development Authority, identified *Pythium aphanidermatum* as "the main pathogen attacking spinach roots" in its hydroponic ponds. In one early run, every transplant collapsed within five days. Virginia Tech met the same disease when it grew spinach in NFT and deep water culture (SPES-466), and a 2021 HortScience paper notes that many hydroponic growers avoid spinach for this reason.

**Bolting.** Spinach flowers in response to long days and heat. Clemson's home garden factsheet says "high temperatures and long days" cause it to bolt, which is why field growers sow it in spring and fall. Indoors you control both, so you can avoid it.

Spinach does have one advantage: Cornell notes that tipburn, the leaf-edge burn that troubles indoor lettuce, "is not a major issue for spinach."

## pH and EC: where the sources differ

Oklahoma State's crop table, the most widely used extension reference, lists spinach at **EC 1.8–2.3 mS/cm and pH 6.0–7.0**. Cornell's research ponds ran lower: pH 5.8 and an EC of about 1,200–1,300 µS/cm (1.2–1.3 mS/cm), a half-strength mix that still produced commercial yields. That is not a contradiction. Oklahoma State gives a target range, and Cornell shows spinach still grows well a little below it.

**Do not run spinach acidic.** Some herb researchers now suggest pH 4.0–4.5 to suppress Pythium, and our [herbs guide](/crops/best-herbs-for-hydroponics/) covers that work. Spinach does not tolerate it. In the HortScience growth-chamber study, spinach at pH 4.0 made only 0.88 g of fresh shoot per plant and at pH 4.5 only 3.8 g, compared with 7.3 g at pH 5.5, after about three weeks. Its title gives the one bright spot: higher nutrient strength improved growth in the acidic solution. It still did not make low pH a good idea.

![Spinach fresh shoot mass per plant by solution pH: 0.88 g at pH 4.0, 3.8 g at pH 4.5 and 7.3 g at pH 5.5, with a recommended band of pH 5.8 to 6.5.](/images/guides/hydroponic-spinach-ph-yield.svg)

*Fresh shoot mass per plant after about three weeks at four solution pH levels (HortScience 56(6):687). Our recommended band combines Cornell's pH 5.8 and the lower half of Oklahoma State's 6.0–7.0.*

**What we recommend:** hold **pH 5.8–6.5**, aiming at 6.0, and **EC 1.5–2.0 mS/cm** for young plants, rising to Oklahoma State's 1.8–2.3 as they size up. The pH band starts at Cornell's 5.8 and covers the lower half of Oklahoma State's range, so it also suits lettuce and herbs in a shared tank. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains how to mix to an EC, the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a label rate to your reservoir, and the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists spinach beside other crops.

## Temperature: warm leaves, cool roots

Cornell recommends air and root-zone temperatures between **20 and 25 °C (68–77 °F)**, depending on the variety. In its temperature trials, fresh weight was highest at 25 °C (77 °F) and lowest at 35 °C (95 °F), where plants were badly stunted, and 20 °C (68 °F) gave the best-shaped leaves. The University of Georgia gives a similar growth range of **60–77 °F (15–25 °C)** and **65–70 °F (18–21 °C)** for germination. Clemson puts the best soil temperature for germination at 70 °F and says germination is inhibited above 85 °F.

The source conflict is in the water. Spinach roots grow fastest when warm: Cornell found roughly **24 °C (75 °F)** was the best root temperature for baby spinach, and one cultivar's shoot weight rose 32% in a 25 °C pond compared with a 20 °C one. But warm water also favors Pythium. When Cornell infected roots on purpose, by day 28 none were still white at 24 °C (75 °F), while half were at 18 °C (64 °F). For its large-leaf system, Cornell chose to cool the solution to **18–20 °C (64–68 °F)** and accept slower growth.

![Temperature targets for hydroponic spinach: germination 65 to 70 °F, air 68 to 77 °F, nutrient solution 64 to 68 °F to slow Pythium root rot, and stunting at 95 °F air.](/images/guides/hydroponic-spinach-temperature-targets.svg)

*Germination from UGA C1304; air and solution from the Cornell CEA spinach report, where 35 °C (95 °F) air stunted plants and 18 °C (64 °F) water greatly slowed Pythium.*

**What we recommend:** keep the room at **65–72 °F (18–22 °C)** and the solution at **64–68 °F (18–20 °C)**. At home that usually means a basement or a cool room in winter, an insulated reservoir and no lights or pumps heating the water. The Ohio State University gives a similar 68–72 °F (20–22 °C) for leafy greens solutions and notes that warmer water holds less oxygen. If your water runs warmer than about 72 °F in summer, grow lettuce instead, or pick heat-tolerant spinach varieties (see below) and expect more risk.

## Oxygen and root health

Cornell held dissolved oxygen **above 7.0 mg/L** at all times, and Virginia Tech recommends 7–10 ppm for DWC with 4 ppm as the minimum. Virginia Tech also notes that raising dissolved oxygen helps control the Pythium it met in spinach. Cornell's other main defense was cleaning: it called for "a very stringent protocol of cleaning between crops" and fresh solution each cycle. In its infection trials, low spore levels did little harm, while high levels cut shoot weight by more than a quarter, so the goal is to keep the disease from building up, not to sterilize everything.

For a home system, that means a running air pump with a stone sized for the tank (our [air pump guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/) gives sizing), a lid that keeps light out, a fresh batch of solution for each crop and a cleaned tank between crops.

## Light and day length

Cornell's recommended system used a DLI of **17 mol/m²/day**, depending on variety and leaf size, and Virginia Tech gives at least 17 for greens and herbs. At 16 hours a day, 17 mol/m²/day takes about 300 µmol/m²/s at leaf height (300 × 3,600 × 16 ÷ 1,000,000 = 17.3).

Long days raise yield but invite bolting, and the varieties differ a great deal:

- In Cornell's trials, 24-hour light gave crops **18–25% heavier** than 16-hour light.
- The fast Japanese variety 'Alrite' bolted under 24-hour light by day 17, and Cornell called that combination "not a useful option".
- Cornell notes Japanese varieties are "very bolting-prone in photoperiods longer than 13 hours," while northern varieties such as 'Eagle' resisted bolting even beyond 16 hours.

**What we recommend:** run lights **14–16 hours a day** near 300 µmol/m²/s and grow a slow-bolting variety. Do not use 24-hour light at home: the gain is modest and the bolting risk is real. If your light's maker gives PPFD at a set distance, set the height to reach about 300; our [LED and DLI notes in the basil guide](/crops/hydroponic-basil/) show how to work it out.

## Varieties: bolting and germination

| Variety | What the source found | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eagle | Bolt-resistant northern type; chosen for long photoperiods | Cornell |
| Alrite | Fast-growing but bolt-prone under long days | Cornell |
| Bloomsdale | Semi-savoy, slow bolting | Clemson HGIC 1320 |
| Whale | Savoy, downy mildew resistant, slow bolting | Clemson HGIC 1320 |
| Sunangel | Higher yield; 57% germination in a summer test | UGA C1304 |
| Kona | Higher yield; 7% germination in the same test | UGA C1304 |
| SV3580VC | Higher yield and sweetness | UGA C1304 |
| Banjo | Best germination in the test, 66% | UGA C1304 |

*Yields here are UGA's descriptions of its Georgia summer DWC trial; it did not publish yields in the circular.*

**Germination is the hidden problem.** In UGA's summer test of 22 commercial varieties, only 'Banjo' and 'Sunangel' germinated above 50%, and several came in under 10%. Warm conditions were part of the reason. UGA suggests priming seed before sowing, for example 18 hours in a 0.3% hydrogen peroxide solution. Cornell sowed de-hulled seed and kept the trays dark and humid at 25 °C (77 °F) for 48 hours before moving them to the pond. Whole seed needed about 12 hours more.

**What we recommend:** sow two or three seeds per plug, keep them at 65–70 °F, and expect gaps. A cooler germination spot does more than any single variety choice.

## Which system to use

| System | Fit for spinach | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFT | Good for baby and full leaf | Highest spinach yield in Virginia Tech's trial (11.55 oz); Oregon State lists spinach |
| Deep water culture | Good, and easiest to keep cool | 8.74 oz in the same trial; UGA's and Cornell's spinach both grew on floating rafts |
| Vertical media stack | Poor | Lowest yield in the trial, 3.37 oz |
| Kratky | Untested | None of our sources tested spinach this way |

*Virginia Tech SPES-466 figures are the runs with supplemental light, and the authors note the trials ran during COVID-19 restrictions. Read them as a ranking, not a home forecast.*

**Our pick:** a [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) tub. It yielded a little less than NFT in Virginia Tech's trial, but its large water volume buffers temperature (Oregon State), and cool, well-aerated water is what keeps spinach alive. An [NFT system](/systems/nft-hydroponic-system/) is the higher-yielding choice if your room stays cool and power is reliable; Oregon State warns plants in NFT can wilt quickly if the pump stops.

## From seed to harvest

![Two spinach timelines. Baby leaf: sow in place at high density, germinate 2 days in the dark, first cut at 12 to 16 days, regrow and cut about weekly. Full leaf: seedlings 2 to 3 weeks, transplant 6 inches apart, harvest 3 to 4 weeks after transplant.](/images/guides/hydroponic-spinach-timeline.svg)

*Baby leaf from the Cornell CEA spinach report, grown at a DLI of about 17–18 mol/m²/day; full leaf from UGA C1304's DWC trial.*

Cornell's baby-leaf system sows seed at its final density with no transplant and cuts **12–16 days from seeding**, depending on leaf size, variety and density, with repeat cuts at about one-week intervals. Under about 18 mol/m²/day, it says to cut no later than 14 days. UGA's full-leaf system grows seedlings for 2–3 weeks, transplants them into a raft with holes 6 in (15 cm) apart, and harvests **3–4 weeks later**. For comparison, Clemson gives 37–45 days from planting for spinach grown in soil.

## Step by step: a home spinach DWC tub

1. **Choose a cool spot.** Aim for a room at 65–72 °F (18–22 °C). Spinach is a cool-season crop, and winter is the easiest time to grow it.
2. **Pick a slow-bolting variety.** Eagle, Bloomsdale and Whale are described as bolt-resistant or slow-bolting (Cornell; Clemson).
3. **Sow in plugs.** Put two or three seeds per rockwool or coir plug and keep them dark, moist and at 65–70 °F until they sprout. Our [growing media guide](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/) compares plugs.
4. **Set up the tub.** Use an opaque container with an air pump and stone running all the time, and a floating raft or lid with 1¾–2 in [net pots](/equipment/hydroponic-net-pots/) 6 in (15 cm) apart.
5. **Mix the solution.** Use a complete hydroponic fertilizer at EC 1.5–2.0 and adjust pH to 6.0. Check the water temperature: 64–68 °F is the goal.
6. **Transplant at 2–3 weeks.** Thin each plug to the strongest seedling and set it in a net pot.
7. **Light 14–16 hours.** Aim for about 300 µmol/m²/s at the leaves.
8. **Check every few days.** Keep pH at 5.8–6.5, let EC rise toward 1.8–2.3, and look at the roots: they should be white.
9. **Harvest at 3–4 weeks after transplant.** Pick outer leaves at 3–6 in (8–15 cm) long, as Clemson advises, and let the center keep growing, or cut the whole plant.
10. **Clean between crops.** Empty and scrub the tub, rinse well and start with fresh solution (Cornell).

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brown, slimy roots; plants wilt and collapse | Pythium root rot | Cool solution to 64–68 °F, keep DO above 7 mg/L, discard affected plants, clean the tank | Cornell; Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Center stretches and forms a flower stalk | Bolting from long days or heat | Cut light to 14–16 h, cool the room, grow a slow-bolting variety | Clemson; Cornell |
| Few seeds sprout | Warm germination or low-vigor seed | Germinate at 65–70 °F; prime seed; sow extra | UGA C1304; Clemson |
| Small, stunted plants in warm room | Air near 95 °F (35 °C) | Keep air at 68–77 °F | Cornell |
| Slow growth, pale leaves, pH low | Solution too acidic | Raise pH to about 6.0; spinach lost about half its growth at pH 4.5 | HortScience 56(6):687 |
| Slow growth at good pH | EC low or too little light | Raise EC toward 1.8–2.3; aim for DLI 17 | Oklahoma State; Cornell |
| Green slime in the tank | Light reaching the water | Cover all openings with opaque material | Oregon State EM 9455 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What pH does hydroponic spinach need?

About 6.0. Oklahoma State lists 6.0–7.0, and Cornell's research ponds ran at 5.8. Do not go much lower: in a HortScience study, spinach at pH 4.5 made about half the shoot mass it made at 5.5.

### What EC or ppm is best for hydroponic spinach?

Oklahoma State gives EC 1.8–2.3 mS/cm. Cornell grew good crops at about 1.2–1.3, so start young plants near 1.5 and raise it as they grow. Convert to ppm with your meter's scale; our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) explains the 500 and 700 scales.

### Why does my hydroponic spinach keep dying?

The most likely cause is Pythium root rot, the main spinach disease in Cornell's and Virginia Tech's hydroponic trials. Warm water favors it. Keep the solution at 64–68 °F (18–20 °C), aerate it to above 7 mg/L dissolved oxygen, and clean the system between crops.

### How do I stop hydroponic spinach from bolting?

Grow a slow-bolting variety, keep lights to 14–16 hours and keep the room below about 77 °F. Cornell found Japanese varieties bolt in days longer than 13 hours, while northern types such as Eagle held out past 16.

### How long does hydroponic spinach take to grow?

Baby leaf can be cut 12–16 days from seeding in a dense, well-lit system (Cornell). Full-size plants take a 2–3 week seedling stage and 3–4 weeks after transplant (University of Georgia), so about 5–7 weeks in all.

### Is spinach easier than lettuce in hydroponics?

No. The targets are similar, but spinach is more prone to root rot and bolting. If you are new to hydroponics, start with [hydroponic lettuce](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) and add spinach in a cool season.
