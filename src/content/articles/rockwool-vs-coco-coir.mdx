**For most home growers, use rockwool to start seeds and coco coir to fill buckets, bags and slabs.** Rockwool cubes are sterile, uniform and the standard plug for NFT, deep water culture and Kratky (Virginia Tech). Coco coir is renewable, holds a lot of water and, in the one tomato trial that grew both side by side, gave **25.8% more fruit**: **84.9 t/ha** in coir against **67.5 t/ha** in rockwool (Xiong et al. 2017). A cucumber trial also found higher yield in coir (He et al. 2022), while a University of Arizona strawberry trial found the opposite for **100% coir**, so the result is not universal.

Each needs one step before planting. **Rockwool** starts at **pH 7–8** (Oklahoma State) or **7–8.5** (UF/IFAS), so soak it in solution adjusted to **pH 5.5–6.0**. **Coco coir** runs **pH 5.5–6.8** (UF/IFAS) but carries sodium and other salts, so rinse it or buy it buffered. Keep loose coir out of recirculating systems, where UF/IFAS says fine particles do not belong.

HydroRigor has not grown crops in either medium for this guide; every figure below comes from the university, extension and peer-reviewed sources listed at the end.

## What each medium is

**Rockwool** (also sold as stone wool or mineral wool) is spun from molten rock (Kentucky CCD-TA-02). Oklahoma State lists it for seed starting, cuttings and large crops such as tomatoes. Kentucky sums up its strengths as high water holding and aeration, sterile and uniform, and its drawbacks as not biodegradable, high cost, the need for precise feeding, and skin irritation when you handle it.

**Coco coir** is the fiber and pith from coconut husks. It is sold as compressed bricks that **expand up to six times** when soaked (Oklahoma State), as loose bags, as grow bags and as slabs. Kentucky lists it as renewable, with good water retention, aeration and nutrient holding (CEC), but notes it may need buffering to remove excess salts and can break down and shrink over time.

In both, the nutrient solution feeds the plant. The medium holds the plant up, stores water between feeds and lets air reach the roots. For how they compare with clay pebbles, perlite and the rest, see our [growing media guide](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/).

| Potassium in medium | 178 mg/kg | 1,560 mg/kg | Xiong et al. 2017, Table 1 |

Three rows need a comment.

**pH.** Extension sources put new rockwool at **7–8.5**, yet the rockwool in the tomato trial measured **6.5**. The trial used a commercial greenhouse slab, and the paper does not say whether it was pre-conditioned. Assume new rockwool is alkaline until your meter says otherwise, and soak it first. Coir's ranges agree (UF/IFAS **5.5–6.8**; trial **6.1**), which is already close to Kentucky's **5.5–6.5** target for the solution.

**Air.** Oklahoma State gives rockwool **18–25% air**; the trial measured **5.1%** air in rockwool and **5.6%** in coir; Illinois gives rockwool **95% pore space and 80% water-holding capacity**. The numbers differ because labs measure air at different water contents and products differ. What every source agrees on is the direction: both media hold far more water than air when wet, so neither should sit in a puddle.

**Potassium.** The coir in the trial held almost nine times the potassium of the rockwool (**1,560 vs 178 mg/kg**), while rockwool held more calcium (**279 vs 58 mg/kg**). That is why coir is often buffered with calcium before use: unbuffered coir can trade potassium and sodium for the calcium and magnesium in your solution. Oklahoma State says to wash coir and add calcium and magnesium.

## Yield in trials: what grew better

Three controlled trials compared the two directly:

| Trial | Crop and setup | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Xiong et al. 2017 | Tomato 'Lucius', slabs, Beijing greenhouse, Oct–May | Coir 84.9 t/ha, peat mix 76.9, rockwool 67.5 | Frontiers in Plant Science 8:1327 |
| He et al. 2022 | Greenhouse cucumber, rockwool vs coir | Coir gave significantly higher yield and leaf area | Heliyon 8(10):e10930 |
| University of Arizona, 2008 | Strawberry, rockwool, 100% coir, coir–perlite mixes | 100% coir restricted growth and yield; rockwool and the highest-perlite mix did best | Arizona CEAC |

![Bar chart of tomato yield by substrate in one trial: rockwool 67.5 tonnes per hectare, peat and vermiculite 76.9, coco coir 84.9. Coir yielded 25.8 percent more than rockwool.](/images/guides/rockwool-vs-coco-coir-tomato-yield.svg)

*Total tomato yield in Xiong et al. 2017, Table 3. All three differed significantly (LSD, P < 0.05). One season, one cultivar, three replicates.*

**The tomato trial in detail.** Tomatoes grew in **100 × 20 × 7.5 cm (about 39 × 8 × 3 in)** slabs at **2.4 plants per m²**, fed by drip at an EC of **2.3 dS/m**, from October to May at **14–23 °C (57–73 °F)**. Coir gave the highest yield and heavier fruit (**74.0 g** against **66.8 g** in rockwool). By our arithmetic from the paper's planting density, that is about **3.5 kg (7.8 lb) of fruit per plant in coir and 2.8 kg (6.2 lb) in rockwool**. Plants in coir took up more potassium and sulfur and photosynthesized faster, and less fertilizer went unaccounted for (lost to drainage or otherwise not recovered): for nitrogen, **29.6 g/m²** in coir against **42.5 g/m²** in rockwool. Fruit quality, the authors say, was generally not obviously different, and blossom-end rot was high in both (**21.2%** of fruit in rockwool, **19.5%** in coir), so coir did not fix that problem.

**Why the strawberry result went the other way.** Arizona's page says strawberries reportedly need a lot of oxygen at the roots, so it recommends high-porosity media. In its 2008 trial, growth and yield were restricted in **100% coir** and greater in rockwool and in the mix with the most perlite; the page gives no yield figures. Arizona now grows strawberries in **50% perlite, 25% coir and 25% peat**. It also warns that coir varies by supplier and suggests a small trial before switching a whole system. For strawberries, see our [hydroponic strawberries guide](/crops/hydroponic-strawberries/).

The pattern across the three: coir did as well as or better than rockwool for fruiting vegetables in slabs and bags, and pure coir fell behind for an oxygen-hungry crop. None of these trials tested lettuce or herbs in net pots.

## Watering: the two behave differently

Coir holds more water than most media. UF/IFAS HS1274 says fine coconut fiber holds **at least twice the water** of perlite or pine bark, and a **3-gallon (about 11 L) bag** of fine coir holds **about 1 gallon (3.8 L)**. Fine coir may need **6–8 waterings a day** on a fruiting crop, against **a dozen or more** shorter ones in perlite. Aim for runoff of **10–20%** of what you apply, and keep runoff EC within **1.0** of the feed (HS1274).

Rockwool slabs are usually watered by measuring how wet the slab is. In a Canadian tomato trial, watering once slab water content fell to **70%** or after the plant and slab lost **500 g (about 1.1 lb)** of weight worked best, with mature plants using **1.22–1.51 L (about 41–51 fl oz)** a day (Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada, HortScience 2008). Kentucky notes rockwool needs **precise fertigation**: it holds little in reserve chemically (no CEC), so mistakes in the solution show up fast.

## How to prepare each one, step by step

These steps follow the sources above; they are a starting point, not a tested HydroRigor method.

**Rockwool cubes and slabs**

1. **Wear gloves** when cutting or handling it; Kentucky lists skin irritation.
2. **Mix a soak solution** of water or weak nutrient solution and adjust it to **pH 5.5–6.0** (Oklahoma State). A [pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/) makes this quick.
3. **Soak the cubes** until they are wet through, then let them drain. UF/IFAS says soaking to lower pH may be required.
4. **Check the runoff pH** after the first feeds and adjust toward **5.5–6.5** (Kentucky). The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) has crop ranges.
5. **Plan for disposal.** UF/IFAS says rockwool cannot be reused; Oklahoma State says slabs can be steam sterilized and last **three to four years**. Without a steamer, treat cubes as single-use.

**Coco coir bricks and bags**

1. **Soak the brick** in plenty of water; it expands up to **six times** (Oklahoma State).
2. **Rinse until the runoff is low in salts**, or buy coir sold as washed and buffered. Oklahoma State and Kentucky both flag sodium and salts.
3. **Add calcium and magnesium** if the coir is not buffered (Oklahoma State). Mix your feed with the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/).
4. **Test a little first.** Arizona warns coir varies by source; try one bucket before converting a system.
5. **Mix in perlite** for oxygen-hungry crops such as strawberries (Arizona CEAC).
6. **Keep it out of recirculating systems**, where loose particles reach the pump (UF/IFAS EP623).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is coco coir better than rockwool?

For fruiting vegetables in slabs, bags and buckets, the trials favor coir: **84.9 vs 67.5 t/ha** for tomatoes (Xiong et al. 2017) and higher cucumber yield (He et al. 2022). For starting seeds for NFT, DWC or Kratky, rockwool cubes remain the standard plug (Virginia Tech). Pure coir did worse for strawberries (Arizona).

### Do I have to soak rockwool?

Yes. New rockwool is alkaline, **pH 7–8** (Oklahoma State) or **7–8.5** (UF/IFAS). Soak it in solution at **pH 5.5–6.0** before sowing.

### Do I have to rinse coco coir?

Unless it is sold as washed and buffered, yes. Oklahoma State says coir is rich in sodium and chlorine and should be washed, with calcium and magnesium added; Kentucky says it may need buffering.

### Can I reuse rockwool or coir?

UF/IFAS says neither is reusable. Oklahoma State says rockwool slabs can be steam sterilized between crops and last three to four years, which is practical for greenhouses, not most homes.

### Can I mix coir and perlite?

Yes, and for strawberries it helped: Arizona grows them in 50% perlite, 25% coir and 25% peat. Our [growing media guide](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/) covers perlite and other mixes.

### Which is better for tomatoes?

In the one direct trial, coir: about 25.8% more fruit and heavier fruit. Feeding and pruning matter as much as the medium; see our [hydroponic tomatoes guide](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).
