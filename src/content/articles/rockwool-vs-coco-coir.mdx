**Use rockwool to start seeds and coco coir to grow fruiting crops in bags or slabs.** Rockwool cubes are the standard plug for deep water culture, Kratky and NFT (Virginia Tech lists rockwool, foam or peat/coir cubes), and they are sterile and uniform. For tomatoes and cucumbers in drip slabs, coir did better in the two peer-reviewed trials we found: tomatoes yielded **84.9 t/ha in coir vs 67.5 in rockwool**, about **26% more** (Xiong et al. 2017), and cucumbers **7.7% more per plant** (He et al. 2022), on the same nutrient solution. The exception is strawberries: in a University of Arizona trial, **100% coir restricted growth**, while rockwool and high-perlite mixes did better, so mix coir with perlite for crops that need air at the roots.

Both need preparing. **Rockwool** starts alkaline, at **pH 7–8** (Oklahoma State) or **7–8.5** (UF/IFAS), so soak it in water adjusted to **pH 5.5–6.0** first. **Coco coir** sits at **pH 5.5–6.8** (UF/IFAS) but carries sodium and other salts, so rinse it or buy it buffered. Neither is meant to be reused at home.

HydroRigor has not grown in either medium for this guide; every figure below comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## Rockwool vs coco coir side by side

Rockwool is spun from molten rock; coir is the fiber and pith of coconut husks. Both hold a lot of water and both are close to inert, so the nutrient solution, not the medium, feeds the plant. Where they differ is in starting pH, salts, how they break down and how they behave in a slab.

### Where the sources disagree

**Air space.** Oklahoma State gives rockwool **18–25% air**; the rockwool slabs measured in the Xiong tomato trial held **5.1%**, and coir **5.6%**. Illinois puts rockwool at **95% pore space and 80% water-holding capacity**. The figures come from different products and methods, so treat them as a range: both media hold far more water than air, and coir and rockwool are close to each other.

**Starting pH.** The extension sheets give rockwool **7–8.5**. The commercial slabs in the Xiong trial measured **pH 6.5** (rockwool) and **6.1** (coir), and their EC was low (**0.06** and **0.10 dS/m**). The trial used commercial growing slabs, while the extension figures describe the material in general. Measure the pH of the water after a test soak instead of assuming either figure.

**Cost.** Kentucky lists rockwool's **high cost** as a drawback; Oklahoma State rates mineral wool **medium** and coconut fiber **low to medium** in cost. Neither gives a price, so we don't either.

### Tomato: coir used nutrients better

The Xiong trial ran a full tomato season (October to May) in a climate-controlled greenhouse at **14–23 °C (57–73 °F)**, with plants **30 cm (12 in)** apart in slabs **100 × 20 × 7.5 cm (about 39 × 8 × 3 in)**, one dripper per plant at **1.5 L/h (about 0.4 gal/h)** and **20–50%** of each watering draining back. Coir gave the highest total yield, heavier fruit, higher photosynthesis and more biomass. Peat-vermiculite came second at **76.9 t/ha**.

The nutrient balance explains part of it. Over the season, more of what went into the rockwool slabs was lost or unaccounted for: **42.5 g/m² of nitrogen** in rockwool vs **29.6** in coir, and **49.6 g/m² of potassium** vs **26.5**. Coir held some nutrients in the slab (it has some CEC; rockwool has none) and the plants took up more potassium, phosphorus and sulfur.

Blossom-end rot hit both: **21.2%** of fruit in rockwool and **19.5%** in coir, a difference that was not significant. The authors conclude coir "could be widely used in tomato production" but that blossom-end rot remains a challenge in it. Fruit quality differed little between substrates.

### Cucumber: a smaller win for coir

In the Shanghai cucumber trial, coir slabs (half fine 0–6 mm, half chunky 10–20 mm fiber) gave **7.7% more yield per plant** and a larger leaf area than rockwool, with no difference in leaf gas exchange. Leaves from coir held more calcium (**54.67 vs 40.40 g/kg**) and magnesium (**9.43 vs 6.80 g/kg**). The authors see coir as a replacement for rockwool that makes soilless growing more sustainable.

### Strawberry: too wet in pure coir

The University of Arizona compared granulated rockwool, **100% coir**, **50% coir + 50% perlite** and **70% coir + 30% perlite**. Growth and yield were restricted in pure coir and greater in rockwool and the mix with the most perlite; the researchers link this to oxygen, because strawberry roots need a lot of it. They now grow in **50% perlite, 25% coir and 25% peat**, and advise a small test of any new coir source because bio-based substrates vary. Wortman et al. (2016) also found the highest tower-strawberry yields in perlite mixed with coir or vermiculite. See our [NFT strawberry guide](/crops/nft-hydroponic-strawberries/) for the rest of the setup.

**What none of the trials tested:** lettuce, herbs, plugs in net pots, or home-sized containers. For leafy greens in DWC or Kratky, the medium is only the starter plug, and no source we found compared rockwool and coir plugs for yield.

## pH and salts: preparing each medium

### Rockwool

1. **Wear gloves** and cut cubes with a knife; Kentucky lists skin irritation from the fibers.
2. **Soak** cubes or slabs in water or dilute solution adjusted to **pH 5.5–6.0** (Oklahoma State). UF/IFAS says soaking to lower pH "may be required".
3. **Check** the pH of the drained water before sowing or transplanting.

### Coco coir

1. **Expand bricks** in water; Oklahoma State says they swell **up to six times** their size.
2. **Rinse** it well, or buy coir sold as washed or buffered, and check the EC of the runoff. Oklahoma State says coir is rich in **sodium and chlorine**; Kentucky says it may need buffering to remove salts.
3. **Feed calcium and magnesium.** Oklahoma State advises adding calcium and magnesium to coir. In the Xiong trial, new coir held **1,560 mg/kg of potassium** but only **58 of calcium** and **55 of magnesium**, against **178, 279 and 216** in rockwool, so a calcium-rich solution matters more in coir.
4. **Test a small batch** of any new coir source before filling every bag (University of Arizona).

Either way, aim the nutrient solution at **pH 5.5–6.5** (Kentucky) and check crop ranges in the [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/).

## Watering: coir needs smaller, more frequent drinks

UF/IFAS (HS1274) gives the clearest home-scale numbers. Fine coconut fiber holds **at least twice the water** of standard perlite or pine bark, and a **3-gallon (about 11 L) bag** of fine coir holds about **1 gallon (about 3.8 L)**. Fine coir may need **6–8 waterings a day** for mature fruiting crops; perlite needs a dozen or more shorter ones. A full-grown tomato or cucumber uses **2–2.5 quarts (about 1.9–2.4 L) a day** at peak harvest.

| Check | Target | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leachate (drain) per day | 10–20% of the water applied | UF/IFAS HS1274 |
| Leachate EC | Within 1.0 of the solution EC | UF/IFAS HS1274 |
| Final solution EC (fruiting crops) | Usually 1.5–3.5 | UF/IFAS HS1274 |
| Waterings, fine coir | 6–8 a day at peak | UF/IFAS HS1274 |
| Strawberry root-zone EC | Not above 1.0 dS/m | University of Arizona |
| Strawberry substrate per plant | 2 L (about ½ gal) or more | University of Arizona |

Rockwool slabs are watered the same way: small, frequent drip events with some drainage. Kentucky's warning that rockwool "needs precise fertigation" reflects that it has no nutrient-holding capacity, so whatever is in the solution reaches the roots unbuffered. If leachate EC climbs more than 1.0 above the solution, water a little longer or dilute the feed (UF/IFAS). Our [drip system guide](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/) covers emitters and timers, and the [Dutch bucket guide](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/) covers buckets, where coir leached **24.0% less** than perlite in one cucumber trial (Yang et al. 2022).

In a DWC bucket or Kratky tote the plug sits in a [net pot](/equipment/hydroponic-net-pots/) above the solution, so either a rockwool cube or a coir plug works; see our [deep water culture guide](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/). ## Reuse and disposal

UF/IFAS lists both rockwool and coir as **not reusable**. Oklahoma State disagrees for rockwool: slabs can be **steam sterilized** between crops and don't break down for **three to four years**. For a home grower without a steamer, treat rockwool cubes as single-use. Rockwool is not biodegradable (Kentucky; UF/IFAS); coir is renewable but breaks down and shrinks over time (Kentucky), so a used bag slumps and holds water differently.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is coco coir better than rockwool?

For tomatoes and cucumbers in slabs, the trials say yes: about 26% more tomato yield (Xiong et al. 2017) and 7.7% more cucumber yield (He et al. 2022). For strawberries, 100% coir did worse than rockwool (University of Arizona). For starting seeds, rockwool cubes remain the standard plug (Virginia Tech).

### Do I need to soak rockwool?

Yes. Oklahoma State gives a starting pH of 7.0–8.0 and says to bring it to 5.5–6.0 with a low-pH soak; UF/IFAS gives 7–8.5.

### Does coco coir need buffering?

Often. Oklahoma State says coir is rich in sodium and chlorine and needs washing plus added calcium and magnesium; Kentucky says it may need buffering to remove excess salts. Coir sold as buffered has been treated already; rinse it anyway and check the runoff EC.

### Can I use coir in DWC or NFT?

As a plug, yes (Virginia Tech lists peat/coir cubes). As loose fill, no: UF/IFAS says coir should stay out of recirculating systems.

### Can I reuse rockwool or coir?

UF/IFAS says neither is reusable. Oklahoma State says rockwool slabs can be steam sterilized and last three to four years, which few home growers can do.

### Which holds more water?

Both hold a lot. In the Xiong trial, water filled 84.1% of the rockwool slab and 80.0% of the coir slab. Fine coir holds at least twice the water of perlite (UF/IFAS).

### Should I mix coir with perlite?

For strawberries and other crops that need air at the roots, yes: Arizona and Wortman et al. found better yields in perlite mixes than in pure coir. Arizona's current mix is 50% perlite, 25% coir and 25% peat.
