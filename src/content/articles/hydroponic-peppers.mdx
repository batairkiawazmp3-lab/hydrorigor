Hydroponic peppers need a solution at **pH 5.5–6.0** (Oklahoma State and the University of Tennessee agree), an EC that starts low, around **0.8–1.8 mS/cm**, and climbs toward **2.0–3.0** once plants carry fruit, and air at **65–80 °F (18–27 °C)** while flowers set. Give each plant **at least 4 gallons (15 L)** of root room (South Dakota State) and expect the first green bell peppers about **70–85 days after transplant**. Chile types take longer: about 100 days to mature green and 140 days to red (Oklahoma State).

The sources disagree on feeding strength; this guide shows where, and says when the research has no number.

## Which system to use

Peppers are a long-lived, fruiting crop with big roots, so the systems that hold more solution or medium per plant do best.

| System | How peppers fare | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bags or buckets of media (perlite, coir, pine bark) with drip | Best pepper yields in Virginia Tech's side-by-side trial; both Florida trials used media | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Jovicich et al. 2004; UF/IFAS HS1113 |
| Dutch buckets | Oregon State lists bell and hot peppers among the usual crops | Oregon State EM 9456 |
| 5-gallon deep water culture bucket | Suited to long-term crops such as peppers if the water is well aerated | UF/IFAS Volusia County |
| NFT channels | Lowest pepper yields in the Virginia Tech trial | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Kratky (no pump) | No source we read tested peppers this way | — |

**Our pick for a home grower:** a [Dutch bucket](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/) or a single 5-gallon [deep water culture bucket](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) per plant. Both give the 4-gallon minimum South Dakota State sets, and both let you stake each plant. Use media and drip if you can; that is what the trial with the best pepper yields used. A note on that trial: Virginia Tech's harvest of tomatoes and peppers was cut short by COVID-19 restrictions, so its ranking is a direction, not a precise yield gap. For drip setups, see the [drip hydroponic system guide](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/); for media choices, [hydroponic growing media compared](/equipment/hydroponic-growing-media/).

## Temperature: the number that decides fruit set

Peppers will grow leaves across a wide range but set fruit in a narrow one. Oklahoma State's figures are for field peppers, but the plant is the same indoors:

- **65–80 °F (18–27 °C):** best yields during fruit set.
- **Above 85 °F (29 °C):** fruit that sets is usually small and poorly shaped.
- **Above 90 °F (32 °C) by day or below 60 °F (16 °C) at night:** very little fruit sets.

Virginia Tech's garden fact sheet calls peppers a warm crop at **70–75 °F (21–24 °C)** and adds that blossoms drop when **nights rise above 75 °F (24 °C)**. In the University of Florida greenhouse trial, plants wilted when it passed 38 °C (100 °F), and the authors recommend heating to keep winter nights **over 15 °C (59 °F)**. UF/IFAS HS1274 asks for nights of no less than **60–62 °F** for tomatoes and peppers to reduce fruit cracking.

Nights of 59–62 °F (15–17 °C): the lowest to aim for, with less cracking above 60 °F. Nights under 60 °F (16 °C): very little fruit sets.](/images/guides/hydroponic-peppers-temperature-bands.svg)

*Pepper fruit set by air temperature (Oklahoma State Pepper Production; Virginia Tech SPES-684; Jovicich et al. 2004; UF/IFAS HS1274).*

In practice: indoors, aim for days in the low to mid 70s °F and nights in the low to mid 60s °F. A grow light adds heat, so check the canopy temperature, not just the room thermostat. None of the sources gives a root-zone or solution temperature for peppers; Oklahoma State's general guide puts hydroponic solutions at about 72–75 °F (22–24 °C).

## Light: give peppers far more than lettuce

Michigan State's Erik Runkle names the daily light integral (DLI) as the biggest limit for vine crops such as tomato, pepper and cucumber, and suggests **at least 15, preferably more than 20 mol/m²/day**. That is well above the 12–14 he gives for lettuce. His rule of thumb: each 1% more light gives about 1% more production.

**Do not run the light around the clock.** Runkle suggests four to six hours of darkness each night. A 2022 Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada paper notes that peppers are susceptible to photoperiod injury and that yield falls under continuous lighting; its control ran 16 hours on and 8 off. A 16-hour day with the brightest light you can afford is a sound plan. The [indoor systems guide](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) shows how to estimate DLI from a light's rating.

No source we read gives a pepper-only DLI, and none tested peppers under a home grow light.

## Feeding: pH, EC and nutrients by stage

**pH is settled.** Oklahoma State and Tennessee both give **5.5–6.0**, and the Florida greenhouse trial held 5.5–6.5. Aim for 5.8 and correct anything outside 5.5–6.0. A calibrated meter matters more than the brand; see [how to choose and calibrate a pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/).

**EC is where the sources disagree.** Oklahoma State's crop table gives pepper **0.8–1.8 mS/cm**, close to lettuce. Tennessee groups peppers with tomatoes and cucumbers: **0.5–1.0 for seedlings, 1.0–1.5 at transplant and 2.0–3.0 for much of the season**. The Florida greenhouse trial sat between them at **1.4–2.5**. UF/IFAS HS1274 adds that peppers tolerate less salt than tomatoes.

| Stage | Tennessee EC (mS/cm) | Florida shade trial N / K (ppm) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seedlings | 0.5–1.0 | — | Tennessee W 844-C |
| Transplant to first flower | 1.0–1.5 | 80 / 120 | Tennessee W 844-C; UF/IFAS HS1113 Table 1 |
| After first flower | 2.0–3.0 | 130 / 200 | Tennessee W 844-C; UF/IFAS HS1113 Table 1 |

The Florida shade trial also held calcium at 150 ppm and raised magnesium from 40 to 50 ppm after first flower. It gave no EC, so the two columns are from different sources and should not be read as one recipe.

**What we recommend:** start at about **1.0–1.5** after transplant, which sits inside both Oklahoma State's range and Tennessee's transplant stage. Raise it gradually once fruit sets, toward 2.0 and no higher than about 2.5 (the top of the Florida trial), and back off if leaf tips burn. This follows Tennessee's staging while respecting Oklahoma State's lower range. Use the [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) and the [pH and EC target table](/tools/ph-ec-targets/), and see [hydroponic nutrients for vegetables](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrients-for-vegetables/) for mixing peppers with other crops.

**Solution changes.** Tennessee advises changing small recirculating tanks every one to two weeks and notes that larger plants unbalance a solution faster. In May, the Florida greenhouse plants drank **2.5–3.0 L (0.66–0.79 gal) per plant per day**, so a bucket can drop fast in warm weather. The [reservoir size guide](/equipment/hydroponic-reservoir-size/) covers how much to hold.

## Spacing, pruning and support

**Spacing.** The Florida shade trial set plants **18 in (46 cm) apart** in the row in 3-gallon pots. In the University of Florida greenhouse trial, more plants per area gave more fruit per area: marketable yield rose from 3.5 to 7.4 kg/m² as density went from 0.14 to 0.35 plants per sq ft (1.5 to 3.8 per m²). Fruit set per plant fell, from 54.8% to 31.5%. Indoors, where light is the limit, give each plant the light it needs before crowding them.

**Pruning.** The greenhouse trial removed the first (crown) flower and the first-node flower 3 weeks after transplant, then began training at 4 weeks. It compared two methods:

| Method | What it means | Result in the trial | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "V" system | Two stems; side shoots pinched at 1.2–1.6 in (3–4 cm) | 3.9 kg/m² marketable; tied 11 times; 61.6% blossom-end rot | Jovicich et al. 2004 |
| "Spanish" system | Two shoots left at the first fork, then unpruned (two to four main stems) | 6.2 kg/m² marketable (not a significant difference); pruned once; 39.0% blossom-end rot | Jovicich et al. 2004 |

The unpruned "Spanish" plants took about 75% less labor and had half the blossom-end rot, with similar marketable yield. For a home grower that points to light pruning: keep two strong stems, remove the first flower, and support the plant with a stake or strings rather than pinching every side shoot. The Florida shade trial tied each plant to a 5-ft bamboo pole with strings added as plants grew.

**Pollination.** The Florida greenhouse used bumblebees at about one per 100 plants. Indoors there are no bees, and no source we read tested hand-pollinating peppers. Tennessee recommends hand pollination, for example with an electric toothbrush, for tomatoes grown indoors; it gives no method for peppers, so this guide has no pepper figure for it.

## Step by step: one pepper in a 5-gallon bucket

1. **Start seed 4–8 weeks ahead.** Oklahoma State gives 4–6 weeks to transplant size and Virginia Tech 6–8; the Florida trials used 5- and 6-week-old plants. Hold seedlings at EC 0.5–1.0.
2. **Set up the bucket.** An opaque 5-gallon bucket with a lid cut for a 6-inch net pot, an air pump and stone for deep water culture (UF/IFAS Volusia County), or a media-filled Dutch bucket on drip. See the [air pump sizing guide](/equipment/air-pump-for-dwc-hydroponics/).
3. **Mix the solution.** pH 5.5–6.0, EC about 1.0–1.5 at transplant.
4. **Transplant and stake.** Add a stake or cage now; the Volusia bucket design drills four ¼-inch holes for a tomato cage.
5. **Set light and temperature.** 16 hours of light a day with darkness at night; days in the 70s °F, nights above 60 °F (16 °C).
6. **Remove the first flowers at 3 weeks** and keep two main stems from week 4.
7. **Raise EC after fruit sets,** toward 2.0, and change the solution every one to two weeks.
8. **Pick every 7–14 days.** Bells are ready green from about 70–85 days. The sources give no day count for bells to color fully on the plant.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flowers drop without fruit | Nights above 75 °F (24 °C), days above 90 °F (32 °C) or nights below 60 °F (16 °C) | Bring the canopy back to 65–80 °F | Virginia Tech SPES-684; Oklahoma State |
| Small, misshapen fruit | Fruit set above 85 °F (29 °C) | Cool the canopy; raise the light | Oklahoma State |
| Sunken dark patch at the fruit tip (blossom-end rot) | Calcium not reaching the fruit, usually from uneven water | Keep the solution level and feeding steady; remove affected fruit, which cannot recover | Mississippi State; Tennessee W 844-C |
| Cracked fruit | Cold nights | Keep nights at 60–62 °F or above | UF/IFAS HS1274 |
| Tall, leafy plants, little fruit | Too much fertilizer, or too little light | Do not push EC past about 2.5; add light | Virginia Tech SPES-684; Runkle 2011 |
| Bucket drops fast | Large plants drink up to 0.66–0.79 gal a day in warm weather | Check the level daily; use a bigger reservoir | Jovicich et al. 2004 |
| Plants wilt at midday | Air above 100 °F (38 °C) | Ventilate or move the light up | Jovicich et al. 2004 |
| pH drifts | Large plants unbalance a small tank quickly | Check daily; change the solution every one to two weeks | Tennessee W 844-C; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |

## Frequently asked questions

### What pH do hydroponic peppers need?

pH 5.5–6.0, from both Oklahoma State and the University of Tennessee. The Florida greenhouse trial used 5.5–6.5.

### What EC or ppm should I use for peppers?

Start around EC 1.0–1.5 mS/cm at transplant and raise it toward 2.0 once fruit sets. Oklahoma State lists 0.8–1.8; Tennessee runs fruiting crops at 2.0–3.0. Neither gives ppm, and ppm depends on your meter's conversion factor, so work in EC.

### How long do hydroponic peppers take?

From transplant, bell peppers are ready green in about 70–85 days (Virginia Tech) or 75–80 days (Oklahoma State); the Florida shade trial picked its first fruit at 77 days. From seed, Virginia Tech gives 100–120 days. Chiles take about 100 days from transplant to mature green and 140 to red.

### Can I grow peppers in a Kratky jar?

No source we read tested it. A pepper plant needs at least 4 gallons of root room (South Dakota State) and drinks a lot once fruiting, which is hard for an unaerated jar. A 5-gallon aerated bucket is the better-documented choice. See the [Kratky guide](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) for what that method suits.

### Can I grow peppers with tomatoes in one system?

They share pH 5.5–6.0 in Tennessee's guidance, but Oklahoma State lists tomatoes at EC 2.0–4.0 against pepper's 0.8–1.8, and peppers tolerate less salt. Keep the EC at the low end of the tomato range or give peppers their own tank. See the [hydroponic tomatoes guide](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).

*Figures in this guide come from the university, extension and peer-reviewed sources listed below. Most pepper research is from greenhouses and fields, not home grow rooms. HydroRigor has not grown peppers for this guide.*
