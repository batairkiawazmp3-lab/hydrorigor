The best hydroponic fertilizer for tomatoes is a **complete, tomato-specific formula that is low in nitrogen and high in potassium, with calcium supplied as a separate part**, such as a 4-18-38 base used with calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate (Penn State Extension). Mississippi State Extension calls all-purpose 20-20-20 a poor choice for tomatoes. Feed by cluster count: UF/IFAS recommends **70 ppm nitrogen and 120 ppm potassium** from transplant to the first cluster, rising to **150 ppm nitrogen and 200 ppm potassium** after the fifth, with **calcium at 150 ppm** throughout and the solution at **pH 5.8–6.2**. University of Tennessee Extension runs fruiting crops at **EC 2.0–3.0 mS/cm** for most of the season.

Below: choosing a fertilizer by its label, numbers by stage, mixing, and fixes. Every figure is tied to a source listed at the end. For light, support and pollination, see [how to grow hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/).

## Best hydroponic fertilizer for tomatoes

There is no single best brand in the university sources, but there is a clear best format. Tomatoes fruit for months, so their needs change more than a lettuce plant's. UF/IFAS keeps nitrogen at 60–70 ppm early to avoid a leafy, "bullish" plant, and warns that too much potassium early interferes with calcium and magnesium uptake. A fertilizer that fits tomatoes lets you start lean and build up. That is why Mississippi State tells growers to buy a soluble fertilizer formulated for greenhouse tomatoes, with much lower nitrogen and much higher potassium than 20-20-20.

| Format | How it fits tomatoes | What to watch | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4-18-38 base + calcium nitrate + magnesium sulfate | The standard tomato program; three dry parts, calcium kept apart | Small doses need a scale reading to 0.1 g | Penn State; Masterblend instructions |
| Two- or three-part bottled line | Easiest for one or two plants; parts are kept apart in their bottles | Generic charts can sit below tomato EC targets | General Hydroponics feedcharts |
| Premixed greenhouse-tomato fertilizer | What Mississippi State advises | Check whether calcium and magnesium are included | Mississippi State; Penn State |
| All-purpose 20-20-20 | Poor fit: too much N, too little K | Not recommended for tomatoes | Mississippi State |

![Four label checks for a hydroponic tomato fertilizer: 1, low nitrogen and high potassium, such as a 4-18-38 base, not 20-20-20; 2, calcium kept apart in its own part or stock, usually calcium nitrate; 3, a complete formula with chelated iron, manganese and zinc on the label; 4, the fruiting rate on its chart reaches EC 2.0–3.0 mS/cm, confirmed with a meter.](/images/guides/tomato-fertilizer-label-checks.svg)

*Four checks that rule out most poor choices. Sources: Mississippi State Extension, Penn State Extension, University of Tennessee W 844-C.*

**The 4-18-38 program in numbers.** One example of this format is Masterblend's 4-18-38 Tomato Formula. Its mixing instructions give **2.4 g of 4-18-38, 1.5 g of magnesium sulfate and 2.4 g of calcium nitrate per gallon (3.8 L)**, dissolved in that order, at half rates until seedlings are established. The label lists 4% nitrogen, 18% phosphate (P₂O₅), 38% potash (K₂O) and chelated iron, manganese, copper and zinc, and says to use it only after a water analysis. Converting the label's oxide figures (P₂O₅ × 0.44, K₂O × 0.83), the full rate of the base alone supplies about **50 ppm phosphorus and 200 ppm potassium**: our arithmetic, matching UF/IFAS's late-season targets. Rates per reservoir size are in our [Masterblend guide](/reviews/masterblend-hydroponic-nutrients/).

**Bottled lines.** General Hydroponics' FloraSeries charts are written for crops in general, not tomatoes. Their Medium bloom rows run at EC 1.6–1.9, below Tennessee's 2.0–3.0 fruiting range; the Aggressive bloom rows (1.9–2.4) reach its lower end. If you use a bottled line, follow one line's chart and let your EC meter, not the bottle, set the strength. Our [General Hydroponics Flora guide](/reviews/general-hydroponics-nutrients/) has the full chart.

Penn State adds two points that apply to any format: buy greenhouse-grade fertilizer (higher purity, dissolves better), and look for chelated micronutrients, which stay available when pH shifts. Organic products can work but bring their own problems; see [organic hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/organic-hydroponic-nutrients/). For liquid versus powder, see [powder vs liquid nutrients](/nutrients-water/powder-vs-liquid-hydroponic-nutrients/).

## Feeding by growth stage

Tomato programs change the recipe by the number of flower or fruit clusters (trusses), not by date. Two university programs follow. They were developed for different systems, so pick one and follow it whole.

| Stage (UF/IFAS) | N ppm | K ppm | Ca ppm | Mg ppm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transplant to 1st cluster | 70 | 120 | 150 | 40 |
| 1st to 2nd cluster | 80 | 120 | 150 | 40 |
| 2nd to 3rd cluster | 100 | 150 | 150 | 40 |
| 3rd to 5th cluster | 120 | 150 | 150 | 50 |
| 5th cluster to end | 150 | 200 | 150 | 50 |

UF/IFAS developed these for perlite, rockwool and NFT tomatoes in Florida. Phosphorus stays at 50 ppm and sulfur at 50–60 ppm, and calcium, magnesium and sulfur may need adjusting for what your water already contains.

Ohio State's three-stage program (HYG-1437, from Dr. Jensen's University of Arizona recipe) runs richer and was written for greenhouse production:

| Stage (Ohio State) | When | Nitrate-N ppm | K ppm | Ca ppm | EC (mS/cm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Seedling to 2nd truss in flower | 90 | 144 | 144 | About 2.0 |
| 2 | 3rd to 5th truss in flower | 120 | 350 | 160 | Not given |
| 3 | Beyond the 5th truss | 190 | 350 | 200 | Closer to 2.4 |

Ohio State raises calcium in stage 3 "largely to prevent blossom end rot" and raises potassium for fruit quality. With a bottled program, move up its stages the same way, as clusters set.

## EC: how strong to make the solution

EC (electrical conductivity) measures total dissolved salts, so it tells you strength, not which nutrients are present. The extension figures differ:

| Source | Seedlings | Fruiting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tennessee W 844-C | 0.5–1.0 mS/cm, 1.0–1.5 at transplanting | 2.0–3.0 mS/cm for most of the season |
| Ohio State HYG-1437 | About 2.0 (stage 1) | Closer to 2.4 (stage 3) |
| Kentucky CCD-TA-01 (fruiting crops) | Not given for tomatoes | Up to 2.5–3.5 mS/cm |
| Oklahoma State HLA-6722 | Not given | 2.0–4.0 mS/cm |

**What we recommend:** start transplants at 1.0–1.5 mS/cm and run fruiting plants at 2.0–3.0 mS/cm, the Tennessee range written for small-scale systems. It sits inside Oklahoma State's range and overlaps Ohio State's and Kentucky's. If your meter reads ppm, check its conversion factor first; the [pH and EC target reference](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) keeps the units straight.

## pH: keep it in a narrow band

| Source | Tomato solution pH |
| --- | --- |
| UF/IFAS HS796 | 5.8–6.2 (final solution) |
| Tennessee W 844-C | Often 5.5–6.0 |
| Ohio State HYG-1437 | 5.5–6.5 at the drip |
| Mississippi State Extension | 5.6–5.8 in aggregate media |
| Oklahoma State HLA-6722 | 6.0–6.5 |

**What we recommend:** aim for 5.8–6.2, the UF/IFAS range for hydroponic tomatoes, which overlaps or touches every range above. Follow Oklahoma State's order: get EC right first, then adjust pH, adding acid or base slowly and waiting a few minutes between additions. A meter you calibrate is essential; see [choosing a pH meter](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/).

## Mixing the solution: two stocks, never combined

![How to mix a hydroponic tomato solution. Stock A holds the tomato base fertilizer with magnesium sulfate; stock B holds calcium nitrate. They are never mixed as concentrates, because calcium forms solids with sulfate or phosphate. Six steps: test the source water for pH, alkalinity and EC; fill the reservoir with water; add one stock and stir; add the other and stir; measure EC, aiming for 2.0–3.0 mS/cm for fruiting plants; adjust pH last, to 5.8–6.2.](/images/guides/tomato-nutrient-mixing-steps.svg)

*Calcium goes in its own stock. Stock split from Penn State Extension and Ohio State HYG-1437; EC from Tennessee W 844-C; pH from UF/IFAS HS796.*

Penn State explains why concentrated tomato nutrients come in parts: at high concentration, calcium reacts with phosphates and sulfates and forms precipitates that clog drip lines. It keeps calcium nitrate in one stock tank and the 4-18-38 base with magnesium sulfate in the other; Ohio State's 100X stocks split the same way.

Step by step:

1. **Test your source water** for pH, alkalinity and EC (Oklahoma State). Calcium, magnesium and carbonates already in the water change what you need to add (UF/IFAS).
2. **Fill the reservoir with water** to the volume you will use. Our [reservoir size guide](/equipment/hydroponic-reservoir-size/) covers how much a tomato needs.
3. **Add one part at its label rate and stir.** The [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) scales a label rate to your reservoir.
4. **Add the next part and stir.** Never pour one concentrate into another.
5. **Measure EC.** Aim for 2.0–3.0 mS/cm for fruiting plants, lower for transplants. If it is high, add water; if low, add concentrate (Oklahoma State).
6. **Adjust pH last** to 5.8–6.2.

More detail, including one-part and three-part products, is in [how to mix hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/).

## Source water comes first

Your water is part of the recipe:

- **Alkalinity above 75 ppm** pushes solution pH up (Oklahoma State). Acid fertilizers, added acid or reverse osmosis bring it down.
- **Sodium and chloride:** Tennessee uses 75 ppm as an upper limit for source water. High sodium can cause blossom drop and blossom-end rot.
- **Calcium and magnesium in the water** count toward the targets. UF/IFAS notes Florida well water may carry 40–60 ppm calcium; Ohio State's recipes assume reverse-osmosis water.

Your water supplier's annual quality report is a free starting point, and an EC reading of plain tap water shows how much of your target is already taken.

## Keeping the solution balanced

Tennessee warns that bigger plants take up nutrients faster, which leads to quicker imbalances in recirculating systems, and topping up to hold one EC number cannot tell you which nutrient is running low. A routine that follows the sources:

- **Daily:** check EC and pH at the same time of day (Oklahoma State).
- **Between changes:** top up with plain water to lower EC, or concentrate to raise it (Tennessee).
- **Every one to two weeks:** replace the solution in small recirculating systems (Tennessee).
- **When a new cluster stage begins:** move to the next stage's recipe or chart rate.

Log EC, pH, cluster count and changes. Our [nutrient solution guide](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) covers top-up versus replace in more depth.

## Blossom-end rot: calcium, water and temperature

Blossom-end rot (a dark, sunken patch at the bottom of the fruit) is the problem most often blamed on nutrients. Calcium is involved, but adding more is rarely the whole answer:

- **Calcium has to reach the fruit.** Tennessee links blossom-end rot to unbalanced water movement through the plant and to high or fluctuating temperatures.
- **Uneven water is a classic trigger.** Mississippi State says moisture stress severe enough to wilt plants will guarantee it.
- **Too much potassium early** can block calcium and magnesium uptake (UF/IFAS; Penn State).
- **Calcium in the recipe matters too.** Ohio State raises it in the fruiting stage largely to prevent the disorder.

Affected fruit will not recover; Mississippi State advises removing it. Fix the cause so the next fruits form cleanly.

## Troubleshooting tomato nutrition

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dark, sunken spots at the fruit's blossom end | Calcium not reaching fruit: uneven water, heat swings, high K early | Keep irrigation steady, avoid temperature swings, check K and Ca | Tennessee; Mississippi State; UF/IFAS |
| Big, leafy plants with few flowers | Too much nitrogen early | Use the early recipe: about 70 ppm N until the first cluster | UF/IFAS HS796; Ohio State |
| Yellowing between the veins of new leaves | Iron unavailable, often because pH is high | Bring pH back to 5.8–6.2; use a fertilizer with chelated iron | Penn State |
| Wilting and burned leaf edges | Excess salts | Check EC; dilute with water if above your target | Penn State; Oklahoma State |
| pH keeps drifting up | Alkaline source water | Test alkalinity; above 75 ppm, acidify or use RO water | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| EC climbs between changes | Plants drinking water faster than they take up salts | Top up with plain water; replace every 1–2 weeks | Tennessee W 844-C |
| White crust or clogged emitters | Calcium mixed with sulfate or phosphate concentrates | Keep calcium in a separate stock; clean the lines | Penn State |
| Blossom drop despite a good recipe | High sodium in source water | Test sodium and chloride; above 75 ppm, change water | Tennessee W 844-C |

## Beyond the nutrient tank

Nutrition cannot make up for the rest of the environment. Tennessee's figures for small-scale fruiting crops: eight hours or more of natural light, daytime temperatures in the 70s °F (about 21–26 °C) with growth slowing above the mid-80s °F (about 29 °C), and hand pollination three to four times a week. It lists 2–4 weeks from seeding to transplant and 7–10 weeks from transplant to harvest. Pick a compact variety indoors ([hydroponic tomato seeds](/crops/hydroponic-tomato-seeds/)). For the system, [Dutch buckets](/systems/dutch-bucket-hydroponics/) on a [drip system](/systems/drip-hydroponic-system/) suit tomatoes, and UF/IFAS Volusia sizes its [DWC bucket](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/) for a single tomato. Tennessee's guide covers peppers and cucumbers in the same systems; see [hydroponic peppers](/crops/hydroponic-peppers/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the best hydroponic nutrients for tomatoes?

A complete fertilizer formulated for tomatoes, low in nitrogen and high in potassium, with calcium supplied separately, used with EC and pH meters. Penn State's example is a 4-18-38 base plus calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate; Mississippi State advises against 20-20-20. Bottled two- or three-part lines work if you follow one line's chart and check EC.

### Is 4-18-38 good for hydroponic tomatoes?

Yes, as one part of a three-part program. On its own it has little nitrogen and no calcium, which is why Penn State and the Masterblend instructions pair it with calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate.

### What EC should hydroponic tomatoes be?

2.0–3.0 mS/cm for most of the fruiting season, after seedlings at 0.5–1.0 and transplants at 1.0–1.5 (University of Tennessee). Other extension ranges run up to 4.0.

### What pH do hydroponic tomatoes need?

5.8–6.2 in the final solution (UF/IFAS). Extension ranges run from 5.5 to 6.5.

### When should I switch to a bloom or fruiting formula?

By cluster count, not date. UF/IFAS raises nitrogen from the first cluster and potassium from the second, with the biggest step after the fifth. Ohio State moves to stage 2 at the third truss in flower.

### Do hydroponic tomatoes need extra calcium?

They need a steady supply: 150 ppm at every stage in the UF/IFAS program, and up to 200 ppm in Ohio State's fruiting stage, usually as calcium nitrate in a separate stock. Extra calcium will not fix blossom-end rot caused by uneven watering or heat.

### How often should I change the nutrient solution for tomatoes?

Every one to two weeks in small recirculating systems, topping up between changes (University of Tennessee), with EC and pH checked daily (Oklahoma State).

*Figures in this guide come from the university, extension and manufacturer sources listed below. Programs differ, so treat them as starting points, follow one program consistently and check your own solution as the crop grows. HydroRigor has not run its own tomato feeding trial for this guide.*
