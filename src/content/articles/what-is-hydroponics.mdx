**Hydroponics is growing plants without soil.** The roots sit in, or are regularly wetted by, water with fertilizer salts dissolved in it, called a nutrient solution. Oregon State University Extension defines it as "a method of growing plants in a nutrient-rich water solution without soil", and the University of Missouri adds that the roots either hang in the solution or grow in a soilless medium such as coconut coir or rockwool.

To make it work, you take over the four jobs soil normally does. Hold the solution at **pH 5.5–6.5**, set its strength (EC) for the crop (**1.2–1.8 mS/cm for lettuce**), keep **7–10 ppm of dissolved oxygen** around the roots, and indoors give the plants **about 12–16 hours of light a day**. A first crop of lettuce is ready about **3–5 weeks after transplanting**.

HydroRigor has not run trials for this guide; every figure comes from a university, extension or peer-reviewed source listed at the end.

## How hydroponics works

Soil holds a plant up, stores water, supplies nutrients and lets air reach the roots. A hydroponic system hands each of those jobs to something you control.

![A plant in a net pot above a reservoir, with the four jobs soil does and what replaces each in hydroponics. Support: a net pot, foam raft or inert medium such as rockwool, coir or clay pebbles. Water and nutrients: a nutrient solution at pH 5.5 to 6.5, EC set by crop, for example 1.2 to 1.8 for lettuce. Oxygen: an air pump, air gap or thin film, 7 to 10 ppm dissolved oxygen and never below 4. Light indoors: a grow light for about 12 to 16 hours a day.](/images/guides/what-hydroponics-replaces-soil.svg)

*The four jobs soil does and what does them in a hydroponic system. pH from Missouri G6984, EC from Oklahoma State HLA-6722, oxygen from Virginia Tech SPES-466, light from Missouri G6986.*

1. **Support.** A net pot, foam raft or inert medium (rockwool, coir, perlite, clay pebbles) anchors the roots without feeding them. Oregon State notes the medium is optional.
2. **Water and nutrients.** Dissolved fertilizer salts replace soil minerals. Penn State counts 17 essential elements; carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come from air and water, and the rest must be in the solution.
3. **Oxygen.** Roots breathe, and water holds little air. Hoagland and Arnon's 1938 University of California circular warned that "an adequate supply of air to the roots must be provided". Systems use an air pump, a moist air gap or a film thin enough for air to reach the roots.
4. **Light.** Indoors this is the one most often short. Missouri puts most crops at about 12–16 hours a day.

The word "hydroponics" was already "recently coined" when Hoagland and Arnon wrote in 1938. Oregon State credits William Frederick Gericke of UC Berkeley with popularizing it; Hoagland and Arnon credit him with adapting a laboratory method to commercial growing.

## What the roots need: pH, EC, oxygen and temperature

Getting these four right is most of hydroponics. The [pH and EC targets tool](/tools/ph-ec-targets/) lists them crop by crop.

| Factor | Target | Why it matters | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH | 5.5–6.5 | Outside this range some nutrients become unavailable; high pH often causes iron deficiency | Missouri G6984; Penn State |
| EC | Set by crop, e.g. lettuce 1.2–1.8 mS/cm | Measures total dissolved salts: too low starves, too high burns | Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Source water EC | 0.2–0.8 dS/m, ideally below 1 | Salts already in tap water use up part of your EC budget | Missouri G6984 |
| Dissolved oxygen | 7–10 ppm, minimum 4 | Low oxygen harms roots | Virginia Tech SPES-466 |
| Solution temperature | About 22–24 °C (72–75 °F) | Cool, shaded solution holds more oxygen | Oklahoma State HLA-6722; Oregon State EM 9453 |

**pH: where the sources differ.** Missouri gives 5.5–6.5 for most crops; Oklahoma State's general fact sheet says 5.0–6.0, and its crop table puts lettuce at 6.0–7.0 and basil at 5.5–6.0. We use 5.5–6.5 as the default because it is the range given for most crops, then check the crop's own guide. Use a calibrated meter; see our [pH meter guide](/best/hydroponic-ph-meter/).

**EC.** Conductivity shows how strong the solution is, not which nutrients are in it (Missouri G6984). Greens run low and fruiting crops high. Adjust pH only after EC is in range, because fertilizer moves pH (Oklahoma State). Our guides to [nutrient solution](/nutrients-water/hydroponic-nutrient-solution/) and [mixing nutrients](/nutrients-water/how-to-mix-hydroponic-nutrients/) go further.

**Oxygen.** Virginia Tech gives 7–10 ppm for deep water culture; the University of Kentucky gives 6–8 ppm for lettuce in the same system, so 7 ppm or more satisfies both. Warm water holds less oxygen, which is why Oregon State says to keep the solution cool and shaded.

## The main types of hydroponic systems

Missouri Extension sorts systems two ways: **water-based** (roots in solution, on rafts, in channels or misted) versus **media-based** (roots in a container of coir, perlite or rockwool fed by drip), and **closed** systems that recirculate the solution versus **open** ones that do not reuse it.

| System | How it feeds and aerates | Suits | Watch-out | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kratky | Fill once; the falling level leaves an air gap | Lettuce and other short crops | Don't top it up; not for long-season crops | UF/IFAS HS1488 |
| Wick | A wick draws solution up into the medium | Small, simple setups | The simplest system | Oklahoma State HLA-6442 |
| Deep water culture | Roots hang in aerated solution under a raft | Lettuce, spinach, kale, herbs | Needs an air pump; more forgiving if it stops | Oregon State EM 9455; Missouri G6986 |
| NFT | A thin film flows down a sloped channel | Short-lived, small-rooted crops | Plants can wilt quickly if the pump stops | Oregon State EM 9457; Missouri G6986 |
| Ebb and flow | A tray floods and drains on a timer | Seedlings, nursery crops | Needs a reliable timer | Oregon State EM 9453 |
| Drip and buckets | Drip lines feed a medium in buckets or bags | Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers | Emitters can clog | Oregon State EM 9456; Missouri G6986 |
| Aeroponics | Roots hang in air and are misted | Leafy greens and herbs | Needs clean water and pressurized misters | Missouri G6986 |

Missouri ranks aeroponics lowest in water and fertilizer use and deep water culture highest, but reverses the order for build cost and the risk of losing plants: deep water culture is cheapest and "more resilient and forgiving" for beginners, because plants do not wilt at once if the air pump fails.

The crop decides the system. In Oregon State's words, "any plant can be grown hydroponically, but the best system depends on your crops and space". Virginia Tech's trials agree: greens and herbs yielded best in NFT and deep water culture, tomatoes and peppers in media bags. Our comparison of [indoor hydroponic systems for home](/systems/indoor-hydroponic-systems-for-home/) goes through six types in detail, and the guides to [deep water culture](/systems/deep-water-culture-hydroponics/), [Kratky](/systems/kratky-hydroponics/) and [hydroponic towers](/systems/hydroponic-tower-garden/) cover individual builds.

## Does hydroponics use less water and grow more?

These are the two claims you will see most often, and the sources do not agree on the size of the effect.

![Three comparison cards from Barbosa and colleagues, 2015, a modeled study. Yield: 41 kilograms of lettuce per square meter per year in a hydroponic greenhouse against 3.9 in an Arizona field, about 11 times more. Water: 20 liters per kilogram against 250, about 13 times less. Energy: 90,000 kilojoules per kilogram against 1,100, about 82 times more, mostly for heating, cooling and grow lights.](/images/guides/hydroponics-vs-field-lettuce-model.svg)

*Modeled annual figures for a commercial hydroponic greenhouse in Yuma, Arizona, against Arizona field lettuce. Barbosa et al., International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 2015.*

- **Oregon State (EM 9453)** lists "uses 100x less water for the same yield" among the advantages, without saying how the figure was reached.
- **Barbosa and colleagues (Arizona State University, 2015)** modeled a commercial greenhouse in Yuma, Arizona, against Arizona field lettuce: **13 times less water per kilogram**, **11 times the yield per square meter**, but **82 times more energy per kilogram**, mostly for heating, cooling and lights. These are calculated, not measured, for a greenhouse running about 12 crops a year.
- **Missouri Extension** says hydroponics can out-yield field production per square foot and is resource-efficient "except energy".
- **Hoagland and Arnon (1938)** compared tomatoes grown in soil and in solution in the same greenhouse and found the yields "not markedly different". Their conclusion: "There is no magic in the growth of plants in water culture."

These fit together once you see what is compared. The big yield gains come from year-round growing in a controlled space against one field crop a season; given the same greenhouse, as in Hoagland and Arnon's trial, soil and solution yields were similar. The water saving is real because a closed system reuses its solution, but the 100-fold figure has no published method, so we quote the modeled 13-fold one. At home: less water, more control and year-round crops, paid for with electricity and attention.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Grow all year, indoors, without a garden (Oregon State; Missouri) | Higher energy use, mostly for lights and climate (Missouri; Barbosa) |
| Less water per kilogram in a closed system (Barbosa) | Higher start-up cost than a garden bed (Missouri) |
| More produce per square foot in a controlled space (Missouri) | Daily pH and EC checks (Oklahoma State) |
| No soil pests or weeding; simpler sanitation (Oregon State) | A pump failure can wilt plants quickly in NFT and aeroponics (Oregon State; Missouri) |
| Close control of nutrients | Needs some skill; mistakes spread through the shared solution (Missouri) |

Hoagland and Arnon (1938) found no significant difference in the minerals, vitamins or flavor of tomatoes grown in soil and in solution. The study is old and our sources include nothing newer on it, so we make no nutrition claim either way. For organic growing, see [organic hydroponic nutrients](/nutrients-water/organic-hydroponic-nutrients/).

## How to start: a first hydroponic grow, step by step

The University of Illinois suggests lettuce, spinach, kale and herbs for a first system, and the simplest builds are Kratky and a small deep water culture tub.

1. **Pick a leafy crop.** Lettuce is the standard first crop; see our [hydroponic lettuce guide](/crops/hydroponic-lettuce/) or the [hydroponic basil guide](/crops/hydroponic-basil/). Leave tomatoes for a second project: they need drip-fed buckets and a higher EC (see [hydroponic tomatoes](/crops/hydroponic-tomatoes/)).
2. **Choose the system.** For no power, use Kratky: UF/IFAS grows three lettuce plants in a 5-gallon (19 L) bucket, 1–1.5 gallons (about 4–6 L) of solution each. For repeated crops, use a deep water culture tub with an air pump, air stone and check valve (Illinois). Our [DIY hydroponic system guide](/systems/diy-hydroponic-system-for-beginners/) has parts lists.
3. **Use food-grade, opaque containers.** Oregon State recommends food-grade materials, with high-density polyethylene a good choice. Illinois says to cover clear sides so light can't reach the solution and grow algae.
4. **Start seedlings.** Sow in rockwool or coir cubes. Kentucky transplants lettuce 14–21 days after seeding, at three to four true leaves.
5. **Mix the solution and check it.** Fill with water, add a hydroponic fertilizer at the label rate, then measure EC (lettuce 1.2–1.8 mS/cm, Oklahoma State) and pH (5.5–6.5, Missouri). Fix EC first, then pH. The [nutrient mixing calculator](/tools/nutrient-mixing-calculator/) helps with the amounts.
6. **Set the light and temperature.** Indoors, run a grow light about 12–16 hours a day (Missouri). Kentucky gives lettuce a daily light integral of 12–17 mol/m²/day and days up to 75 °F (24 °C).
7. **Keep up the routine.** In a pumped system, check pH and EC daily and replace the solution about every two weeks (Oklahoma State); top up with water between changes. In a Kratky bucket, don't top up at all.
8. **Harvest.** Kentucky gives lettuce 21–35 days after transplanting; UF/IFAS gives about 3–4 weeks for its bucket.

If you would rather buy than build, our guide to the [best hydroponic system for beginners](/best/hydroponic-systems-for-beginners/) compares the options by crop and space.

## Troubleshooting common first-grow problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Green slime or film in the reservoir | Light reaching the solution | Cover clear sides; fit the lid or raft closely | Illinois; Oregon State EM 9455 |
| Yellowing between the veins of new leaves | Iron deficiency, often from high pH | Bring pH back to 5.5–6.5 | Penn State; Missouri G6984 |
| Wilting with burnt leaf edges | Too much salt (EC too high) | Dilute with plain water; recheck EC | Penn State; Oklahoma State HLA-6722 |
| Brown, slimy roots | Low oxygen, warm solution | Check the air pump; keep solution cool and shaded | Virginia Tech SPES-466; Oregon State EM 9453 |
| Whole channel wilts at once | Pump stopped in NFT | Restore flow; have a power and pump backup | Oregon State EM 9457; Missouri G6986 |
| Brown leaf tips on lettuce (tipburn) | Calcium not reaching fast-growing leaves | Keep EC in range; see the lettuce guide | Penn State; University of Kentucky |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is hydroponics in simple terms?

Growing plants with their roots in water that has fertilizer dissolved in it, instead of in soil (Oregon State EM 9453; Missouri G6986).

### Who invented hydroponics?

Solution culture was a laboratory method first. Hoagland and Arnon (1938) credit W. F. Gericke of the University of California with adapting it to commercial growing, and Oregon State credits him with popularizing the word.

### Does hydroponics use less water?

Yes, in a closed system that reuses its solution. A modeled Arizona study found lettuce grown hydroponically needed about 13 times less water per kilogram than field lettuce, though it used about 82 times more energy (Barbosa et al., 2015). Oregon State quotes "100x less", without a method.

### Can any plant be grown hydroponically?

Oregon State says any plant can, but the best system depends on the crop. Leafy greens and herbs are easiest; tomatoes, cucumbers and peppers do best in drip-fed buckets or bags (Oregon State EM 9456; Virginia Tech SPES-466).

### Do you need electricity for hydroponics?

Not for the Kratky method, which uses no pump (UF/IFAS HS1488). Every other system needs power for a pump or timer, and indoor growing needs it for lights.
